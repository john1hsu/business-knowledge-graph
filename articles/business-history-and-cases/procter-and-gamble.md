# Procter & Gamble

The Procter & Gamble Company (P&G) is a Cincinnati-based maker of branded household and personal-care products. It was formed in 1837 when William Procter, a British candlemaker, and James Gamble, an Irish-born soapmaker, combined their businesses; both products depended on animal fat, which Cincinnati's hog-packing trade supplied cheaply.[^s1] The company is a staple of business history because it invented the brand-management system that most consumer marketers still use, built an early and unusually large market-research function, and later became the best-known large-company example of sourcing innovation from outside its own laboratories.[^s2][^s3]

## History

P&G grew by selling soap and candles to the Union Army during the Civil War and then to the wider public.[^s1] Its landmark products include Ivory soap (1879), Crisco shortening (1911), Tide (1946, the first synthetic laundry detergent) and Joy (1949, the first liquid synthetic detergent).[^s1] It was among the largest national advertisers and a heavy user of coupons and free samples; in 1932 it introduced radio listeners to one of the first serials to be called a "soap opera", a name taken from the sponsor.[^s1]

In 2005 P&G agreed to buy Gillette in a share-exchange deal valued at about $57 billion, which the company said would give it 21 brands with more than $1 billion in annual sales and leading global positions in categories covering about two-thirds of sales.[^s5] As of fiscal 2026 (year ended June 30, 2026), P&G reported net sales of $87.0 billion and net earnings of $16.0 billion, sold products in about 180 countries and territories, and employed about 104,000 people.[^s4] Fabric & Home Care was its largest segment at 35% of net sales, followed by Baby, Feminine & Family Care (24%), Beauty (19%), Health Care (14%) and Grooming (8%).[^s4]

## The brand-management system

On May 13, 1931, Neil McElroy, a young P&G marketer working on the Camay soap advertising campaign, wrote a three-page memo complaining that Camay was competing not only with Lever and Palmolive but with P&G's own Ivory.[^s2] He proposed that each brand get its own small team, a "brand man" with assistants, that would run it as a separate business and aim it at a distinct group of consumers.[^s2] President Deupree approved the plan, and every later P&G chief executive, including McElroy himself, came up through brand management.[^s2] Business historian Thomas McCraw notes that the system pushed decisions down to the people with the best information, a marketing parallel to Alfred Sloan's multidivisional structure at General Motors.[^s2]

The brand system rested on data. P&G set up a formal market-research department in 1925 under economist D. Paul "Doc" Smelser, who ran it for 34 years; over that time the company sent thousands of field researchers door to door, and by the 1970s it was conducting about 1.5 million telephone or mail interviews a year.[^s2]

## Connect + Develop

By 2000 P&G's in-house innovation model was struggling and its share price had fallen from $118 to $52.[^s3] Under CEO A.G. Lafley the company set a goal of sourcing half of its innovations externally, tapping an estimated 1.5 million outside scientists and engineers alongside about 7,500 internal researchers.[^s3] In their 2006 Harvard Business Review account, the P&G executives behind the programme reported that more than 35% of new products had outside elements (up from about 15% in 2000), R&D productivity had risen nearly 60%, and R&D spending had fallen from 4.8% to 3.4% of sales.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest evidence for P&G's model is longevity: brand management spread across consumer industries and was still in wide use in the early 21st century.[^s2] But independent brand teams also breed proliferation. In August 2014 P&G said it would exit 90 to 100 brands and keep 70 to 80 of its largest, a tacit admission that the portfolio had become too broad; the brands to be shed accounted for about 10% of sales but only about 5% of profit.[^s6] The Connect + Develop figures come from the company's own managers rather than an independent audit, so they show the programme's ambitions more reliably than its causal effect.[^s3] P&G also depends heavily on a few large retailers: Walmart and its affiliates accounted for about 16% of sales in each of fiscal 2024, 2025 and 2026.[^s4]

## Connections

P&G operates in the consumer packaged goods industry and is the founding example of brand management, the practice of building brand equity by treating each brand as its own business backed by market research.[^s2] McCraw's comparison with General Motors ties the brand system to the wider debate on centralization and decentralization.[^s2] Connect + Develop makes P&G a reference case of open innovation.[^s3] Gillette has been part of the company since the roughly $57 billion 2005 acquisition, a large-scale case in mergers and acquisitions.[^s5] The competitive pressure from Lever that prompted McElroy's memo underlies the rivalry edge to Unilever.[^s2] Its concentration of sales with Walmart links it to supply-chain coordination problems such as the bullwhip effect.[^s4]
