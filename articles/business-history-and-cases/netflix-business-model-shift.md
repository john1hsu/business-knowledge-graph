# Netflix's Shift from DVD Rental to Streaming

Netflix's shift from DVD rental to streaming is the sequence of business-model changes by which a mail-order disc-rental company became a global streaming and content-production business. Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph founded Netflix in 1997 in Los Gatos, California, as an online DVD rental service charging per rental.[^s1] It is one of the few widely taught cases in which an incumbent replaced its own profitable model before a rival did it for them, and one in which the cost of doing so was briefly very visible.[^s2][^s5]

## How the shift unfolded

The first business-model change came early. In 1999 Netflix introduced an online subscription, and by 2000 it offered unlimited rentals for a flat fee with no due dates, late fees or shipping charges. That combination is the feature usually credited with disrupting the store-based Blockbuster rental model.[^s1] University of Auckland researchers who studied the company note it had already dropped DVD sales to concentrate on rentals after Amazon entered that market.[^s5]

Streaming arrived in 2007. Netflix offered a streaming-only plan by 2010, the year in which, according to the Auckland study, most subscribers were watching more streamed than mailed content.[^s1][^s5] In July 2011 the company split its combined plans into separate DVD and streaming plans, which raised prices for members who wanted both.[^s2] Later that quarter it announced that the DVD service would be rebranded and run from a separate website, the plan publicly branded Qwikster. Netflix's own annual report calls the consumer reaction very negative and records significant cancellations, and the company withdrew the rebranding and site separation.[^s2] The Auckland researchers put the subscriber loss at more than 800,000.[^s5]

By the end of 2011 the business reported three segments. Domestic streaming had 20.2 million paid subscriptions, international streaming 1.4 million and domestic DVD 11.0 million, out of 26.3 million unique subscribers. Revenue was about $3.2 billion in 2011, up from about $2.2 billion in 2010.[^s2] The filing expected DVD subscriptions to keep falling while DVD remained a significant source of domestic contribution profit for the time being.[^s2]

The third stage was content. Netflix moved into original programming in 2013, and the first season of *House of Cards* won three Primetime Emmys. International expansion, under way in Canada, Latin America, the Caribbean and Europe by 2012, reached more than 190 countries and territories by 2016.[^s1] Netflix announced in April 2023 that it would end DVD-by-mail and stopped mailing discs on September 29, 2023. It ended 2023 with about 260.3 million paid memberships and $33.7 billion in revenue.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The case is usually read through disruption theory. Christensen and colleagues cite Blockbuster's response to Netflix, which bolted an online offering onto its store network, as one of several clumsy "hybrid" responses by incumbents. They also note a competing strand of research arguing that hybrids can help firms learn and bridge a transition.[^s4] Netflix itself ran a DVD-plus-streaming hybrid for years. Its experience suggests that the hybrid was less of a problem than how the company tried to unwind it.[^s2][^s4]

The Auckland study frames the shift as an example of dynamic capabilities: sensing a change in broadband, seizing it with small experiments, and reconfiguring the company. It credits a culture that tolerated failed experiments, while treating Qwikster as evidence of the costs of moving too fast.[^s5] A second critique concerns economics rather than timing. Streaming swapped the DVD model's owned inventory for multi-year, fixed-fee licensing commitments, which totalled about $3.9 billion at the end of 2011 and $21.7 billion at the end of 2023.[^s2][^s3] The bet's payoff was therefore exposed to content costs that the DVD business never carried.

## Lessons for managers

The case is used to discuss when to cannibalize a profitable core and how to sequence it. Netflix's pricing change and brand split landed in the same quarter and triggered cancellations. The company then let DVD shrink for more than a decade, running it as a separate segment until it stopped mailing discs in 2023.[^s2][^s3] It also shows a subscription relationship being carried across delivery technologies: the flat monthly fee of 2000 remained the revenue model in 2023.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The case is a standard example of **disruptive innovation** and, in its later stages, of **business-model innovation**: the subscription stayed while the delivery channel and product changed.[^s1][^s4] Its deliberate **cannibalization** of the DVD business, and the period in which Netflix ran both businesses, make it a reference point for **organizational ambidexterity** and **The Innovator's Dilemma**.[^s2][^s4] The Auckland researchers explicitly tie it to **dynamic capabilities** and repeated **strategic renewal**.[^s5] It is often set against the **Kodak decline**, where an incumbent did not escape a dying physical-media business. It rests on a **subscription business model** led by co-founder **Reed Hastings**.[^s1][^s2]
