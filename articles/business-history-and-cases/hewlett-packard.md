# Hewlett-Packard

**Hewlett-Packard Company (HP)** was an American electronics and computing company started on 1 January 1939 by William R. Hewlett and David Packard, two young Stanford electrical-engineering graduates who had begun building equipment in a Palo Alto garage the year before.[^s1] Its first notable customer was Walt Disney Productions, which bought eight audio oscillators for the film *Fantasia* in 1940.[^s1] Over seven decades HP moved from test instruments to computers, calculators and printers, became a reference point for Silicon Valley management culture, and in 2015 divided itself into two listed companies.[^s1][^s6] For business students it is a case in founder culture, diversification, contested mergers and corporate break-ups.

## From instruments to computers and printers

HP grew by steady product diversification. It went public in 1957 to fund new products, bought the graph-recorder maker F.L. Moseley in 1958 and entered medical instruments through the 1961 Sanborn acquisition.[^s1] Its first computer, the HP 2116A, followed in 1966, then desktop and pocket calculators and the HP 3000 minicomputer in 1972.[^s1] The Computer History Museum notes that the 2116A was first designed to control HP's own test instruments, and that by 1978 HP ranked second among minicomputer makers.[^s2] Not every bet paid off: Britannica records that HP passed on an intern's personal-computer prototype in 1976, which Steve Wozniak then took to Apple, and that its 1980 HP-85 desktop suffered from incompatibility with the IBM PC.[^s1] The 1984 LaserJet became its most successful product line.[^s1] HP spun off its instrument business as Agilent Technologies in 1999 and in 2007 became the first technology company to pass $100 billion in annual sales.[^s1]

## The HP Way

HP's founders codified an informal management philosophy, later called the HP Way, that stressed open communication, integrity, teamwork and respect for customers, competitors and employees.[^s2] Two of its practices became widely imitated. One was management by objectives, which the founders adopted early.[^s1] The other was management by walking around: Hewlett and Packard regularly toured factory floors and labs, on the premise that nothing replaces personal involvement and one-to-one conversation with staff.[^s3][^s2] The company archive presents the practice as a way to make sure employees at every level had the support needed to do their best work.[^s3] An open-door policy and generous corporate giving, up to 4.4 percent of pretax profit, rounded out the model.[^s2][^s1] The Computer History Museum describes the HP Way as a template many later Silicon Valley start-ups copied.[^s2]

## Fiorina, Compaq and the proxy fight

By the mid-1990s HP's growth had slowed, and critics argued it lacked a clear answer to the industry's main strategic challenges.[^s4] The board then hired Carly Fiorina, its first CEO from outside the company; Robert Burgelman's Stanford case "New HP Way" (2000) follows her attempt to reorganize HP to move faster against nimbler rivals.[^s4] Her signature move, the acquisition of Compaq, was approved unanimously by HP's board on 3 September 2001 with projected cost synergies of $2.5 billion.[^s5] Walter Hewlett opposed the deal and campaigned against it in the shareholder vote.[^s5] Shareholders approved the deal on 19 March 2002 by a preliminary margin of about 45 million votes, and Hewlett then sued, alleging misleading integration disclosures and pressure on Deutsche Bank to switch its vote.[^s5] In April 2002 the Delaware Court of Chancery ruled for HP on both claims, finding no credible evidence of coercion.[^s5] Fiorina was forced out in 2005; her successor Mark Hurd left in 2010, and Meg Whitman became CEO in 2011, the year HP bought Autonomy for $11.1 billion.[^s1]

## The 2015 separation

Announced in October 2014, the split took effect through a pro rata spin-off distributed on 1 November 2015 to holders of record on 21 October, one Hewlett Packard Enterprise share for each HP share.[^s6] The renamed HP Inc. kept the printing and personal-systems businesses and the HPQ ticker, while Hewlett Packard Enterprise took enterprise hardware, services, software and financial services, a business with $55 billion of revenue in fiscal 2014.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

HP is cited both as a model and as a warning. Its founders' practices shaped management teaching, yet Burgelman's case frames the late-1990s company as slow and strategically unclear despite that culture.[^s4] The Compaq merger was fought out in a close shareholder vote and in court, and the court record shows internal integration estimates diverging from public synergy targets even as the vote went ahead, although the judge found the public statements supportable when made.[^s5] Two CEOs forced out within five years, a third replaced soon after, and then a break-up suggest the HP Way did not prevent strategic drift at scale.[^s1]

## Connections

HP was **founded by William Hewlett and David Packard** and **operates in the computer industry**.[^s1] It **exemplifies management by objectives** and **management by walking around**, the best-known practices of its **organizational culture**.[^s1][^s3] Its 2015 separation **exemplifies a corporate spin-off**.[^s6] Its printer and ink-cartridge business is the standard modern illustration of the **razor-and-blades model**, it was an early Western adopter of **hoshin kanri**, and its printer supply chain is a classic example of the **bullwhip effect**, each covered in those articles. It invites comparison with **Intel**, a fellow Silicon Valley firm that renewed itself, and with **Apple**, which grew from the prototype HP declined.[^s1][^s2]
