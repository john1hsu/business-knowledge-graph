# Xerox

**Xerox** is an American document-technology company that began in 1906 as the Haloid Company, a maker of photographic paper, and in 1947 acquired commercial rights to xerography, the dry imaging process Chester Carlson had first demonstrated in 1938.[^s1] It became Haloid Xerox in 1958, launched the 914, its first commercial plain-paper copier, in 1959, and took the name Xerox Corporation in 1961.[^s1] Business schools return to Xerox for three lessons: how a company that invented much of personal computing captured little of its value, how an American incumbent fought back against cheaper and better foreign rivals, and how earnings pressure led to an accounting enforcement case.

## Invention without capture: PARC

Xerox created the Fuji Xerox joint venture with Fujifilm in 1962 and in 1970 founded PARC, a research centre in Palo Alto, California, whose work included laser printing and Ethernet.[^s1] Henry Chesbrough and Richard Rosenbloom's 2002 study in *Industrial and Corporate Change* uses Xerox to explain why technical brilliance does not guarantee profit.[^s2] They argue that a business model is the mechanism that converts technical potential into economic value, and that Xerox's own model had let it commercialise copying technology other leading firms had turned down.[^s2] The same successful logic, however, shaped how Xerox evaluated PARC's later inventions and spin-offs, filtering out opportunities that did not fit it.[^s2] The PARC spin-offs that thrived built business models quite different from Xerox's, while those that failed showed little search for or learning about a workable model.[^s2] The study is a founding text for the idea that a dominant logic can blind an incumbent, and it underpins the "false negatives" argument in the open-innovation literature.

## Quality recovery and benchmarking

By the mid-1970s foreign and domestic competitors had overtaken Xerox on copier cost and quality, and the company lost world market share through the 1970s and early 1980s.[^s3] Its Leadership Through Quality strategy, which grew out of a quality-improvement programme begun in 1984, halted and then reversed that loss.[^s3] Xerox's Business Products and Systems unit won the 1989 Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award; NIST's profile notes that it tracked about 240 performance attributes, each with a target set at the level of the world leader in that attribute regardless of industry.[^s3] Over five years the unit reported 78 percent fewer defects per 100 machines, 40 percent less unscheduled maintenance and 73 percent fewer defective supplier parts reaching production, and it became the first in its industry to offer a three-year warranty.[^s3] Setting targets against the best performer in any industry is the practice that later made Xerox the textbook case of competitive benchmarking.[^s3]

## Accounting enforcement

The recovery did not end the pressure on earnings. In April 2002 the SEC charged that between 1997 and 2000 Xerox used undisclosed accounting actions, most allegedly outside GAAP, to meet Wall Street expectations.[^s4] The core technique was shifting more of each equipment lease's value into the up-front "box" sale, which accelerated recognition of over $3 billion in equipment revenue and raised pre-tax earnings by about $1.5 billion.[^s4] Without admitting or denying the claims, Xerox paid a $10 million civil penalty, agreed to restate its 1997–2000 results and had outside directors review its internal accounting controls.[^s4]

## Later restructuring

Anne Mulcahy became Xerox's first female CEO in 2001 and was succeeded by Ursula Burns in 2009.[^s1] The company bought services firm Affiliated Computer Services in 2010 but spun its services businesses off as Conduent in 2017, and a planned 2018 combination with Fujifilm was abandoned after shareholder litigation.[^s1] In July 2025 it completed the acquisition of printer maker Lexmark for about $768 million.[^s5] For fiscal 2025 Xerox reported revenue of $7.02 billion, up 12.9 percent because of Lexmark but down 7.6 percent on a pro forma basis, and a GAAP net loss of $1.03 billion.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The "Xerox fumbled the future" story is often told as managerial blindness, but Chesbrough and Rosenbloom frame it more precisely: the firm's copier model was successful, and its very success constrained the search for new models.[^s2] The Baldrige results show measurable operational gains, yet the SEC case shows that quality leadership did not protect the company from pressure to manage reported earnings.[^s3][^s4] The 2025 results, with acquisition-driven growth masking underlying decline, show a mature business still searching for a growth model.[^s5]

## Connections

Xerox **exemplifies benchmarking** and **total quality management** through Leadership Through Quality, and its Baldrige win links it to the **Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award**.[^s3] The **hoshin kanri** article records Xerox among Western adopters of policy deployment. The PARC spin-off study ties it to **business model** theory and to **open innovation**.[^s2] The SEC case makes it an example of **accounting fraud** through revenue acceleration.[^s4] It **operates in the office-equipment industry**.[^s1] It invites comparison with the **Kodak decline**, another imaging incumbent whose core product lost ground, and with the **IBM turnaround**, a contemporaneous recovery of a U.S. technology incumbent; like those firms, Xerox shows how a successful model can constrain later choices.[^s2]
