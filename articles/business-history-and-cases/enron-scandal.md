# Enron Scandal: Off-Balance-Sheet Fraud and the 2001 Collapse of Enron

The **Enron scandal** is the collapse of Enron Corp., the Houston energy-trading company that filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on December 2, 2001, after it emerged that its reported profits and balance sheet had been propped up by undisclosed partnerships and side deals.[^s5] At the time of the filing Enron was the seventh-largest publicly traded US company, reporting more than $100 billion in gross revenues for 2000 and employing over 20,000 people.[^s1] The case became the defining example of how weak boards, conflicted executives and compliant gatekeepers can let a firm deceive capital markets for years.[^s1][^s6]

## How the fraud worked

The core device was the **special purpose entity (SPE)**: a separate vehicle that, if structured properly, could hold assets or debt off the sponsor's consolidated balance sheet. The SEC alleged that CFO Andrew Fastow used SPEs he secretly controlled or that were guaranteed against loss, such as Chewco and the LJM partnerships, so that Enron overstated net income and understated debt.[^s2] In the Nigerian barges deal, for example, Enron booked roughly $12 million of 1999 earnings from a "sale" backed by a secret promise to buy the asset back at a guaranteed return.[^s2] The Raptor I–IV vehicles, which involved a put on Enron's own stock, were used to "hedge" investment losses, again allegedly supported by an undisclosed side deal.[^s3]

SPEs were only part of the scheme. The SEC's case against chief accounting officer Richard Causey also alleged inflated valuations of merchant assets to hit budget targets, reserve accounts used to smooth trading profits, and a resegmentation that moved losses from Enron Energy Services into another unit to hide them.[^s3] By an October 2000 company presentation, about $27 billion of Enron's roughly $60 billion in assets, nearly half, sat with unconsolidated affiliates, and its 1999 and 2000 annual reports listed almost 3,000 related entities.[^s1]

## Collapse and aftermath

Enron's stock reached about $90 in mid-2000.[^s5] In October 2001 the company announced a $638 million third-quarter loss and a $1.2 billion cut to shareholders' equity, partly tied to Fastow's partnerships; an SEC investigation followed, a rescue merger with Dynegy fell apart in November, and the shares fell below $1 before the bankruptcy filing.[^s5] Employees whose 401(k) plans were concentrated in Enron stock recovered only a fraction of their savings.[^s5]

Prosecution was extensive. Auditor Arthur Andersen was convicted in June 2002 of obstruction for shredding evidence and lost its licence to audit public companies; the Supreme Court unanimously overturned the verdict about three years later, too late to save the firm.[^s5] Fastow pleaded guilty and received a six-year sentence.[^s5] On May 25, 2006, a Houston jury convicted former chairman and CEO Kenneth Lay on all six counts against him and former CEO Jeffrey Skilling on 19 of 28 counts, finding that from at least 1999 to 2001 they misled investors about Enron's growth, debt, credit quality and cash flow.[^s4] Lay died before sentencing; Skilling served about 12 years.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations concluded in July 2002 that Enron's board failed its fiduciary duty: it knowingly accepted high-risk accounting after repeated Andersen briefings to the audit committee, waived the code of conduct so Fastow could run the LJM funds, permitted billions in undisclosed off-the-books activity, approved excessive pay, and lacked independence because of directors' financial ties to the company.[^s1] The directors themselves disputed that verdict, saying they had relied on management, auditors and counsel; the Subcommittee rejected the defence.[^s1]

Healy and Palepu widen the lens. They argue that Enron's inflated valuation raised doubts about whether capital-market intermediaries, regulators and governance experts were doing their jobs, and that those intermediaries' own incentive and governance problems helped the firm rise and fall.[^s6] This view frames Enron less as a few bad actors and more as a system-wide gatekeeper failure, which is why reforms targeted auditors and boards rather than Enron alone.[^s6][^s1]

## Connections

Enron is the standard case for **corporate governance** and **agency costs**: managers with private information, weak board and **audit committee** oversight, and conflicted gatekeepers let the **information asymmetry** between insiders and shareholders be exploited.[^s1][^s6] Its abuse of **special purpose entities** to manage reported results is why it sits under accounting fraud and corporate scandals.[^s2][^s3] The board's failures are a textbook breach of **fiduciary duty**.[^s1] The scandal directly prompted the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act**, which, among other things, stiffened penalties for destroying records and barred audit firms from consulting for the same clients, a response to the dual auditor-consultant role that **Arthur Andersen** played at Enron.[^s5][^s1]
