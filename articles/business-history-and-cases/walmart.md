# Walmart

Walmart Inc. is a U.S. retailer headquartered in Bentonville, Arkansas. Sam Walton, who had run a Ben Franklin variety-store franchise in Bentonville for about a decade, opened the first Wal-Mart Discount City in Rogers, Arkansas, in 1962.[^s1] The company went public in 1970, listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 1972, and changed its legal name from Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. to Walmart Inc. in 2018.[^s1] It is a core business-school case because a rural discounter with no obvious advantages became the world's largest private employer by 1999 largely through logistics, information systems and disciplined geographic expansion.

## History

Walton's early strategy was to put discount stores in small Southern and Midwestern towns that national chains ignored. By the end of the 1960s the company had 18 Walmart stores and $30.8 million in annual sales; by the end of the 1970s, 276 stores and $1.24 billion; and by the end of the 1980s, about 1,528 stores in 27 states and nearly $26 billion.[^s1] It opened its first Sam's Club warehouse club in Midwest City, Oklahoma, in 1983, its first Supercenter combining general merchandise with groceries in the late 1980s, and expanded during the 1990s into Mexico, Canada, China, Germany and the United Kingdom.[^s1] In 1999 it had about 1.14 million employees, making it the world's largest private employer.[^s1] More recently it bought Jet.com (2016) to build e-commerce capability.[^s1]

As of fiscal 2026 (year ended January 31, 2026), Walmart reported total revenues of $713.2 billion, with net sales of $483.0 billion at Walmart U.S., $130.4 billion at Walmart International and $93.0 billion at Sam's Club U.S.[^s4] It operated more than 10,900 stores and clubs in 19 countries, employed about 2.1 million associates, and said e-commerce sales reached $150.4 billion, up 46%.[^s4] Its U.S. network relied on 192 distribution facilities served largely by a private truck fleet.[^s4]

## How the low-cost machine worked

Economist Emek Basker attributes Walmart's advantage mainly to technology and scale. The company linked its stores, distribution centres and headquarters by computer by the late 1970s, installed bar-code readers in all distribution centres by the late 1980s (reportedly halving shipment-processing labour costs), and launched Retail Link in 1990 to share sales and inventory data directly with suppliers.[^s2] Britannica adds early adoption of point-of-sale scanning and a private satellite network connecting every unit to headquarters.[^s1]

Geography mattered as much as computers. Walmart clustered stores around its distribution centres, expanding outward from Arkansas so that each new store sat close to existing ones and to a warehouse.[^s1][^s3] Thomas Holmes modelled this rollout and inferred the value of density from how much sales cannibalization among neighbouring stores Walmart was willing to accept; he concluded the benefits of density were large and went well beyond cheaper trucking.[^s3] Basker cites an estimate that placing a store next to a distribution centre rather than 100 miles away added about $220,000 in annual operating profit (2005 dollars), and research estimating that a 10% rise in volume cut marginal cost by about 2%.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The productivity evidence is strong. In Basker's figures, sales per worker in general merchandise retailing rose 35.3 log points from 1982 to 2002, versus 54.5 at Walmart U.S. and 18.5 for the rest of the sector, so Walmart accounted for almost half the segment's productivity growth.[^s2] Consumers benefited: studies she reviews found Supercenter grocery prices around 10% below competitors' and short-run price cuts of 1–2% by rival grocers after entry.[^s2]

The costs are contested. Estimates of local job effects range from small net gains to net losses, one study found that ten new stores lowered average state retail wages by about 2%, and entry is associated with the closure of several small competitors in a typical county.[^s2] Britannica notes long-running criticism of low wages, the company's anti-union stance and its displacement of small-town merchants.[^s1]

## Connections

Walmart operates in the retail industry and was created by Sam Walton; Sam's Club is part of the company and the main rival to Costco in the membership warehouse club model.[^s1][^s4] It is the canonical example of economies of density, a geographic form of economies of scale, and Holmes's study shows how a firm can rationally trade off cannibalization against logistics savings.[^s3] Retail Link and its distribution network make it a reference case in supply chain management, and research on its entry effects on prices, wages and rivals is a staple of industrial organization.[^s2]
