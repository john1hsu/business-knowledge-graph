# General Motors: From Sloan's Decentralization to the 2009 Bankruptcy

General Motors (GM) is the American automaker that William C. Durant formed in 1908 by combining several car companies, among them Buick, Cadillac and Oakland, the brand later sold as Pontiac.[^s1] It added Chevrolet in 1918, created the financing arm GMAC in 1919, and overtook Ford as the leading US automaker by 1929; for most of the twentieth century it was the world's largest vehicle manufacturer.[^s1] In business education GM matters for two reasons: it is the classic case of the decentralized multidivisional organization, and its slow decline and 2009 bankruptcy are a standard case of an incumbent losing to rivals with better operating practices.

## Durant, du Pont and Sloan's reorganization

Durant built GM through acquisition rather than integration, and in 1920 he lost control of the company to Pierre S. du Pont and John J. Raskob.[^s2] Alfred P. Sloan Jr., who had come to GM when his parts business joined it, became an operating vice president under the new owners and was made president and chief executive in 1923; he stayed CEO until 1946 and chairman until 1956.[^s2]

Sloan's answer to the sprawl Durant left behind had two halves. He grouped the car makers into five divisions, each aimed at a different price band, and he let each division run its own operations while a strong head office, with large financial and advisory staffs, set and coordinated company-wide policy.[^s2] Under this model GM passed Ford in US sales in the late 1920s, became the world's biggest business corporation, and eventually took more than half of American car sales.[^s2] Sloan described the system in his 1964 memoir, *My Years with General Motors*.[^s2]

## GM as the model of the M-form

Business historian Alfred D. Chandler Jr. made GM a central case in *Strategy and Structure* (1962), which traced how large US firms moved from a single functional structure to a divisional one as their markets grew, and which praised Sloan's 1920s decentralization.[^s3] Chandler's argument was that the multidivisional (M-form) structure freed senior executives from day-to-day operations so they could concentrate on long-range planning and resource allocation, and his work helped drive the wave of decentralization in big companies in the 1960s and 1970s.[^s3] The same account notes that the classic multidivisional form has since fallen out of fashion.[^s3]

## Decline

By Helper and Henderson's figures, GM's share of the US market fell from 62.6 percent in 1980 to 19.8 percent in 2009.[^s4] Helper and Henderson call the usual explanation, heavy pension and retiree health costs, seriously incomplete: in their analysis the larger causes were weaker design, quality and productivity, rooted in GM's inability to build the trust-based "relational contracts" with workers and suppliers that underpinned Toyota's system.[^s4] They point to evidence that money and access were not the constraint: GM spent more than $45 billion on acquisitions and automation in 1980–85, and from 1984 it had a close view of Toyota's methods through the NUMMI joint venture in California, yet it took more than two decades to imitate those practices consistently.[^s4] Legacy costs were real (about $1,600 per vehicle in 2005, by their figures), but the authors argue they were partly a consequence of shrinking share rather than its root cause.[^s4]

## Bankruptcy and the "new GM"

GM received federal loans in 2008 as the financial crisis hit.[^s1] It filed for Chapter 11 on June 1, 2009, and after about 40 days a new company bought most of the old company's assets; the US Treasury received a 60.8 percent stake in the new GM.[^s5] Treasury's total assistance to old and new GM came to about $50.2 billion. It sold shares in the November 2010 IPO and finished selling its stake on December 9, 2013, ending with a net shortfall of about $10.5 billion.[^s5] In 2010 GM cut its divisions to four (Buick, Cadillac, Chevrolet and GMC), having already dropped Oldsmobile in 2004 and then Pontiac and Saturn.[^s1]

The successor, General Motors Company, is a Delaware corporation formed in 2009.[^s6] According to its fiscal 2025 10-K, it had about 155,000 employees at the end of 2025, reported three segments (GM North America, GM International and GM Financial), and held 17.2 percent of US industry vehicle sales in 2025.[^s6]

## Connections

GM is the standard illustration of the **multidivisional form**, through Sloan's divisions-plus-central-control design and Chandler's history of it.[^s3] Its price-band divisions are an early example of **segmentation**: each division sold to a different price range.[^s2] GM **competes with Ford**, which it overtook in the late 1920s,[^s2] and its approach of offering several brands at different prices **contrasts with** the single-model strategy of the **Ford Model T**. It also **competes with Toyota**, whose production system, which GM saw at first hand through NUMMI, explains much of GM's lost share.[^s4] The 1980–2009 decline makes GM a canonical case of **corporate decline** in the **automotive industry**, and its slow adoption of Toyota's methods shows how hard it was to move beyond its own **mass production** system.[^s4]
