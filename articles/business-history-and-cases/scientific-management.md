# Scientific Management

Scientific management, often called Taylorism, is the system of industrial management associated with Frederick W. Taylor (1856-1915), whom Britannica and OpenStax both describe as its father.[^s1][^s3] Its premise is that the way work is done should be set by systematic study rather than by tradition or the craftsman's rule of thumb, and that designing the work is a job for management. Taylor's system shaped industrial engineering and production management worldwide, and his time-and-motion approach fed directly into mass production.[^s3]

## Origin

Taylor joined Midvale Steel in 1878 as a machine-shop laborer and rose to chief engineer; in 1881 he began timing shop tasks there, the work that became the base of his theories.[^s3] After Midvale he worked as a consulting engineer, a profession he helped create, and his biggest client was Bethlehem Steel, where he developed high-speed tool steel and ran his shoveling and pig-iron handling studies.[^s3] His ideas appeared first in papers such as "Shop Management" (1903) in the transactions of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, which elected him president in 1906, and then in the commercially published *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911).[^s3]

The problem Taylor set out to solve was what he called soldiering: workers deliberately holding output down, partly from natural inclination but mostly as a systematic defence against piece-rate cuts and job losses.[^s2] He argued that management's aim should be the greatest prosperity for both employer and employee, and that only higher productivity could deliver both.[^s2]

## How it works

Taylor assigned management four duties: build a science for each element of a job to replace rule of thumb; scientifically select, train and develop each worker; cooperate with workers so the work follows that science; and divide work and responsibility between managers and workers, with managers taking the planning they are better suited to do.[^s2][^s1] In practice this meant breaking jobs into elements, timing each with a stopwatch, rebuilding the job around the fastest method, issuing written instructions, standardizing tools, scheduling rest, and paying differential piece rates that rewarded output above the standard.[^s1]

His best-known illustration is pig-iron loading at Bethlehem. Taylor reported that a gang averaging about 12.5 tons per man per day could, with selected and coached men such as the worker he called Schmidt, reach about 47 tons, while pay rose from about $1.15 to $1.85 a day.[^s2] Associates extended the method: Henry Gantt devised the chart that tracks planned against actual progress, Carl Barth studied fatigue and built calculating slide rules, and Frank and Lillian Gilbreth developed motion study, with Lillian giving more weight to the worker's perspective.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The system met hostility. Unions and many workers resented it, especially when pushed to extremes, and in 1912 Taylor testified before a special committee of the US House of Representatives investigating his and other shop-management systems.[^s3] OpenStax summarizes the standing criticisms: Taylor treated people like machine tools, overrated money as a motivator against group pressure, ignored strategy, and offered a method for supervisors rather than a full theory of management.[^s1]

Historians have also questioned the record. Daniel Nelson's study of actual installations found that practice resembled Taylor's published account only superficially, and he reads Taylorism less as a solution to the labour problem than as a refinement of the earlier systematic-management movement for coordinating factory work.[^s4] Charles Wrege, who spent decades in the Taylor archives, argued that the pig-iron studies were largely run and written up by Taylor's assistants, that the anecdotes in *Principles* were embellished, and that the book was substantially drafted by Morris L. Cooke.[^s5]

Still, the long-run productivity case is strong. Peter Drucker called the fifty-fold rise in manual-worker productivity management's central achievement of the twentieth century, and argued that the methods behind it would not work for knowledge workers, whose productivity was the next challenge.[^s6]

## In practice

Taylor's legacy is visible wherever work is standardized and measured. Industrial engineering and production management grew from his system, and his time-and-motion methods shaped mass production.[^s3] Modern incentive pay, down to the sales commission, descends from his differential piece rate, and OpenStax notes that later management researchers built on his work rather than discarding it.[^s1] The practical lesson managers usually draw is double-edged: measured, standardized work raises output, but imposing it without workers' cooperation, which Taylor himself listed as a duty of management, invites the resistance his system met.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Scientific management sits under **management theory** as its first systematic school; it was **created by** Frederick W. Taylor and **introduced in** *The Principles of Scientific Management*.[^s3] Nelson's work supports the **influencedBy** edge to **systematic management**, its predecessor.[^s4] It **influenced** **mass production**, the **Ford Model T** being the classic case, and the **Gantt chart**, built by Taylor's associate.[^s3][^s1] It was designed to **mitigate** **soldiering**, the deliberate restriction of output.[^s2] **Henri Fayol** is its administrative-school counterpart; **total quality management**, the **Toyota Production System** and **material requirements planning** are later operations approaches in the same lineage of studying and planning production work.[^s1]
