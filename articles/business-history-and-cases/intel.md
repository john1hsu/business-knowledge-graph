# Intel

**Intel Corporation** is an American semiconductor maker that Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore, both formerly of Fairchild Semiconductor, incorporated on 18 July 1968 to build memory chips.[^s1] In 1971 it began selling the 4004, which the Computer History Museum describes as the first microprocessor offered as a component, and after Japanese producers came to dominate memory, Intel shifted its business to microprocessors for PCs and data centres.[^s1] For business scholars Intel is a canonical case of strategic renewal: a firm that abandoned the product it invented, rebuilt its manufacturing against foreign competition and turned a hidden component into a consumer brand.

## The memory exit

Intel had invented DRAM chips and long thought of itself as the DRAM company, so its 1985 withdrawal from that market was a defining turning point.[^s3] Robert Burgelman's field study of Intel's memory and microprocessor businesses, published in *Administrative Science Quarterly* in 1994, explains how it happened.[^s2] He found that inertia let the competence Intel was proud of fall behind the changing basis of competition in memory, while the stated corporate strategy drifted away from what middle managers were actually doing.[^s2] Crucially, Intel's internal resource-allocation rules had already been moving scarce fabrication capacity toward the more profitable microprocessor business before top management formally changed strategy.[^s2] Burgelman called this the work of the firm's "internal selection environment", and it became a standard illustration of how strategy can emerge bottom-up.[^s2] His companion teaching case on Intel's position around 1990 covers the follow-on questions: aligning technology strategy with industry dynamics, commodity versus proprietary products such as EPROM and flash, the RISC-versus-CISC debate and forward integration.[^s3]

## Rebuilding manufacturing

Leaving DRAMs did not remove Intel's production problem. Christophe Lécuyer's 2019 *Business History Review* study shows that Japanese chipmakers outperformed Intel in manufacturing from the late 1970s through the mid-1980s, and that by 1985 management saw this weakness as a threat to the firm's survival.[^s4] Top management responded with a sweeping overhaul: engineers adopted Japanese production technologies and operating routines, put wafer fabrication on a more scientific footing, created new ways to transfer processes from development into production and standardised the company's factories.[^s4] Lécuyer concludes that these reforms brought Intel to manufacturing parity with Japanese rivals by the early 1990s.[^s4]

## Intel Inside

As PCs reached ordinary buyers, Intel was well known to computer makers but invisible to consumers.[^s5] The Intel Inside program, officially launched in 1991 and developed by Intel's Dennis Carter with an outside advertising partner, subsidised original equipment manufacturers that put the Intel logo on their machines and in their advertising.[^s5] By the end of 1992 more than 500 OEMs had joined, and 70 percent of eligible OEM ads carried the logo; Intel's own history notes that the initial $250 million commitment drew scepticism before *Advertising Age* praised it as the most effective co-op program ever.[^s5] The campaign is the standard example of ingredient branding for a technology component.[^s5]

## Recent restructuring

By fiscal 2025 Intel reported revenue of $52.9 billion (versus $53.1 billion in 2024) and a net loss attributable to Intel of $267 million, with about 85,100 employees at year-end.[^s6] It now reports Intel Products (client and data-centre segments) alongside Intel Foundry, which makes chips for outside customers.[^s6] In August 2025 it agreed to issue 275 million shares to the U.S. Department of Commerce, tied to accelerated CHIPS Act disbursements of $5.7 billion, plus a warrant exercisable if Intel's ownership of its foundry business falls below 51 percent.[^s6] It also sold equity to SoftBank ($2.0 billion) and NVIDIA ($5.0 billion), and sold 51 percent of its Altera programmable-chip unit in September 2025.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Intel's history cuts both ways for management theory. Burgelman's work credits the firm's internal selection with saving it, yet the same study stresses that formal strategy lagged reality, a warning that success depended partly on middle managers rather than foresight at the top.[^s2] Lécuyer's account shows that recovering from a manufacturing deficit took most of a decade of organisational reform.[^s4] The 2025 filing, with flat revenue, a net loss and government and partner capital, shows that leadership in process technology, once regained, was not permanent.[^s6]

## Connections

Intel **exemplifies strategic renewal** and **dynamic capabilities**: Burgelman's study is about how a firm reconfigures resources as the basis of advantage shifts.[^s2] It **exemplifies ingredient branding** through Intel Inside.[^s5] It was **founded by Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore** and **operates in the semiconductor industry**.[^s1] Intel is also the setting in which Andy Grove developed **OKRs**, and an early corporate reader of **The Innovator's Dilemma**, both covered in those articles. Its renewal invites comparison with the **Kodak decline**, where a dominant firm failed to escape its core product, and with the **IBM turnaround**, another 1990s recovery of a U.S. technology incumbent.[^s2][^s4]
