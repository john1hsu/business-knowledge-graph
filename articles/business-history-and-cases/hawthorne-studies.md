# Hawthorne Studies

The Hawthorne studies were a long-running series of workplace experiments at the Hawthorne Works, the plant outside Chicago where Western Electric, AT&T's manufacturing arm, made telephone equipment.[^s1][^s6] Beginning in 1924 as an engineering test of lighting and ending in 1933, they became the founding case of the human relations school: the claim that output depends on workers' attitudes, social ties and supervision, not only on pay and physical conditions.[^s2][^s3] They are also one of business history's most re-examined episodes, because later scholars found the evidence for their best-known results far weaker than textbooks implied.[^s4][^s5]

## What happened

**Illumination tests (1924-1927).** Western Electric engineers varied lighting in three manufacturing departments to see how it affected efficiency, with support from the National Research Council and the Rockefeller Foundation, and later Harvard Business School.[^s1] Accounts of the tests found no meaningful link between light levels and productivity, which pushed the researchers toward other explanations.[^s1] The popular version holds that output rose whether lighting was brightened or dimmed.[^s3]

**Relay Assembly Test Room (1927-1932).** The company moved a small group of young women who assembled telephone relays into a separate room, where a recording device punched a hole in a paper tape for every finished relay, giving a precise output record.[^s1] Over five years researchers changed rest periods, hours and incentive pay; output rose substantially, and the operators said the room gave them more freedom than the shop floor and built strong friendships.[^s2] Supervisors could not tell whether the gains came from rest breaks, shorter hours, wages, the small group or the attention itself.[^s1] In 1928 superintendent George Pennock asked Harvard's Elton Mayo to interpret the results, and Mayo and his protégé Fritz Roethlisberger concluded that mental attitudes, good supervision and informal group relations were the keys to productivity.[^s1][^s2]

**Bank Wiring Observation Room.** A later study of fourteen men in the bank wiring room found no rise in output, partly because the men had an informal understanding not to exceed what they judged a fair day's work.[^s2]

## Interpretation and influence

OpenStax summarizes the lesson managers took away: productivity rests on a complex set of employee attitudes, being singled out for study created group pride, and supervisors who gave workers some control raised motivation further.[^s3] Attention to employees' welfare, rather than economic incentive alone, came to be seen as a motivator in its own right, a direct challenge to the classical era's emphasis on pay.[^s3] The test-room conclusions became the empirical base of the human relations movement and of organizational behavior as a field.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The data have been contested for decades. In 1978 Franke and Kaul applied regression to the first relay experiment and argued that measured variables explained more than 90 percent of the variance in output, leaving little room for human-relations factors.[^s5] Milton Bloombaum's 1983 reanalysis disputed this, attributing their result to algebraic artifacts, confounded variables and violated assumptions, and found the original researchers' conclusions broadly supported.[^s5]

The illumination story fared worse. Steven Levitt and John List recovered the original lighting data, long thought destroyed and never formally analyzed, and reported that the striking patterns described in textbooks were fictional, although subtler observation effects did appear.[^s4] Separately, the "Hawthorne effect" label, which first appeared in a 1953 research-methods textbook as a name for changes in behavior caused by being studied, has drifted in meaning; a 2014 systematic review of 19 studies found some evidence of research participation effects but no single, well-defined effect, and recommended retiring the unqualified term.[^s6]

## Why it matters for managers

The case still teaches two things. Substantively, it shifted management from treating workers as interchangeable inputs toward attending to group dynamics, supervision and morale, the agenda later pursued by motivation and team research.[^s2][^s3] Methodologically, it warns that a pilot program's results may reflect the novelty and attention of the pilot rather than the change being tested, so improvement initiatives need comparison groups and careful measurement before scaling.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

The studies sit under **management theory** as the episode that opened its human relations school, and they **contrast with** **scientific management**, which treated output mainly as a matter of method and pay.[^s3] They were **created by** company engineers working with **Elton Mayo** and **Fritz Roethlisberger**, and they **influenced** the **human relations movement**, **organizational behavior** and the research-design concept of the **Hawthorne effect**.[^s2][^s6] The bank-wiring findings relate to **group norms**, the test-room results to **employee motivation**, and the stress on worker participation to later **total quality management**.[^s2][^s3]
