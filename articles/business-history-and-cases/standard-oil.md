# Standard Oil: Rockefeller's Trust and the 1911 Antitrust Breakup

**Standard Oil** was the oil refining and marketing combine that John D. Rockefeller and his partners assembled in the late nineteenth century. At its peak it refined the overwhelming majority of American oil, and its 1911 court-ordered breakup remains the defining case in US antitrust history and a standard business-school example of how scale, integration and aggressive tactics can build, and then lose, a dominant position.[^s1][^s3]

## Origins and growth

Rockefeller entered Cleveland's refining business in 1863 with Maurice B. Clark and Samuel Andrews, bought out Clark in 1865, and added Henry M. Flagler as a partner in 1867. The firm was incorporated in Ohio as the Standard Oil Company in 1870, by which time the partners already ran Cleveland's largest refineries.[^s1] Growth came from consolidating rival refineries and from negotiating favourable freight rates with railroads; a 1906 federal Commissioner of Corporations report singled out secret railroad rebates as a key source of Standard's dominance.[^s3] By 1880 the company refined an estimated 90 to 95 percent of US oil output, and the trust still controlled about 90 percent of refined oil around 1890.[^s1][^s3]

## Organizational structure

Rockefeller's group first held its companies together through a **trust**. Under the 1882 Trust Agreement nine trustees, Rockefeller among them, took control of the shares of the constituent companies, eventually about 40 corporations, 14 of them wholly owned.[^s1] The Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved in 1892, but it continued to operate from New York. In 1899 the New Jersey affiliate was renamed Standard Oil Company (New Jersey) and became a **holding company** that received the trust's assets.[^s1] The company integrated both horizontally across refining and vertically into pipelines and distribution.[^s3]

## The antitrust case

Investigative journalist Ida Tarbell, who grew up in the Pennsylvania oil region, published a nineteen-part exposé in *McClure's Magazine* from 1902 and a best-selling book in 1904, accusing the company of unfair tactics that ruined rivals and helping to build political pressure for action.[^s3] The federal government sued in 1906 under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, alleging a conspiracy dating from about 1870 that used the trust, the 1899 stock consolidation, pipeline control, railroad rebates, local price-cutting and market division.[^s2]

In *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States* (1911), Chief Justice Edward White's opinion held that the 1899 consolidation was both an unlawful combination in restraint of trade and monopolization under Sections 1 and 2. The Court also announced the **rule of reason**: the Act forbids only unreasonable or undue restraints, judged by intent and effect rather than by a literal reading of the statute.[^s2] As a remedy it ordered the combination dissolved, while extending the compliance period to at least six months.[^s2] The New Jersey company had to divest 33 subsidiaries, eight of which kept "Standard Oil" in their names.[^s1]

## Successors

Many of the spun-off companies became major oil firms in their own right. Standard Oil (New Jersey) became Exxon in 1972; Standard Oil of New York became Mobil, and the two re-merged as ExxonMobil in 1999. Standard Oil of California became Chevron in 1984, Standard Oil (Indiana) became Amoco before merging with BP in 1998, and BP bought Standard Oil (Ohio) in 1987.[^s1] Even before the breakup, Standard's share was eroding as the 1901 Spindletop discovery in Texas created new competitors such as the forerunners of Texaco and Gulf Oil.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Historians and economists still disagree about what Standard Oil's dominance shows. Contemporary defenders such as George Gunton argued in 1888 that large combinations brought efficiencies that benefited consumers, workers and investors, while Tarbell, Theodore Roosevelt and federal investigators portrayed abuse of power.[^s3] In economics, John McGee's 1958 study argued that predatory pricing was irrational and that the trial record did not show Standard used it; this argument shaped later US antitrust doctrine. Dalton and Esposito's 2007 re-examination of the same record reports considerable evidence of predatory price cutting and disputes McGee's conclusion.[^s5] Naomi Lamoreaux argues that the early antitrust regime born of the Standard Oil era targeted large firms' exclusionary conduct toward rivals rather than size as such, and that policy later drifted toward market power and then consumer welfare. She suggests that returning to a conduct focus could address current worries about large technology firms without penalizing growth through innovation.[^s4]

## Connections

Standard Oil sits in the **oil industry** and is the classic case of a firm built on **economies of scale** and **vertical integration**, and through mergers that consolidated a fragmented refining sector.[^s1][^s3] Its 1899 reorganization made it an early **holding company**, and its alleged local price-cutting makes it the canonical test case for **predatory pricing**.[^s2][^s5] Its prosecution under the **Sherman Act** defined the rule of reason at the core of **antitrust law**, and Lamoreaux traces a line from it to current debates in **industrial organization** about big technology platforms.[^s2][^s4]
