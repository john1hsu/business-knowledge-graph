# Toyota: From Automatic Looms to the World's Largest Automaker

Toyota Motor Corporation is a Japanese vehicle manufacturer headquartered in Toyota City, east of Nagoya.[^s1] It is a central case in business history for two reasons: its production practices became a model that Western rivals tried to learn, most visibly General Motors at NUMMI, and it shows how a late entrant overtook the Detroit incumbents.[^s3] In 2008 it passed General Motors to become the world's largest automaker for the first time.[^s1] In calendar 2025 the Toyota group, counting Daihatsu and Hino, sold a record 11.3 million vehicles, up 4.6%, and stayed the top-selling automaker for a sixth consecutive year, ahead of Volkswagen Group's just under 9 million.[^s6]

## Origin

The company grew out of a textile-machinery business. Toyoda Kiichiro set up an automobile department in 1933 inside Toyoda Automatic Loom Works, the firm founded by his father, the loom inventor Toyoda Sakichi.[^s1] The department produced its first passenger car, the Model AA, in 1936 and was spun off as Toyota Motor Company, Ltd. in August 1937, with the family name altered to "Toyota" because it sounded better in Japanese.[^s1][^s2] During the Second World War it built trucks, and it resumed passenger-car output in 1947 with the Model SA.[^s1] Manufacturing and sales were long run by separate companies; Toyota Motor Company and Toyota Motor Sales merged in 1982 to form the present Toyota Motor Corporation.[^s2]

## Growth and internationalization

Toyota's entry into the United States was slow. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A. was founded in 1957, but its first export sedan, the Toyopet, sold poorly in 1958; the rugged Land Cruiser did better, and the redesigned Corona (1965) became its first major US success, followed by the Corolla in 1968.[^s1] Rather than build a US plant alone, Toyota first formed a joint venture with General Motors in 1984, New United Motor Manufacturing, Inc. (NUMMI), and began its own US production in 1986.[^s1] It then moved upmarket with the Lexus brand in 1989, launched the Prius in 1997 as the first mass-produced hybrid car, and listed its shares in New York and London in 1999.[^s1]

## NUMMI as a test of transferability

NUMMI reopened a Fremont, California, plant that GM had closed in 1982 after years of wildcat strikes, roughly 20% daily absenteeism and a backlog of more than 6,000 grievances.[^s3] Under Toyota's management, largely with the former workforce, the plant reached Toyota's own quality level by 1987 and came close to its productivity, with daily attendance averaging 98% in its first year.[^s3] The researchers attribute the turnaround less to copying Japan than to a hybrid culture built on job security (a no-layoff policy), a flat wage structure, the interdependence created by the production system, and a deliberately gradual rollout.[^s3] Inkpen's study in *Strategic Management Journal* uses NUMMI to show why such knowledge is hard to move: it is sticky and causally ambiguous, and GM absorbed it only when it applied deliberate transfer mechanisms and treated the effort as organizational change with trial-and-error learning.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Toyota's reputation for quality was dented when an international safety recall of more than eight million vehicles in 2010 temporarily halted production and sales of several top models.[^s1] Takahiro Fujimoto, a leading scholar of the Toyota system, argued that growth alone was not the cause; the firm had absorbed more lines, more global models and more complex vehicles than its capabilities could handle, and some headquarters managers had drifted from the Toyota Way. He also argued that the defects were mainly design-quality rather than manufacturing-quality problems, so the episode did not discredit the production system itself.[^s5] Toyota was further hit by sales collapsing in the 2008 financial crisis and, from 2014, by US recalls of millions of vehicles fitted with potentially faulty Takata airbags.[^s1]

## Scale today

In the fiscal year ended March 31, 2026, Toyota reported sales revenues of ¥50,684.9 billion, net income attributable to the parent of ¥3,848.0 billion, and consolidated vehicle sales of 9,595 thousand units.[^s2] That consolidated fiscal-year figure is not directly comparable with the 11.3 million calendar-2025 group total, which also counts Daihatsu and Hino.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

Toyota **exemplifies the Toyota Production System**, the operations framework described in its own article, whose pull production, takt time, andon cord and kaizen were the system NUMMI imported; the links to **Taiichi Ohno** and **lean manufacturing** follow the history set out in those articles.[^s3] It was **created by Kiichiro Toyoda** and **operates in the automotive industry**.[^s1] It **competes with General Motors**, its partner at NUMMI and the rival it overtook in 2008, and with **Volkswagen Group**, its nearest rival by volume in 2025.[^s1][^s6] NUMMI makes Toyota a reference case for **organizational learning** and knowledge transfer between alliance partners.[^s4]
