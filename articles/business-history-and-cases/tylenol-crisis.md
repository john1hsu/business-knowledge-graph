# Tylenol Crisis of 1982: Johnson & Johnson's Recall and the Birth of Tamper-Evident Packaging

The **Tylenol crisis** began in late September 1982, when seven people in and around Chicago died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been filled with potassium cyanide; the first victim was a 12-year-old girl in Elk Grove Village.[^s6] The product belonged to McNeil Consumer Products, a Johnson & Johnson (J&J) division, and the capsules came from stores in the Chicago area.[^s2] The killer has never been identified; a New York man who sent J&J a $1 million extortion demand was ruled out as the poisoner and served 13 years for extortion.[^s6] The company's response is one of the most taught cases in crisis management and corporate ethics.[^s2]

## What was at stake

Tylenol was J&J's most important consumer franchise. In 1981 it supplied roughly 7% of the company's worldwide sales and an estimated 15–20% of its profits, and McNeil held about 35% of a US analgesic market Fortune sized at $1 billion.[^s1] After the deaths, Tylenol sales initially fell about 80%, and J&J's third-quarter 1982 earnings dropped to 51 cents a share from 78 cents a year earlier.[^s1] Wharton's later account puts the brand's market share at 7% at the low point.[^s2] Surveys at the time found that most Tylenol users did not blame the maker, but 61% said they were unlikely to buy the capsules again.[^s1]

## The response

Chairman James Burke took personal charge, handing his other duties to the executive committee, and flew to Washington to meet the FBI and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).[^s1] Both agencies initially advised against a hasty national recall, the FDA fearing it could add to public alarm; after a copycat strychnine poisoning in California, the FDA agreed that all Tylenol capsules should come off the market.[^s1] J&J recalled about 31 million bottles with a retail value above $100 million, estimating the recall alone would cost about $100 million before taxes; it also offered to exchange capsules for tablets and kept the press continually informed.[^s1][^s2] Burke later said the company's Credo, which ranks responsibility to customers ahead of employees, communities and stockholders, gave him the backing to persuade shareholders to fund the recall.[^s2]

Instead of retiring the brand, as some insiders urged, J&J returned Tylenol to shelves about two months later in tamper-resistant packaging.[^s2] Its share recovered to about 30% by mid-1983 and roughly 35% by the end of that year.[^s2] One side effect was brand awareness of the parent: fewer than 1% of consumers had known J&J made Tylenol before the poisonings, against more than 47% afterwards.[^s1]

## Regulatory legacy

The FDA issued its first tamper-resistant packaging rule for over-the-counter (OTC) drugs on November 5, 1982 (47 FR 50442), explicitly in response to the Chicago deaths.[^s3] Congress followed with the Federal Anti-Tampering Act of October 13, 1983, which created 18 U.S.C. § 1365 and made tampering with consumer products a federal crime.[^s5] Later poisonings tightened the rules: a 1989 amendment added stricter requirements for two-piece hard gelatin capsules after three deaths in 1986, and a 1998 final rule renamed the standard "tamper-evident," required those capsules to be sealed, and required labels to describe every tamper-evident feature.[^s3] Today 21 CFR 211.132 treats an OTC product sold at retail without a qualifying tamper-evident package and label statement as adulterated or misbranded, with limited exclusions such as dermatological, dentifrice, insulin and lozenge products.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The case is usually told as proof that putting customers first pays off: the brand lost share and the company absorbed a nine-figure charge, yet within about a year it was back near its former position.[^s1][^s2] The record also argues for some caution. The recall decision came after regulators changed their minds, not purely from the Credo; the episode shows that a consumer brand's equity, and not just its products, can be lost overnight; and the later capsule deaths that drove the 1989 and 1998 rules show that packaging reduced the risk without removing it.[^s1][^s3] Because the tampering was done by an outside, still-unknown criminal rather than by the company, the case is also a weaker guide for crises a firm has created through its own failures.[^s2]

## Connections

The case is a standard example of **crisis management** and a classic **product recall**, and it is often cited as an exemplar of **corporate social responsibility** and **stakeholder theory** in practice, because the Credo's ranking of customers above shareholders framed the decision.[^s2] It sits within **business ethics** debates over corporate purpose, including the **Business Roundtable statement on corporate purpose**, and shows how quickly **brand equity** can be destroyed and rebuilt.[^s1] Its direct legacies are **tamper-evident packaging** rules under the FDA and the **Federal Anti-Tampering Act**, both rooted in the **pharmaceutical industry** where Johnson & Johnson and James Burke operated.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
