# Apple iPhone Launch

The **Apple iPhone launch** is the business case of how a personal-computer and music-player company entered the mature, carrier-dominated mobile phone industry. Apple announced the iPhone at Macworld San Francisco on January 9, 2007, describing it as three devices in one: a mobile phone, a widescreen iPod with touch controls and an internet communications device for email, web browsing, search and maps.[^s1] The case is taught less for the hardware than for the commercial choices around it: an exclusive carrier deal, premium pricing that was cut within weeks, unusual revenue accounting, and the 2008 pivot from a closed device to a third-party software platform.[^s3][^s6]

## Origin and launch decisions

The January 2007 announcement priced the phone at $499 for a 4GB model and $599 for an 8GB model, with U.S. availability promised for June 2007, Europe later that year and Asia in 2008.[^s1] Instead of a physical keypad and stylus, the device relied on a 3.5-inch multi-touch screen plus accelerometer, proximity and light sensors, and it bundled features such as visual voicemail, an HTML email client, the Safari browser and Google Maps.[^s1] Apple chose Cingular (soon rebranded AT&T) as its exclusive U.S. carrier and sold the phone through both companies' retail and online stores, an arrangement that let Apple control the customer experience in a market where operators normally dictated handset design.[^s1][^s2]

## Pricing, accounting and scale

About ten weeks after the U.S. launch, on September 5, 2007, Apple cut the 8GB iPhone from $599 to $399 and let the 4GB model sell out, saying it expected to sell its one-millionth unit before the end of that month.[^s2] Apple's fiscal 2008 10-K shows how fast the business scaled: iPhone units grew from about 1.39 million in fiscal 2007 (one quarter of sales) to about 11.6 million in fiscal 2008, and iPhone and related net sales were $1,844 million in fiscal 2008, against total company net sales of $32,479 million.[^s3] Because Apple expected to deliver future software features free of charge, it used subscription accounting, recognizing iPhone handset revenue and cost of sales on a straight-line basis over an estimated 24-month economic life rather than at the point of sale.[^s3]

## From product to platform

The original iPhone shipped without a store for native third-party applications. That changed in July 2008, when Apple began shipping the iPhone 3G, released iPhone 2.0 software to all users and opened the App Store, which let users download third-party programs directly to an iPhone or iPod touch.[^s3] Apple reported more than 10 million downloads in the first weekend from a catalogue of over 800 native apps, of which more than 200 were free and more than 90% cost under $10.[^s4] By the end of fiscal 2008 Apple had multi-year carrier agreements to distribute the iPhone 3G in over 70 countries, generally non-exclusive except in the U.S. and several European markets.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

West and Mace argue, in a peer-reviewed study, that the iPhone's rapid success came less from novelty than from treating the mobile internet as an extension of the existing wired internet, using Apple's systems capabilities; they find that "real internet" browsing was central to the strategy and that iPhone owners used mobile web browsing far more than owners of other phones in the U.S. and Europe.[^s5] This challenges the operator-led "walled garden" vision of mobile data that the industry had pursued for two decades.[^s5] Yoffie and Slind's Harvard case frames the launch as part of Apple's broader shift away from Macintosh dependence, while questioning whether a position straddling PCs and consumer electronics could be sustained.[^s6] Critiques of the launch itself centre on its early missteps: a price cut so soon after launch, and a closed software model that Apple reversed within a year.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The launch **exemplifies** the **platform business model** and a **two-sided market**: the App Store linked users and developers, so each side's growth attracted the other, a **network effect** that hardware alone could not produce.[^s4][^s3] It **contrasts with** **first-mover advantage**, since Apple entered years after established smartphone makers yet captured value through later-mover integration of browsing, media and software.[^s5] Its broader context is **product launch** and **new market entry**, and it sits within Apple's wider **business ecosystem** of iTunes, iPod and Mac that the HBS case describes.[^s6] The **App Store** that followed in July 2008 turned the device into a developer platform and is a node in its own right.[^s4][^s3]
