# Blockbuster: Rise and Fall of the Video Rental Chain

Blockbuster Inc. was the dominant store-based video rental chain in the United States and a large international retailer. Its corporate predecessor was incorporated in Delaware in 1982 and entered movie rental in 1985.[^s1] At the end of 2004 the company operated or franchised 9,094 stores, 5,803 in the U.S. and 3,291 abroad, and reported revenue of $6.1 billion for that year.[^s1] Six years later it was in Chapter 11. The case is widely taught as an example of an incumbent whose profitable physical network made it slow to adopt the delivery models that replaced it.[^s4][^s5]

## Rise and business model

Blockbuster's model rested on neighbourhood stores stocked with new-release movies, rented for a fixed period with "extended viewing fees" (late fees) for overdue returns. Viacom owned a controlling stake until it divested Blockbuster through a split-off exchange offer completed in October 2004.[^s1] MIT Sloan's account of the case notes that recent releases made up roughly three-quarters of Blockbuster's revenue, which tied the chain to the cost of physical stores and new-title inventory.[^s5]

## The response to Netflix

Netflix attacked from a different position: no stores, mail delivery, flat-fee subscriptions and a catalogue of older and hard-to-find titles. MIT Sloan professor Birger Wernerfelt uses the pair to argue that an underdog wins when its model does not provoke imitation and the incumbent reacts slowly and invests less.[^s5] Blockbuster did respond. It launched a U.S. by-mail service in August 2004 and had more than 750,000 online subscribers by March 2005.[^s1] From January 1, 2005 it dropped late fees at over 4,500 company-operated U.S. stores. It estimated the fees would otherwise have brought in $400–450 million of revenue and $250–300 million of operating income in 2005, and it budgeted about $50 million to launch the change.[^s1] Its later Blockbuster Total Access programme let online subscribers return mailed DVDs to stores for free in-store rentals, a hybrid of its two channels.[^s2]

A popular story holds that Blockbuster turned down a chance to buy Netflix for $50 million around 2002. Netflix co-founder Reed Hastings tells it that way. Former CEO John Antioco disputed it in 2022, saying no serious talks happened then. He said serious merger discussions came only in early 2007, after Total Access had taken share from Netflix.[^s6]

## Leadership change and collapse

John Antioco led Blockbuster from 1997 to 2007. In early 2005 activist investor Carl Icahn told him he had bought nearly 10 million shares, and after losing a proxy fight to Icahn, Antioco faced boardroom opposition to his strategy.[^s7] By his account later management had little interest in online rental and wound Total Access down.[^s6] James W. Keyes, formerly CEO of 7-Eleven, became chairman and CEO in July 2007. He positioned the company around convenient access through stores, mail and the internet, and bought Movielink for digital downloads.[^s2] Blockbuster's own 10-K for fiscal 2007 already named Netflix, Redbox kiosks and mass merchants such as Wal-Mart as competitors.[^s2]

The squeeze came from both sides. A 2010 Kellogg case reports that by the end of 2009 Blockbuster's debt reached $856 million and its revenue had fallen 23% to $4.1 billion. Its share of a $6.5 billion rental market was 27%, and its market value had dropped 47% to $62 million, while Netflix's rose 55% to $3.9 billion.[^s4] Keyes judged that the company had to shift from in-store rental toward digital and mail delivery while renegotiating terms with studios.[^s4] The NYSE suspended its shares in July 2010. On September 23, 2010 Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of New York. Fiscal 2010 revenue fell about 20% to $3.24 billion, and stores shrank to 5,335.[^s3] DISH Network won the April 2011 auction and closed the purchase of the assets for about $320 million on April 26, 2011.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The standard reading treats Blockbuster as a textbook incumbent failure. Wernerfelt notes that leaders resist scrapping a core model even when a new one is better, and suggests testing alternatives in small parts of the business.[^s5] The record complicates a pure "missed it" story, however. Blockbuster launched online rental within a few years, abandoned a fee stream worth hundreds of millions, and by its former CEO's account was gaining on Netflix before strategy shifted.[^s1][^s6] Both the Kellogg case and the company's own filings point to two simultaneous attackers: Redbox on price and convenience, and Netflix on mail and streaming. They also highlight debt and studio terms as constraints on the pivot.[^s3][^s4] The famous $50 million anecdote itself is contested.[^s6]

## Connections

Blockbuster is a node under **corporate decline** and **technological disruption**, and it **exemplifies disruptive innovation** through an entrant whose lower-cost model the leader was slow to copy.[^s5] It is the mirror case of **Netflix's shift from DVD rental to streaming**, against which it **competes**, along with **Redbox** kiosks.[^s2][^s4] Netflix's deep back catalogue links it to the **long tail**, and its flat fee to the **subscription business model**.[^s5] Its late-fee economics and store base illustrate the **innovator's dilemma**. The Icahn conflict ties it to **shareholder activism**.[^s7] It was **acquired by DISH Network** out of bankruptcy.[^s3]
