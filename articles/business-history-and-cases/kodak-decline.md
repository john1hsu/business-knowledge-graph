# Kodak Decline

The decline of Eastman Kodak is the slow collapse of the company that created amateur photography. George Eastman's 1888 Kodak camera opened a mass market for snapshots that the firm dominated for more than a century, financed above all by the profits on film.[^s1] In the 1970s Kodak held about 85 percent of U.S. camera sales and 90 percent of film sales.[^s6] On January 19, 2012, Kodak and its U.S. subsidiaries filed for Chapter 11 protection in the Southern District of New York.[^s3] The case is a staple of strategy teaching because Kodak itself invented the technology that undermined it.

## Origin: inventing the disruptor

Kodak hired electronics engineer Steven Sasson in 1974 and assigned him to build a camera around a charge-coupled-device sensor. In December 1975 he took the first image with a prototype that recorded 100 × 100 pixel black-and-white pictures to cassette tape, weighed 3.6 kilograms and needed about 23 seconds to store each shot.[^s5] Kodak patented the device in 1978 but never produced it; executives reportedly saw no market, and Sasson was barred from showing it outside the company.[^s5] This episode anchors the popular story that Kodak "buried" digital photography to protect film.

## What actually happened

Business-history research tells a more complicated story. Vinokurova and Kapoor document that from the 1970s, as film growth slowed, Kodak repeatedly attempted strategic renewal: it diversified into plain-paper copiers and pharmaceuticals and invested heavily in digital imaging products.[^s1] Kodak announced Photo CD in 1990 and launched it in 1992; it co-developed the Advanced Photo System with Fuji, Minolta, Canon and Nikon from 1991, but APS sales in 1997 came in at under half the 8 million units expected.[^s2] Kodak was the U.S. market-share leader in digital cameras in 2004 and 2005, and it licensed its digital-camera patents to rivals.[^s2] It launched a consumer inkjet printer in 2007, betting that people would keep printing photos even from digital cameras.[^s2][^s6] The cash strain of funding inkjet while closing film plants preceded the 2012 bankruptcy.[^s2]

The company emerged from Chapter 11 on September 3, 2013, much smaller. It had sold digital-imaging patents in February 2013, treated personalized and document imaging, digital capture and Kodak Gallery as discontinued operations, and repositioned itself as a technology company focused on imaging for business. At the end of 2013 it had about 8,800 employees worldwide, compared with more than 145,000 in the 1980s.[^s2][^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The conventional reading treats Kodak as a textbook case of incumbent myopia and of the **innovator's dilemma**: a firm too wedded to high-margin film to embrace a cheaper technology. Recent scholarship critiques that view. Vinokurova and Kapoor argue that Kodak saw digital coming and invested in it, yet failed for three contextual reasons: film's exceptional profitability set aspirations few new ventures could meet; it was unclear when consumers would adopt digital, and with which business models; and film's continued strength created an illusion of resilience that made unprofitable digital bets hard to sustain.[^s1][^s2] Digital camera sales only passed one million units worldwide in 1999 and matched film-camera volume in 2002, so timing really was uncertain.[^s2]

The contrast with Fujifilm is the other main lesson. Ho and Chen (2018) argue that disruption need not make all of an incumbent's competences obsolete: a firm that maps its technological capabilities can carry them into new fields, but it must redefine its core business in time and restructure to execute consistently.[^s4] Columbia's case frames Fuji's diversification and aggressive acquisitions as the path Kodak did not manage, and asks students to weigh how management, board and culture shaped each outcome.[^s6]

## In practice

Managers use the case less as a warning to "invent the future" than as a lesson that invention is not enough. Kodak had the patents, the research spending and outside CEOs, but its consumer business model depended on selling prints and consumables, an assumption that failed when photos moved to screens.[^s1][^s6] The practical takeaway is to run many cheap experiments, judge new ventures against realistic rather than film-level profit benchmarks, and decide early which existing competences can travel into new markets.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Kodak is a standard illustration of **disruptive innovation** and the **innovator's dilemma**, though the research above disputes the simple version.[^s1] Its struggle to run film and digital side by side is a failure of **organizational ambidexterity** and **dynamic capabilities**, and its print-and-film model resembles the **razor-and-blades model**.[^s2][^s6] It is a case of **strategic renewal** and **corporate decline**, and it contrasts with **Fujifilm's transformation** and the **IBM turnaround**, where incumbents did reinvent themselves.[^s4][^s6]
