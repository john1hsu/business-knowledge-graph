# Google: From Stanford Search Project to Alphabet Inc.

Google is the internet search and advertising company that Larry Page and Sergey Brin founded in 1998 after meeting as graduate students at Stanford University; since 2015 it has been the largest subsidiary of the holding company Alphabet Inc.[^s1][^s4] Business schools study it for three reasons: how a ranking technology became one of the most profitable advertising businesses ever built, how its founders tried to keep a large firm entrepreneurial through an unusual corporate structure, and how its dominance in search became the subject of a landmark U.S. monopolization case.[^s1][^s6]

## Origins: from BackRub to a company

Page and Brin's first search engine, called BackRub, ranked web pages partly by the links pointing to them, giving more weight to links from pages that were themselves heavily linked.[^s1] Their Stanford paper describing the prototype named this measure PageRank, modelled it as a "random surfer" following links, and computed it iteratively across the link graph.[^s2] In an appendix the authors warned that search engines funded by advertising would tend to favour advertisers over users, an irony given what the company became.[^s2] In mid-1998 they raised about $1 million from angel investors, family and friends, moved to Menlo Park, and in 1999 added a $25 million venture round while handling about 500,000 queries a day.[^s1]

## How the business model works

Google grew by becoming Yahoo!'s search provider in 2000; by 2004, when Yahoo! dropped it, users were searching Google some 200 million times a day.[^s1] The company monetised that traffic by selling ads alongside results and, after buying AdSense maker Applied Semantics in 2003, by placing ads on third-party sites; later purchases of DoubleClick (2007, for $3.1 billion) and AdMob (2009, for $750 million) extended it into display and mobile advertising.[^s1] Search ads are priced by auction: economists Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz showed that the "generalized second-price" auction used by search engines resembles, but does not behave like, the textbook Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanism, because bidding one's true value is not an equilibrium strategy.[^s3] Alphabet describes its Google Services revenue as coming mainly from advertising, alongside subscriptions such as YouTube and Google One, platforms such as Google Play, and devices such as Pixel.[^s5] In fiscal 2025 Alphabet reported revenues of $402.8 billion, of which Google advertising contributed $294.7 billion and Google Search & other alone $224.5 billion; online advertising made up more than 70% of total revenues.[^s5]

## Governance and the Alphabet reorganization

Google went public in 2004 through an auction-style IPO that raised $1.66 billion, and in 2001 the founders had brought in Eric Schmidt as chairman and CEO; Page took back the CEO role in 2011.[^s1] In August 2015 Page announced Alphabet, a new parent in which Google would become a wholly owned, "slimmed down" subsidiary under CEO Sundar Pichai, while ventures far from the core, such as the longevity company Calico and the X lab, would be run as separate businesses with their own leaders.[^s4] He framed the change as giving more management scale and accountability, and recalled the founders' original pledge: "Google is not a conventional company. We do not intend to become one."[^s4] Alphabet now reports Google Services and Google Cloud as segments and groups non-Google businesses such as Waymo as Other Bets; Google Cloud's revenue reached $58.7 billion in 2025, with operating income of $13.9 billion.[^s5]

## Antitrust: the search monopoly case

In August 2024 the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia found that Google had unlawfully maintained a monopoly in violation of Section 2 of the Sherman Act.[^s6] Remedies announced on September 2, 2025 bar Google from exclusive contracts for distributing Search, Chrome, Google Assistant or the Gemini app, and require it to make certain search index and user-interaction data available to some competitors and to offer them search and search text ad syndication.[^s6] The Justice Department said it would review the opinion and consider its options, including seeking additional relief.[^s6]

## Lessons and critiques

The case illustrates how a free product funded by advertisers can become a two-sided market: in 2025 advertising still produced more than 70% of Alphabet's revenue, and the court's remedies target the revenue-share and placement contracts through which Google secured default distribution.[^s5][^s6] It also shows the tension Brin and Page themselves flagged in 1998 between advertiser revenue and neutral results.[^s2] Alphabet was presented as a way to run speculative bets independently of the core, yet as of 2025 Other Bets earned revenue mainly from Waymo and internet services while Google's segments carried the group.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Google was **created by Larry Page and Sergey Brin**, whose PageRank paper supplied its founding technology.[^s1][^s2] Its advertiser-funded free search **exemplifies a two-sided market** and the **aggregation theory** account of internet platforms, and it sits in the **digital advertising industry**, the broader node here, given that advertising supplies most of its revenue.[^s5] The 2015 reorganization makes Alphabet an example of a **holding company** that separates a core business from unrelated ventures.[^s4] The 2024–2025 monopolization ruling places it **under antitrust law**, as Apple's App Store cases place **Apple Inc.**, its fellow platform giant.[^s6] Internal management practices drawn from Google are covered in the **OKRs** and **Google Project Aristotle** articles.[^s1]
