# IBM Turnaround

The IBM turnaround is the recovery of International Business Machines from near-collapse in the early 1990s to renewed profitability and market leadership by the early 2000s. It is closely identified with Louis V. Gerstner Jr., who became chairman and CEO in April 1993 and handed the CEO role to Samuel Palmisano on March 1, 2002.[^s2] Gerstner, a former McKinsey partner and American Express executive who arrived from RJR Nabisco, was the first leader recruited from outside IBM since its founding.[^s5] Business schools teach the case as a study of large-firm renewal: cost restructuring, a contrarian decision to keep the company whole, culture change, and a shift from products to integrated services.

## The crisis

Through the mid-1980s IBM held roughly 40% of computer-industry sales and 70% of its profits, but growth fell below 6% by 1990 as computing moved toward cheaper distributed machines.[^s3] Harreld, O'Reilly and Tushman report that between 1986 and 1993 IBM took $28 billion in charges and cut 125,000 jobs, ending more than 70 years without layoffs.[^s3] According to Knowledge at Wharton, over 90% of profit still came from mainframes, whose sales were falling fast, and shares that sold for $43 in 1987 could be bought for about $12.[^s4] IBM's own filings show a net loss of $4.97 billion in 1992 and $8.10 billion in 1993, on 1993 revenue of $62.7 billion, with restructuring charges of $8.9 billion in 1993.[^s1]

## What Gerstner changed

**Cost and size.** IBM described 1993 as an effort to "right-size" the company by cutting workforce, office space and capacity. Regular full-time employees fell from 301,542 at the end of 1992 to 256,207 at the end of 1993, and workforce reserves set up in 1992–1993 anticipated about 110,000 reductions in total.[^s1] Assets were also sold: Loral bought IBM's Federal Systems Company for $1.503 billion in cash in March 1994.[^s1]

**Keeping IBM whole.** Before Gerstner arrived, IBM was moving toward increasingly autonomous business units that could be spun off. In 1993 management reversed course and committed to remaining an integrated information-technology provider.[^s1] Gerstner reasoned that customers needed someone to assemble systems from many vendors, and that IBM's breadth suited that role; he later called this his most important decision.[^s4][^s5]

**Services, software and openness.** IBM began selling "solutions" that could include rivals' hardware and software, committed to open standards, and moved its emphasis from boxes toward services.[^s4] It also re-engineered mainframes onto lower-cost chip technology rather than abandoning them.[^s5] By 2001, services ($35 billion) and software ($13 billion) made up 58% of revenue.[^s3]

**Culture.** Gerstner saw IBM's inward-looking, fief-based culture as the core problem: units competed with each other and hoarded information. He tied executive pay to total-company results, changed promotion rules, and expected senior leaders to do analysis themselves rather than delegate it.[^s4] Harreld and colleagues quote his diagnosis that IBM had plenty of good strategies but was "frozen in place," lacking the ability to reallocate resources.[^s3]

## Results

IBM's 2002 proxy statement credited Gerstner's tenure (to year-end 2001) with a 938% increase in total stockholder return, and reported 2001 revenue of $85.9 billion.[^s2] Harreld, O'Reilly and Tushman put market capitalization at $30 billion in 1993 and $173 billion in 2001.[^s3] From 1999 IBM added a formal Business Leadership Model and an "Emerging Business Opportunities" process that gave new ventures separate leaders, funding and monthly senior review; of 18 opportunities identified from 1999 to 2005, Life Sciences grew to over $2 billion in revenue, while others were folded into existing units or failed.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The case rests heavily on insider accounts, notably Gerstner's 2002 memoir *Who Says Elephants Can't Dance?*. Critics cited by Knowledge at Wharton argue the turnaround looks less impressive than he suggests: a contested pension-plan change flattered results and large share buybacks lifted the stock, and neither features in the book, which also passes lightly over failures such as an education-software venture.[^s4] Shareholder-return figures also depend on the depressed 1993 starting point.[^s2][^s4] Academic accounts present the later renewal mechanisms as imperfect and say the hard part is aligning strategy, structure, culture and process, not any single tool.[^s3]

## Connections

The case is a standard example of a **corporate turnaround** and of large-scale **change management**, with **organizational culture** at its center.[^s4] Its origin is a familiar **disruptive innovation** pattern, in which cheaper distributed computing eroded a profitable mainframe franchise.[^s3] Harreld, O'Reilly and Tushman use IBM's post-1999 strategy machinery to illustrate **dynamic capabilities** (sensing and seizing opportunities by reconfiguring assets) and **organizational ambidexterity**, balancing exploitation of the core with exploration of new businesses.[^s3] It is often contrasted with **Kodak's decline**, a firm that did not reconfigure in time. The turnaround is inseparable from **Louis V. Gerstner Jr.**[^s5]
