# Apple Inc.: From Personal Computers to a Platform Ecosystem

**Apple Inc.**, known as Apple Computer until a 2007 name change that signalled its move into consumer electronics, is a California technology company that Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak and Ronald Wayne founded on 1 April 1976, working out of the Jobs family garage.[^s1][^s3] It is one of the most taught cases in business schools because its history contains both halves of the strategy story: a pioneer that lost its way in personal computers, and a turnaround built on consumer devices tied together into a platform.[^s3][^s4]

## History

Apple was incorporated in January 1977 and released the Apple II that April, one of the first microcomputers sold widely to ordinary buyers.[^s1] Its December 1980 initial public offering was the largest since Ford's in 1956.[^s1] The Macintosh, launched in January 1984 with a famous Super Bowl advertisement, sold below expectations, and in September 1985 chief executive John Sculley pushed Jobs out; Jobs went on to found NeXT.[^s1] Through the 1990s Apple suffered compatibility problems, litigation, failed products such as the Newton handheld and a revolving door of CEOs, and its sales and share fell.[^s1]

In 1996 Apple bought NeXT, whose software later became the basis of macOS, and in 1997 Jobs returned as interim chief executive.[^s1] David Yoffie's Harvard teaching case records how he cut the product line, ended the Newton and won Microsoft's commitment to keep developing Office for the Mac.[^s3] The 1998 iMac revived the brand, followed by iTunes and the iPod in 2001, a music download store in 2003, the iPhone in 2007 and the iPad in 2010.[^s1] Tim Cook, one of Jobs's first hires after his return, ran operations so that Jobs could concentrate on strategy and design, and later succeeded him as chief executive.[^s4][^s1]

## Business model

Apple's annual report describes a company that designs nearly the whole solution itself: hardware, operating systems, applications and services, supported by a third-party ecosystem of software and accessories.[^s2] In fiscal 2025 (the year ended 27 September 2025) net sales were $416.2 billion, up from $391.0 billion in fiscal 2024, and net income was $112.0 billion.[^s2] The iPhone alone produced $209.6 billion, about half of sales, while Services such as the App Store, advertising, AppleCare, cloud and payments produced $109.2 billion.[^s2] Services carry far richer margins: 75.4 percent gross margin in fiscal 2025 against 36.8 percent for products.[^s2] Apple spent $34.6 billion on research and development that year and returned about $89 billion through buybacks and $15.4 billion in dividends; it had roughly 166,000 full-time-equivalent employees.[^s2] It reports by geography, and Greater China alone accounted for $64.4 billion of fiscal 2025 sales.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Scholars draw two lessons from Apple. Yoffie argued in 2007 that Apple's mind share had always exceeded its market share: the celebrated iMac sold about 6 million units over three years while roughly 300 million PCs were sold, so design leadership alone did not win the computer market.[^s3] Yoffie and Michael Cusumano's later study of Jobs, Gates and Grove adds that Jobs was slow to think in platforms, keeping the Mac as a proprietary hub; only after he let iTunes run on Windows PCs did Apple's devices become a mass-market platform rather than a niche.[^s4]

The critique of the integrated model is that control becomes lock-in. In March 2024 the European Commission fined Apple more than €1.8 billion for App Store rules that, for almost ten years, barred music-streaming apps from telling iPhone users about cheaper subscriptions outside the app.[^s5] Later that month the U.S. Justice Department and 16 state and district attorneys general sued Apple under Section 2 of the Sherman Act, alleging that restrictions on super apps, cloud gaming, cross-platform messaging, third-party smartwatches and digital wallets raise the cost of leaving the iPhone.[^s6] The U.S. claims are allegations in a complaint, not findings.[^s6]

## Connections

Apple was **created by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak** (with Ronald Wayne) and **operates in the computer industry**, where its trajectory contrasts with **Hewlett-Packard** and with **Intel**, the other canonical renewal case.[^s1] Its iPhone-and-App-Store system **exemplifies the platform business model** and a **business ecosystem** of complementors, a shift the **Apple iPhone launch** article covers in detail.[^s4][^s2] Its 1997–2001 recovery **exemplifies strategic renewal**.[^s3] The regulators' complaints are, in substance, about **switching costs**, and they place Apple's App Store **under antitrust law** in both the EU and the U.S.[^s5][^s6]
