# Ford Model T

The Model T was the automobile Ford Motor Company produced from 1908 to 1927, nicknamed the "Tin Lizzie" or "flivver." It launched at $850, roughly 18 months of an average worker's wages, and by 1925 sold for under $300, about four months' wages; more than 15 million were built, and at times it accounted for as much as 40 percent of U.S. car sales.[^s1] It is the canonical business case for mass production: a single standardized product whose cost fell steadily as volume rose, and whose eventual eclipse shows how a cost-leadership strategy can be outflanked by a rival competing on variety and novelty.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin and product design

Henry Ford introduced the car on October 1, 1908, at $850. Its chassis used lightweight, high-strength vanadium alloy steel, which made a cheap car durable enough for rough rural roads.[^s2] Ford deliberately kept the design nearly unchanged for almost two decades, betting that a single model built in enormous volume would win on price.[^s5]

## How the production system worked

In 1913 Ford engineers at the Highland Park, Michigan, plant combined interchangeable parts and finely divided labor with a new principle: moving the work to the worker.[^s3] They tried the idea first on flywheel magnetos around April 1913. One worker had needed about 20 minutes to finish a flywheel; on the line, output first reached one every 13 minutes and, after further adjustment, one every five minutes.[^s3] The method spread to engines and transmissions and, in August 1913, to the whole chassis. By early 1914, three continuous lines turned out a finished Model T every 93 minutes, versus about 12½ hours under the old stationary method.[^s3] The line was never treated as finished: constant small changes, such as altering platform heights or relocating a drill press, compounded into large gains.[^s3]

The savings were passed on as price cuts, which expanded demand and justified still more investment in efficiency. By the end of 1913 the price had dropped to $550, and it eventually fell as low as $260; peak annual output reached 1.8 million cars in 1923.[^s2][^s3]

## The five-dollar day

Repetitive line work created a labor problem. In 1913 Ford's annual turnover reached 370 percent: it hired 50,448 men to keep an average workforce of about 13,600.[^s4] On January 5, 1914, the company nearly doubled pay to $5 a day, structured as profit sharing with eligibility screened by a "Sociological Department" that inspected workers' homes and habits.[^s2][^s4] Raff and Summers (1987) found that turnover fell to 54 percent in 1914, absenteeism dropped from 10 percent to 2.5 percent between October 1913 and October 1914, long queues of applicants formed, and productivity rose substantially, so the program likely paid for itself.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Economists treat the five-dollar day as strong evidence for efficiency-wage theory: paying above market can raise profits by cutting turnover and raising effort.[^s4] The firm's net income rose from $27.1 million in 1913 to $40.3 million in 1915, even as wages doubled.[^s4] The main critique of the Model T strategy is its rigidity. In the 1920s General Motors, under Alfred P. Sloan Jr., fearing a saturating market, introduced annual model changes, trade-ins and installment buying to give owners reasons to replace working cars.[^s5][^s6] GM's sales soared while Ford's fell.[^s6] Although the Model T held about 60 percent of the new-car market in 1921, Chevrolet was outselling it by early 1927.[^s5] Production ended on May 26, 1927, with the 15 millionth car; Ford then shut down to retool for the Model A, unveiled in December 1927.[^s2][^s5]

## In practice

The case is taught for three lessons: process innovation can turn a luxury good into a mass-market product; scale and learning economies create a powerful but narrow advantage; and a firm optimized for one product can be slow to respond when customers start valuing variety and status. Ford's openness about Highland Park, documented in Arnold and Faurote's *Ford Methods and the Ford Shops* (1915), spread its methods across industry, reflecting confidence that rivals could not catch up quickly.[^s3]

## Connections

The Model T is the defining example of **mass production** and illustrates **economies of scale** as volume drove unit costs and prices down.[^s1][^s3] Its line built on the division of labor associated with **scientific management**, and it is the usual point of comparison for later, more flexible systems such as the **Toyota Production System**. The five-dollar day exemplifies **efficiency wages**.[^s4] GM's **annual model change** and multi-tier product line, an early form of **segmentation, targeting and positioning**, contrast with Ford's one-model approach, and the car was followed by the **Ford Model A**.[^s5][^s6] It was created under **Henry Ford**.[^s2]
