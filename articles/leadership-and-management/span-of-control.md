# Span of Control

**Span of control** (also span of management or span of supervision) is the number of employees a manager directly supervises.[^s1] It is one of the basic parameters of organizational design: for a given headcount, narrow spans require more management levels and produce a tall hierarchy, while wide spans need fewer levels and produce a flat one.[^s1] Choosing spans therefore decides how many managers each level of the management pyramid needs.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea that a superior can only oversee a limited number of people was formalized in classical administrative theory. In a 1933 paper, V. A. Graicunas argued that a manager handles not only one-to-one relationships with each subordinate but also relationships with groups of subordinates and among the subordinates themselves, so the coordination load grows geometrically with each extra report: four subordinates imply 44 potential relationships and eight imply 1,080.[^s2] The paper reached a wide audience through *Papers on the Science of Administration* (1937), co-edited by Luther Gulick and Lyndall Urwick.[^s2] Urwick turned the argument into a rule that a superior can directly supervise about five, and at most six, subordinates whose work interlocks, and in 1956 he defended that limit against critics who argued for wider spans.[^s2] Gulick's own 1937 essay held that the right span depends on how diverse the work is, how stable it is over time, and how dispersed it is in space.[^s3]

## How it works

The right span is contingent rather than fixed. Textbook treatments point to organization size, industry, the manager's experience and, above all, the similarity of the work: a warehouse supervisor whose staff do routine, similar tasks can oversee many people, while a pharmaceutical research leader managing complex, dissimilar work may oversee only a few chemists.[^s1] Narrow spans give close control, familiarity with each employee and quick feedback, but add layers and cost, slow decisions, isolate top managers and limit autonomy.[^s1] Wide spans cut overhead, speed decisions and give employees more autonomy, at the price of thinner supervision and possible coordination problems.[^s1] Even early analysts noted that training, planning, delegation and staff support can stretch a manager's effective span.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Herbert Simon's 1946 critique of the "principles of administration" left the numerical span rules without empirical support, and Meier and Bohte note that the field then produced mostly descriptive work for decades.[^s3] Testing Gulick's variables on 678 Texas school districts (1994–97), they found that diversity and stability shape spans differently at different hierarchical levels, and that wider spans at both the teacher-to-administrator and middle-management levels were associated with higher student test scores, contradicting the claim that average spans perform best.[^s3] They caution that the result applies most directly to professionalized, flat public organizations.[^s3]

In large firms, spans at the top widened sharply. Rajan and Wulf, studying more than 300 large U.S. companies, found that the CEO's median number of direct reports rose from 4 in 1986 to 6 in 1998, while the average number of levels between the CEO and division heads fell by about a quarter in their balanced panel.[^s4] Wulf's later work questioned the assumed benefit: flattening moved some decision rights up to top functional managers and made CEOs more involved with their direct reports, so it looked more like centralization than empowerment.[^s5] Wider spans, in other words, do not by themselves push decisions down.

## In practice

Spans continue to widen across the workforce. Gallup reports that the mean number of direct reports among the U.S. managers it surveys rose from 8.2 in 2013 to 12.1 in 2025, while the median stayed at roughly five to six, so the increase is driven by a minority of very large teams (13% of managers had 25 or more reports in 2025).[^s6] Gallup does not name an ideal number; it finds that large teams can work when managers have strong talent, spend no more than about 40% of their time on individual-contributor work, and give regular meaningful feedback.[^s6] Delayering programs that widen spans to save cost should therefore budget for manager capability, not only headcount.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

Span of control is a core dimension of **organizational structure** and a classic principle of **management theory**.[^s1][^s3] Its formal history is **influenced by V. A. Graicunas, Lyndall Urwick and Luther Gulick**, and it is **studied in public administration**, where Meier and Bohte tested Gulick's determinants.[^s2][^s3] It interacts with **decision rights** and **centralization and decentralization**, since flattening can pull decisions upward rather than push them down.[^s5] Wide spans depend on effective **delegation**, which makes subordinates accountable to their manager.[^s1] The **situational leadership** article links here because Hersey and Blanchard tied span to subordinates' maturity.
