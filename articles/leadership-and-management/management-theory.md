# Management Theory

**Management theory** is the accumulated set of explanations and prescriptions for how managers should coordinate people, work and resources toward an organization's goals. People have managed large undertakings for as long as there has been commerce, but management became a distinct field of study only when the Industrial Revolution produced large firms that needed specialization and coordination at scale.[^s7] The field is usually taught as a sequence of overlapping schools, each reacting to the blind spots of the one before: classical theory, the human relations movement, systems theory, contingency theory and, most recently, evidence-based management.[^s2][^s3]

## Classical schools (c. 1880s–1920s)

The classical schools searched for a single best way to run work and organizations. Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management studied individual tasks to standardize methods on the shop floor. Henri Fayol, a French mining executive, looked instead at the top of the firm: he named five managerial functions (planning, organizing, staffing, directing and controlling) and fourteen principles such as unity of command, the scalar chain and esprit de corps.[^s1] The German sociologist Max Weber contributed the ideal type of bureaucracy, built on legal authority: specialized roles, merit-based hiring and promotion, a clear hierarchy, written rules and authority vested in the office rather than the person.[^s1]

Critics noted early that these designs have costs. Robert K. Merton argued that bureaucratic rules can become ends in themselves, and rules can never anticipate every case; Fayol's principles also play out differently depending on a firm's technology and national culture.[^s1]

## Human relations movement (1920s–1950s)

The human relations school challenged the classical assumption that workers respond only to rational incentives. Its origin story is the Hawthorne studies at Western Electric's plant in Cicero, Illinois (1924–1932), interpreted by the Harvard psychologist Elton Mayo, who concluded that output depended on group attitudes and supervisors' attention to social needs.[^s2] Mary Parker Follett argued for power grounded in expertise and for resolving conflict by integration, and Chester Barnard, president of New Jersey Bell, framed the executive's job as securing cooperation through communication and incentives.[^s2] The common thread is that perceptions, social ties and meaning shape performance, so managers must align workers' and the organization's interests.[^s2]

## Systems and contingency theory (1950s–1970s)

Systems theory, drawing on Ludwig von Bertalanffy, Kenneth Boulding, Daniel Katz and Robert Kahn, treated the firm as an open system that takes in inputs, adds value and exchanges outputs and information with its environment, so outside forces such as labor law, markets and competitors shape managerial results.[^s3] A 1972 *Academy of Management Journal* article on general systems theory argued that the open-systems model had spurred new organization theory but left dilemmas unresolved, and presented contingency views as a move toward more concrete, applicable theory.[^s5] Contingency theory, associated with the British researcher Joan Woodward, rejected universal rules altogether: the right structure or practice depends on the situation, for example on a firm's production technology.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The field's own scholars have been its sharpest critics. In 1961 Harold Koontz described a "management theory jungle" of six competing schools — management process, empirical, human behavior, social system, decision theory and mathematical — published in the *Academy of Management Journal*.[^s4] He blamed the confusion on loose terminology, disagreement about what management is, premature dismissal of earlier principles and theorists' unwillingness to understand one another, and called for clearer definitions and testing of fundamental principles.[^s6] Empirical foundations have also been questioned: the OpenStax text calls the Hawthorne experiments scientifically weak even as it credits them with redirecting research toward real working lives.[^s2] Since the 1970s the schools have been blended rather than replaced, and Jeffrey Pfeffer's call for evidence-based management asks managers to adopt only practices that research has tested.[^s3]

## In practice

The schools remain visible in everyday management. Organization charts, job descriptions and standard operating procedures descend from Fayol and Weber; attention to group attitudes and supervision reflects the human relations movement; and the habit of asking how the environment, technology or strategy changes the right answer is the contingency view at work.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The Hawthorne plant remains the field's best-known case: a productivity study about lighting that ended up reorienting management thinking toward the social side of work.[^s2]

## Connections

Management theory is a part of the broader discipline of `kt:management` and overlaps heavily with `kt:organization-theory`, which studies structures that management theory tries to design.[^s5] It has `kt:scientific-management`, the `kt:human-relations-movement` and `kt:contingency-theory` as major schools, and it was shaped by `kt:henri-fayol`, whose functions and principles anchor the administrative school.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The `kt:hawthorne-studies` are related as the empirical episode that launched the human relations school.[^s2] Related leadership models such as the contingency theory of leadership apply the same "it depends" logic to individual leaders.[^s3]
