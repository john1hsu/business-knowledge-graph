# Performance Management

**Performance management** is the ongoing process through which an organization links the work of individuals and teams to its goals: managers and employees agree on expectations, track progress, build capability, evaluate results and recognize contributions. The U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM) describes it as a systematic process that involves employees in improving organizational effectiveness and accomplishing the mission.[^s1] The term is broader than **performance appraisal**, the periodic rating of an individual: appraisal is one step in a performance-management cycle, whereas performance management covers everything managers do to influence performance before and after the rating.[^s3]

## How it works

OPM's widely used model breaks the cycle into five linked activities.[^s1]

1. **Planning**: setting expectations and goals so that individual effort supports organizational objectives.[^s1]
2. **Monitoring**: measuring performance consistently and giving ongoing feedback, not waiting for year-end.[^s1]
3. **Developing**: raising the capacity to perform through training, stretch assignments or better work processes.[^s1]
4. **Rating**: assessing performance against the agreed elements and standards and recording a summary rating for the period.[^s1]
5. **Rewarding**: recognizing individuals and groups through formal awards, pay and informal recognition.[^s1]

In corporate practice the same logic appears as cascading goals (often written as SMART goals), pay-for-performance links, real-time handling of poor performers and documentation of performance conversations; OpenStax argues the process should be owned jointly by line managers and HR rather than treated as an HR-only exercise.[^s2] Goal-setting frameworks such as management by objectives, OKRs and the balanced scorecard supply the planning and measurement layer of the cycle.[^s2]

## History

Formal rating of workers is roughly a century old. The U.S. military created a merit-rating system during World War I to identify poor performers, and appraisal spread through industry afterwards: about 60% of U.S. companies used appraisals after World War II and nearly 90% by the 1960s.[^s2] In the federal civil service, a graphic rating scale introduced in 1924 was followed by several government-wide systems, and the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 put job-related appraisal at the center of personnel management.[^s1] At General Electric, Jack Welch, CEO from 1981, championed forced ranking of employees into A, B and C players, a practice GE later moved away from after he retired.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

A century of research, reviewed by DeNisi and Murphy in 2017, concentrated on rating scales, rater training, rating sources, cognitive processes and rater bias; the authors conclude the field learned much about rating accuracy but far less about how to actually improve performance, and they call for research on performance management as a whole.[^s3] Feedback, the mechanism most systems rely on, is not reliably beneficial: Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes found a positive average effect (d = .41), yet over one-third of feedback interventions reduced performance.[^s4]

Critics of traditional systems point to their cost and unreliability. Deloitte estimated (as of 2015) that it was spending close to 2 million hours a year on performance management, and research on "idiosyncratic rater effects" showed that ratings reveal more about the rater than the person rated.[^s5] Forced ranking can undermine collaboration, and annual cycles let problems go unaddressed for months.[^s2] On the other side, Murphy and DeNisi argue (as of 2023) that reports of firms abandoning appraisal are overstated, that appraisal documents decisions and supports rewards, and that firms should fix appraisal by clarifying goals, focusing on what employees control and making systems fair, rather than dropping it.[^s6] A CEB survey of more than 9,000 managers and employees likewise found that having no evaluations was perceived as worse than having them.[^s2]

## In practice: Deloitte

Deloitte's redesign, published in *Harvard Business Review* in April 2015, is a widely cited example. The firm dropped cascading objectives, once-a-year reviews and 360-degree feedback tools in favor of a faster, more real-time system built on a new way of collecting reliable performance data.[^s5] Instead of rating skills, team leaders answer four questions about future actions they would take regarding each person, such as whether they would give them the highest compensation increase and whether they are ready for promotion.[^s2] The design aimed to be faster, more individualized and oriented to future performance rather than past assessment.[^s5]

## Connections

Performance management sits under **management control systems** and **human resource management**, as the mechanism that translates organizational goals into individual expectations and consequences.[^s1] It contains **performance appraisal** as its rating step.[^s3] **Management by objectives**, **OKRs** and the **balanced scorecard** are goal and measurement frameworks used in its planning stage, and **goal-setting theory** explains why specific goals and feedback can raise performance, while the feedback meta-analysis shows the limits of that mechanism.[^s2][^s4] **Deloitte** exemplifies the shift from annual ratings toward continuous, development-oriented performance management.[^s5]
