# The Work of Managers: Functions, Roles and Skills

**Management** is the process of guiding how an organization develops, maintains and allocates its resources so that it reaches its goals; managers are the people who carry that process out.[^s1] As a field, management sits at the centre of business administration: operations management, organizational behavior, decision-making and public administration all treat it as their parent discipline. This node describes what managing *is*; the history of competing schools of thought about it is covered under management theory.

## Functions: planning, organizing, leading, controlling

The most common textbook description breaks management into four functions. Planning anticipates problems and opportunities and designs responses to them; organizing coordinates and allocates the resources the plans require; leading guides people through implementation; and controlling reviews results and makes corrections that feed the next round of planning.[^s1] The functions form a cycle rather than a sequence, and managers usually perform several at once.[^s1] This functional view descends from Henri Fayol's classical administrative theory.

## Roles: what managers actually do

Henry Mintzberg approached management from observation rather than prescription. In a 1975 Harvard Business Review article, later reissued as an HBR Classic, he drew on a study of five chief executives and other research on managers to contrast common beliefs about managerial work with what managers were actually seen to do.[^s4] He grouped managerial work into three kinds of roles.[^s4] OpenStax lists the ten roles within them:[^s2]

- **Interpersonal:** figurehead, leader and liaison, which stem from formal authority and relationships inside and outside the chain of command.[^s2]
- **Informational:** monitor, disseminator and spokesperson, through which managers collect, share and represent information.[^s2]
- **Decisional:** entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator and negotiator, often exercised with incomplete or ambiguous information.[^s2]

Diary studies point the same way: in Rosemary Stewart's study of 160 British managers, 47% of time went to peers, 41% to their own unit and 12% to superiors.[^s2]

## Skills and levels

Managers are usually arranged in a pyramid of top, middle and supervisory (first-line) levels. The skills they need are often grouped as technical (specialized know-how such as preparing financial statements), human relations (communicating with and motivating people) and conceptual (seeing the organization as a whole and its place in its environment).[^s3] Conceptual skills matter most at the top of the pyramid, while technical skills matter most for supervisors who work alongside the employees producing goods or services.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Whether "good management" can be measured was long disputed. Nicholas Bloom and John Van Reenen scored management practices in 732 medium-sized manufacturers in the US, France, Germany and the UK and found the scores strongly associated with productivity, profitability, Tobin's Q, sales growth and survival.[^s5] They also found a long tail of badly run firms in every country, more common where product-market competition was weak and where family firms passed control to the eldest son.[^s5] The later World Management Survey had covered about 11,000 firms in 34 countries by 2016, and the researchers estimated that management quality accounted for about 30 percent of cross-country productivity differences in their sample.[^s6]

That research argues that basic monitoring, target-setting and people practices behave like a technology that raises output, which challenges the contingency view that good practice depends mainly on a firm's size, industry, country and culture.[^s6] The two descriptive traditions also sit uneasily together: the functional model presents an orderly cycle of planning and review,[^s1] while Mintzberg's managers spend their time building networks, negotiating, handling conflicts and deciding on little or ambiguous information.[^s4] A practical limitation is self-assessment bias; managers tend to rate their own practices highly, which is why the researchers relied on double-blind interviews.[^s6]

## In practice

The survey approach gives managers a concrete checklist: are processes monitored, are targets clear and linked to data, and are people hired, promoted and removed on performance? Because these practices need skilled staff and buy-in at every level, firms cannot switch them on quickly.[^s6]

## Connections

Management is studied within business administration and was shaped by Fayol's functions and Mintzberg's roles.[^s1][^s4] It contrasts with leadership, which emphasizes influence and direction more than planning and control, and it contains specialized branches such as operations management. Its measurement through the World Management Survey links it to productivity research.[^s5][^s6] Related nodes include management theory (the schools of thought), span of control (how many people one manager directs), decision-making (the decisional roles) and organizational behavior (the human side of managing).[^s2][^s3]
