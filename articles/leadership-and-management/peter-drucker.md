# Peter Drucker

Peter Ferdinand Drucker (born 19 November 1909 in Vienna; died 11 November 2005 in Claremont, California) was a writer, teacher and adviser whose books helped establish management as a field of study and practice. He earned a doctorate in international and public law at the University of Frankfurt in 1931, left Germany for England in 1933 after Hitler came to power, and moved to the United States in 1937, becoming a citizen in 1943. He taught at New York University from 1950 to 1971 and at Claremont Graduate University from 1971 until his death.[^s1] The Drucker Institute counts 39 books plus many articles, and notes that he described himself as a social ecologist who studied how people organize and interact.[^s2]

## Key ideas

**The corporation as a social institution.** In 1943 General Motors invited Drucker to study its organization. The resulting book, *Concept of the Corporation* (1946), is described by Britannica as the first complete assessment of a large company as a social institution; *The Practice of Management* (1954) then set out a general approach to running a business.[^s1]

**Management by objectives (MBO).** Britannica credits Drucker with inventing MBO, an approach that steers managerial work through explicitly set objectives.[^s1] Management historian Ronald Greenwood traced its development in the *Academy of Management Review* (1981), crediting Drucker with the concept and General Electric executive Harold Smiddy with helping put it into practice.[^s3]

**The knowledge worker.** Drucker coined the term in the late 1950s and spent decades analysing an economy in which more people work with their minds than their hands. The Drucker Institute also credits him with anticipating privatization, decentralization, Japan's economic rise and the need for lifelong learning.[^s2]

**The theory of the business.** His 1994 *Harvard Business Review* article argued that every organization runs on assumptions about its customers, competitors, technology and core strengths, and that firms falter when those assumptions stop matching reality. Archival material shows he was already sketching the idea in a 1958 memo for General Electric, and he treated it as an act of entrepreneurial judgment rather than calculation.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Drucker wrote mainly from observation and consulting rather than controlled studies, and scholars have long split between praise and criticism of his management writing. A 2023 *Journal of Management History* study observed that no systematic evaluation of his whole body of work existed and used computer-aided text analysis of his writing to fill that gap.[^s6] His best-known technique also drew a sharp critique: W. Edwards Deming's point 11b called on managers to eliminate management by objective and numerical goals and to substitute leadership in their place, a direct challenge to managing through targets.[^s5]

## In practice

Drucker preferred not to be called a consultant, yet he advised corporations, nonprofits and governments.[^s1] Clients and admirers listed by the Drucker Institute include General Electric, IBM, Intel, Procter & Gamble, the Girl Scouts, the Salvation Army and the Red Cross. BusinessWeek called him "the man who invented management", and he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2002.[^s2] A foundation for nonprofit management founded in his honour in 1990 was renamed the Leader to Leader Institute in 2003.[^s1]

## Connections

Drucker sits under **management theory** as one of its founding writers.[^s1] His most direct legacy in this graph is **management by objectives**, a technique Greenwood's history attributes to him and which the **OKRs** and **balanced scorecard** nodes both record as an influence on their own goal-setting designs.[^s3] His study of **General Motors** links him to the history of the multidivisional corporation,[^s1] while the **knowledge worker** and the **theory of the business** are concepts he named.[^s2][^s4] **W. Edwards Deming** is the contrasting voice, rejecting goal-driven control in favour of improving the system.[^s5]
