# Contingency Theory of Leadership

The **contingency theory of leadership**, usually identified with Fred Fiedler's *contingency model of leadership effectiveness*, holds that no leadership style is best everywhere: a group performs well when the leader's style fits the degree of control the situation offers.[^s1] Fiedler introduced the model in 1964 and set it out more fully in a 1967 book, arguing that both task-oriented and relationship-oriented leaders can be effective, but in different settings.[^s4] It was one of the first theories to move leadership research away from the search for universal traits and behaviors, building on Ralph Stogdill's earlier observation that the qualities a leader needs depend heavily on the situation.[^s1]

## How it works

The model has two halves. The first is the leader's style, measured with the **least-preferred-coworker (LPC) scale**: the leader describes the person they have worked with least well on a set of bipolar adjectives. Leaders who still describe that person fairly kindly (high LPC) are classed as relationship-oriented; those who rate them harshly (low LPC) are classed as task-oriented.[^s1][^s2] In its later form the scale had 18 eight-point items, and Fiedler treated scores of 63 or lower as low LPC and 73 or higher as high LPC, with a middle band between.[^s4]

The second half is **situational favorableness**, later renamed situational control. Three factors determine it, in descending order of weight: leader-member relations, task structure and the leader's position power.[^s1] Splitting each factor into good/poor, structured/unstructured and strong/weak gives eight situations, or octants, ranked from most to least favorable to the leader.[^s5]

The prediction is curvilinear. Task-oriented, low-LPC leaders do best when the situation is either very favorable or very unfavorable; relationship-oriented, high-LPC leaders do best in the moderately favorable middle.[^s1][^s2] Because Fiedler regarded style as relatively stable, his practical advice was to change the situation to fit the leader, for example by restructuring the task or adjusting formal authority, rather than trying to retrain the leader's personality.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Support is real but uneven. Strube and Garcia's meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* (1981) pooled 145 hypothesis tests plus the 33 results the model was originally built on and concluded that the model predicted group performance robustly.[^s3] A 1990 review by Peter Verkerk notes that their analysis left only octants 1 and 4 significant on their own, and that Peters, Hartke and Pohlmann (1985) also found overall support but not for the laboratory predictions in octant 2.[^s4] Textbook summaries describe the evidence as partial, with stronger backing for the low-LPC predictions than the high-LPC ones.[^s2]

The main critiques concern the LPC measure itself. Over the years it was given at least five different interpretations, from social distance to cognitive complexity, and reported test-retest reliabilities across 23 studies ranged from .01 to .92.[^s4] Critics also argue that the theory links an attitude score to performance without explaining which behaviors produce the result.[^s1] An early University of Illinois study of 141 undergraduates found that beliefs about effective leader behavior varied mainly with leader-member relations and position power, while task structure explained less than 0.6% of the variance.[^s5] A further limitation is cultural: most leadership research comes from North America and Western Europe, and OpenStax cautions that responses to leader behavior differ across cultures.[^s1]

## In practice

The model's practical legacy is diagnostic. Before choosing a leader for a team or redesigning a role, a manager can ask how good relations with the group are, how clearly the task is defined and how much formal authority the position carries, then decide whether a task-focused or relationship-focused leader fits, or whether the job should be engineered to fit the incumbent.[^s1] Fiedler and colleagues packaged this "leader match" logic into training, which a 1979 field experiment by Fiedler and Mahar was reported to validate.[^s4]

## Connections

The theory sits under the broad study of **leadership** and within **management theory**'s shift from one-best-way prescriptions to contingency thinking.[^s1] It was **created by Fred Fiedler** and is **measured by** the **least-preferred-coworker scale**.[^s4] It **contrasts with trait theories of leadership**, which looked for qualities that work in every setting.[^s1] Later contingency approaches relaxed Fiedler's fixed-style assumption: **path-goal theory** has leaders adjust their behavior to followers and tasks, **situational leadership** matches style to follower readiness, and the **Vroom–Yetton decision model** prescribes how participative a decision should be.[^s2] Work on **Hofstede's cultural dimensions** extends the contingency idea to national culture.[^s1]
