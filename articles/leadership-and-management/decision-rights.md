# Decision Rights: Allocating Authority Over Who Decides What

**Decision rights** are the organization's answer to the question "who gets to decide this?" They specify, decision by decision, who holds the authority to commit resources, who must be consulted or must approve, and who carries the choice out. Introductory management texts describe the underlying building block as authority: legitimate power granted by the organization that lets a person direct action and expect it to be carried out, and that managers delegate down the chain of command while remaining accountable for the outcomes.[^s6] Decision rights turn that general idea into a deliberate design variable, alongside reporting lines and incentives.

## Origin: knowledge, agency and the assignment problem

The economic theory of decision rights comes from Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling's paper "Specific and General Knowledge, and Organizational Structure" (working paper 1990; published in *Contract Economics*, 1992).[^s1] They separate *general* knowledge, such as prices and quantities that are cheap to transmit, from *specific* knowledge, such as local know-how that is costly to move. Building on Friedrich Hayek's argument that dispersed knowledge calls for decentralization, they argue that performance improves when decision rights are co-located with the knowledge needed to use them, either by moving information up to decision makers or by moving rights down to those who hold it.[^s1]

Moving rights down is not free. Self-interested agents may pursue goals that differ from the firm's, so delegation creates agency costs, while centralization creates costs of acting on poor information. Jensen and Meckling place the optimal level of decentralization where the marginal gain from better information equals the marginal rise in agency costs. They pair every rights assignment with a control system of performance measurement and rewards, because markets solve the problem by letting owners sell rights (alienability), whereas firms must design substitute rules.[^s1]

Philippe Aghion and Jean Tirole later sharpened the picture by distinguishing *formal* authority, the right to decide, from *real* authority, effective control over decisions. Real authority depends on who is informed; a subordinate gains it when the boss is overloaded, the decision is urgent or the rules are lenient. Granting more real authority encourages initiative but weakens the principal's control.[^s2]

## How it works in practice

Practitioners translate the theory into decision maps or role charts. Bain & Company's RAPID tool, described by Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko in 2006, assigns five roles for each important decision: Recommend, Agree (a limited veto), Perform, Input, and Decide, with a single person holding the D as the point of accountability.[^s5] The authors locate most bottlenecks at four interfaces: global versus local, center versus business unit, function versus function, and the company versus outside partners.[^s5]

Jensen's own practical advice, summarized by HBS Working Knowledge in 2005, is to review assignments regularly as conditions change, avoid both overcentralization and decision by consensus, assign rights unambiguously, and not reassign a right merely because a sound decision produced a bad outcome. Jensen argued that many apparent communication problems are really unclear decision rights.[^s3] One example: a conglomerate that pulled foreign bid pricing back to US headquarters lost business to a European rival that promised quotes within 24 hours.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Survey evidence suggests clear decision rights matter more for execution than reorganizing boxes on the chart. In a 2008 *Harvard Business Review* study drawing on 26,743 responses from 31 companies, Gary Neilson, Karla Martin and Elizabeth Powers found that the first eight of 17 execution traits related to decision rights or information. The top trait was that everyone knows which decisions they are responsible for: 71% agreed in strong-execution firms versus 32% in weak ones.[^s4] Bain reported in 2006 that only about 15% of more than 350 global organizations it surveyed practised effective decision making.[^s5]

These findings come largely from consultancies' own diagnostic surveys, so they show association rather than causation and are self-reported. The theory also has limits: Aghion and Tirole show that formal charts may misstate where control really lies,[^s2] and Jensen and Meckling stress that even an optimal allocation leaves some poor decisions, because agency and information costs can only be traded off, not eliminated.[^s1]

## Example

Caterpillar, after losing roughly $1 billion (as reported in the 2008 study), reorganized into business units with their own profit-and-loss accountability, pushing pricing and operating decisions down; Neilson and colleagues report it was back to a working model within about 18 months.[^s4]

## Connections

Decision rights are a component of **organizational structure** and a core task of **management**, and they are the concrete content of **delegation** and of the **centralization and decentralization** choice.[^s6][^s1] They interact with **span of control**: wide spans only work if subordinates hold the rights to act without escalating.[^s3] The concept was **created by Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling**, **introduced in** their 1992 paper, and **influenced by Friedrich Hayek**'s knowledge argument.[^s1] It **depends on** the economics of **agency costs**, which set the price of delegation.[^s1] The **RAPID** framework is a practitioner tool for documenting decision rights,[^s5] and clear rights are a leading driver of **strategy execution**.[^s4]
