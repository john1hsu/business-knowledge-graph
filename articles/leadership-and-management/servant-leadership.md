# Servant Leadership

**Servant leadership** is a value-based approach to leading in which the leader's primary motive is to serve others, and leadership is a role taken up in order to serve better. Robert K. Greenleaf coined the term in his 1970 essay *The Servant as Leader*, arguing that the servant-leader is a servant first, someone who naturally wants to serve and only later consciously chooses to lead.[^s1]

## Origin

Greenleaf spent roughly forty years at AT&T, retiring in 1964 as its director of management research, and that same year founded the Center for Applied Ethics, later renamed the Greenleaf Center for Servant-Leadership.[^s2] He said the idea came from reading Hermann Hesse's novel *Journey to the East*, in which Leo, a servant who does the travelling party's menial chores and sustains its spirit, turns out to have been the guiding leader of the Order all along; when Leo disappears, the group falls apart.[^s1][^s2] Greenleaf drew the lesson that a great leader is experienced first as a servant. He developed the theme across later essays collected by Paulist Press in 1979, and spent his remaining years as a writer, teacher and consultant until his death in 1990.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

Greenleaf offered a single outcome-based standard rather than a list of behaviours. His "best test" asks whether those served grow as persons and whether, while being served, they become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous and more likely to become servants themselves; he added a question about the effect on the least privileged in society.[^s1] The model is therefore follower-centred: the leader's attention goes to followers' development and wellbeing, and organizational performance is expected to follow from that rather than being the leader's first aim.[^s1][^s3]

Later scholars turned the philosophy into a measurable construct. A 2019 systematic review of 285 articles published between 1998 and 2018 found that the field had suffered from a lack of coherence and clarity about what servant leadership is, offered a new definition, and distinguished it from other value-based approaches such as transformational, authentic and ethical leadership.[^s3] The same review evaluated 16 different servant-leadership measures on the rigour of their construction and validation, and mapped the construct's antecedents, outcomes, mediators and moderators.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The main empirical question has been whether servant leadership is distinct from transformational leadership. A meta-analysis of 130 independent samples from 124 publications by Lee, Lyubovnikova, Tian and Knight (2019) found a large corrected correlation of .52 between the two, which the authors acknowledged points to some empirical redundancy.[^s5] Yet the same study found that servant leadership still predicted outcomes beyond transformational, authentic and ethical leadership combined, including individual task performance, organizational citizenship behaviour, creativity, voice and team performance.[^s4][^s5] Its effects on individual and team behaviour ran partly through trust in the leader, procedural justice and leader-member exchange.[^s4] It showed no incremental validity for counterproductive work behaviour.[^s5]

Critiques remain. The construct-clarity problem flagged by Eva and colleagues means studies have used inconsistent definitions and many measures of uneven quality, which complicates comparison across findings.[^s3] Meta-analytic effect sizes also vary with research design, national culture and industry, so the strength of the relationship depends on context.[^s4] The original concept is also partly normative and spiritual: Greenleaf framed it as a matter of character and a test of moral outcomes, which sits uneasily with the behavioural measurement that later research requires.[^s1][^s3]

## In practice

The best-known field study is research by Robert Liden, Sandy Wayne, Chenwei Liao and Jeremy Meuser at the restaurant chain Jason's Deli, covering 961 employees in 71 restaurants across ten U.S. metropolitan areas.[^s6] Using surveys of managers, employees and customers plus company records, they found that servant leaders build a "serving culture" in which staff copy the serving behaviour; stores with such leaders showed 6% higher job performance, 8% more customer-service behaviours and employees 50% less likely to leave.[^s6] The study illustrates the theory's mechanism: service is modelled by the leader, spreads through the unit's culture, and then shows up in performance.[^s6]

## Connections

Servant leadership is a theory within the broader field of **leadership** (`kt:leadership`). It was created by **Robert K. Greenleaf** (`kt:robert-k-greenleaf`) and introduced in his 1970 essay **The Servant as Leader** (`kt:the-servant-as-leader-1970`).[^s1][^s2] It **contrasts with transformational leadership** (`kt:transformational-leadership`): both are value-based and correlate strongly, but servant leadership puts followers' growth ahead of organizational goals and explains outcome variance that transformational leadership does not.[^s3][^s5] Its effects run partly through **leader-member exchange** (`kt:leader-member-exchange`) and trust, and the Jason's Deli research shows it operating through **organizational culture** (`kt:organizational-culture`).[^s4][^s6] Its emphasis on voice and follower wellbeing links it to **psychological safety** (`kt:psychological-safety`) and to **employee motivation** (`kt:employee-motivation`).[^s4][^s5]
