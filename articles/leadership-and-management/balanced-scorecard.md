# Balanced Scorecard

The **balanced scorecard** (BSC) is a performance-management framework that asks leaders to look at an organization through several measurement lenses at once rather than relying on financial results alone. Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton introduced it in a 1992 *Harvard Business Review* article, arguing that traditional financial measures can send misleading signals about continuous improvement and innovation, and that executives need a balanced view drawn from several perspectives simultaneously.[^s1] Kaplan later described the scorecard's purpose as integrating the measurement of intangible assets, such as customer relationships, processes and employee capabilities, into companies' management systems.[^s2]

## Origin

The framework grew out of a 1990 multi-company research project run by the Nolan Norton Institute on performance measurement in companies whose value creation depended on intangible assets.[^s2] The HBR article describes the work as a yearlong project with 12 companies that were at the leading edge of performance management.[^s1] A pivotal input came from Analog Devices: at a 1990 project meeting, Arthur Schneiderman, its vice president of quality and productivity, presented a corporate scorecard on which every quality measure had improved, yet the company's stock price had fallen by nearly 70% over the same three years.[^s2] OpenStax credits Schneiderman's 1987 work at Analog Devices as the starting point that Kaplan and Norton then developed into the four-perspective model.[^s3] Kaplan also traces earlier roots to Peter Drucker's management by objectives (1954), a General Electric measurement project of the 1950s and Robert Anthony's planning-and-control framework (1965).[^s2]

## How it works

A scorecard organizes objectives and measures into four perspectives — **financial**, **customer**, **internal business process** and **learning and growth** — each framed by a guiding question, with financial results remaining the ultimate outcome measures.[^s2] Typical financial measures include return on investment, residual income, economic value added and revenue growth; customer measures include satisfaction, retention and market share; internal-process measures cover defects, downtime and cycle time; and learning-and-growth measures include training hours, employee turnover and new-product introductions.[^s3] Nonfinancial measures look at current activities expected to drive future results, while financial measures report what has already happened.[^s3]

Implementation starts from strategy: corporate goals are translated into objectives and targets at each level of the organization, and measures should be obtainable and within the control of the person evaluated.[^s3] Kaplan and Norton's later work extended the scorecard from a measurement tool into a strategy-execution system, introducing **strategy maps** (2000–2004) that chart cause-and-effect links from intangible assets and processes to the customer value proposition and financial outcomes, followed by books on alignment (2006) and linking strategy to operations (2008).[^s2] Over roughly 15 years, thousands of private, public and nonprofit organizations adopted it.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The most persistent academic critique concerns the scorecard's cause-and-effect claims. Nørreklit and colleagues, analysing the core BSC texts in 2012, argued that the model is presented less like scholarly work than like a persuasive "myth" genre, with the weakness most visible in its causal statements, and called for a more rigorous treatment of how such relationships are constructed.[^s4]

Field evidence also shows practical problems when scorecards drive pay. Ittner, Larcker and Meyer's multiyear study of a balanced-scorecard bonus plan at a large retail bank, published in *The Accounting Review* in 2003, found that supervisors placed greater weight on financial measures, did not give more weight to the nonfinancial measures that best predicted results, and used subjectivity to add or ignore measures; complaints of favouritism followed and the bank eventually abandoned the scorecard.[^s5] Michael Jensen raised a related objection in 2001: an unweighted set of measures gives managers no single score to maximise. Kaplan conceded that compensation cannot rest on unweighted metrics but argued that incentives are only one part of a management system, likening the scorecard to an aircraft's instrument panel.[^s2]

## In practice

Analog Devices is the canonical cautionary example behind the framework: operational quality gains that were never connected to customer or financial outcomes did not protect shareholder value, which convinced Kaplan and Norton that a complete measurement system must link operational improvement to customer and financial performance.[^s2] OpenStax notes that scorecards are also used in strategy-review meetings and can carry ethics-related measures such as training participation, compliance and turnover, and that broad buy-in and jointly set targets are needed for the tool to work.[^s3]

## Connections

The balanced scorecard is a tool of **performance management** and of **management control systems**, the broader disciplines that set targets, measure results and act on them.[^s2][^s3] It was created by **Robert S. Kaplan** and **David P. Norton** and was influenced by **management by objectives**; its cause-and-effect logic was later formalised as the **strategy map**.[^s2] **Analog Devices** exemplifies the problem it was designed to solve.[^s2] Kaplan positions it against **stakeholder theory**: the scorecard starts from strategy and includes stakeholders only where they are essential to it, whereas stakeholder theory starts from stakeholder objectives.[^s2] Because a scorecard measure is only useful against a basis for comparison, **benchmarking** is a natural source of its targets, and the scorecard's cascading of corporate goals into objectives at each level parallels **OKRs**.[^s3]
