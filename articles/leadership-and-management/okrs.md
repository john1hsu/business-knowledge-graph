# Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)

**Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)** is a goal-setting framework in which an organization, team or individual states an *objective*, meaning what is to be achieved, and supports it with a small set of *key results* that are measurable and verifiable, so that each one is either met or not.[^s1] A common guideline is one objective backed by three to five key results, and Andy Grove summarised the logic as a sentence template: "I will (objective) as measured by (key results)."[^s1]

## Origin

Grove, the Hungarian-born manager who later led Intel, is usually described as the father of OKRs.[^s2] He adapted the idea from Peter Drucker's **management by objectives** (MBO), introduced in the 1950s to focus people on results rather than on visible activity.[^s2] Grove's early version at Intel, sometimes called "iMBOs", explicitly tied each objective to its key results, and it shifted the cycle from MBO's annual rhythm to a quarterly one.[^s1] What Matters, the organisation founded around John Doerr's OKR work, credits the method with helping Intel move from memory chips to microprocessors, a period in which it reports revenue rising from $1.9 billion to $26 billion under Grove.[^s2] Doerr, who had learned the method under Grove, introduced OKRs to Google's founders in 1999 and has since spread the approach to organisations such as the Gates Foundation.[^s2]

## How it works

OKRs normally run on a quarterly cycle and are graded at the end of it; objectives can persist for a year or more while their key results change as work progresses.[^s1] Unlike traditional MBO, OKRs are deliberately separated from compensation, and they are meant to make strategy visible across the organisation.[^s1]

Google's internal playbook distinguishes two kinds of OKR. **Committed** OKRs are outcomes a team agrees to deliver, adjusting schedules and resources to do so, and are expected to score 1.0 on a 0–1.0 scale; a miss calls for an explanation and a blameless postmortem.[^s3] **Aspirational** OKRs describe a desired future without a clear path or resources yet, are expected to average about 0.7, and roll forward until completed.[^s3] The playbook says committed OKRs should consume most, but not all, of a team's capacity, while committed and aspirational OKRs together should exceed it.[^s3] It also lists common traps: mislabelling the two kinds, writing business-as-usual or low-value objectives, setting timid or sandbagged targets, and choosing key results that are necessary but not sufficient to achieve the objective.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Peer-reviewed evidence on OKRs specifically is still thin. Nopadol Rompho's qualitative study of 204 staff in 26 Thai organisations, published in *Benchmarking: An International Journal* (online 2023), found that OKRs helped employees accept performance indicators, aligned indicators with strategy and reduced improper target setting; the author cautions that the organisations had used OKRs for only about six months and that interview data limit generalisation.[^s4]

The broader goal-setting literature supplies the main critique. Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman argued in *Academy of Management Perspectives* (2009) that the benefits of specific, challenging goals have been overstated and their side effects overlooked: narrowed focus, distorted risk-taking, more unethical behaviour, inhibited learning, corroded culture and reduced intrinsic motivation.[^s5] They recommend treating goals like prescription-strength medication that needs careful dosing.[^s5] Bazerman illustrated the risk with the Ford Pinto, where a weight-and-cost target contributed to overlooked safety problems, and suggested favouring learning goals where desired behaviours are unclear.[^s6] OKR practice addresses part of this critique by decoupling goals from pay and accepting partial scores on stretch objectives, but it does not remove the risk of narrow focus.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

OKRs are a tool of **performance management** and of **strategy execution**, translating strategy into measurable quarterly commitments.[^s1] The framework was created by **Andy Grove** and **influenced by management by objectives**, from which it differs in cadence, in linking objectives to verifiable results and in separating goals from compensation.[^s1][^s2] **Intel** and **Google** are its canonical adopters.[^s2] Its aspirational OKRs operationalise **stretch goals**, the practice that **goal-setting theory** research endorses and that Ordóñez and colleagues warn can backfire.[^s3][^s5] OKRs are often compared with the **balanced scorecard**: both cascade strategic goals into measures, and Rompho's study frames OKRs as a response to the same performance-measurement problems such systems target.[^s4]
