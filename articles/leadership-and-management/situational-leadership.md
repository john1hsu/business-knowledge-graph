# Hersey-Blanchard Situational Leadership Model

The **Hersey-Blanchard Situational Leadership Model** holds that there is no single best way to lead: the right style depends on how ready a follower is to take on a particular task, and a leader should adjust as that readiness changes.[^s1] Paul Hersey and Kenneth H. Blanchard, then at Ohio University, introduced it as the **life cycle theory of leadership** in the May 1969 issue of *Training and Development Journal*, and it became widely known under the Situational Leadership name.[^s1] The authors cited Fred Fiedler's contingency research as support for a situational view, but their prescription is that leaders diagnose the environment and adapt their own behavior to it.[^s1]

## Origin

Hersey and Blanchard built on the Ohio State leadership studies, which described leader behavior along two independent dimensions: *initiating structure* (organizing roles, methods and deadlines) and *consideration* (warmth, trust and socio-emotional support).[^s1][^s2] They relabeled these task behavior and relationship behavior and added a third, effectiveness dimension, following William Reddin's three-dimensional model: any style can be effective or ineffective depending on whether it fits the environment.[^s1] They also argued that Blake and Mouton's Managerial Grid described attitudes rather than behavior and wrongly implied that one high-task, high-relationship "team" style was always best.[^s1] Their notion of follower maturity drew on Chris Argyris's work on independence and responsibility and on David McClelland's achievement motivation.[^s1]

## How it works

The model crosses task and relationship behavior to form four styles. Later Hersey-Blanchard materials name them S1 *telling* (high task, low relationship), S2 *selling* (high task, high relationship), S3 *participating* (high relationship, low task) and S4 *delegating* (low task, low relationship).[^s2]

The deciding variable is the follower's **readiness** (originally "maturity"), defined as the ability and willingness to take responsibility for directing one's own behavior on a given task.[^s2] Readiness runs from R1 (low) to R4 (high) and is task-specific, not a personality trait: the same engineer may be R4 at debugging and R1 at presenting to the board.[^s2] The 1969 article stressed that maturity reflects experience and education, not chronological age.[^s1]

Matching is prescriptive. Low-readiness followers get S1; as readiness rises the leader adds support (S2), then withdraws direction (S3), and finally steps back to S4, tracing a bell-shaped curve across the grid.[^s1][^s2] Hersey and Blanchard used a parent-child illustration, from close structure in infancy to independence in adulthood, and warned that offering too much relationship support too early can be read as permissiveness, so changes should be gradual and reward successive approximations of the desired performance.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The model is popular in training but its empirical record is weak. A 1983 critique in the *Academy of Management Review* argued that the concept of job-relevant maturity is ambiguous, that the prescriptive model has internal flaws, and that the LEAD questionnaire used to measure style has problems.[^s3] Norris and Vecchio's 1992 replication with 91 nurses and their supervisors found results in the predicted direction for low- and moderate-maturity followers, but none were statistically significant.[^s4] A 1998 study of 151 senior executives at a Fortune 100 company found that only 18 leader-follower pairs matched the prescribed fit, against 126 that did not, and called the theory intuitively appealing but empirically contradictory.[^s5]

More recent work suggests the problem may partly be measurement. Thompson and Glasø (2018) found support for the model's principles when the leader's rating of a follower and the follower's self-rating agreed, but not for Blanchard's advice to defer to the follower's self-rating when they disagree; they propose a "convergent" version built on leader-follower agreement.[^s6]

## In practice

The model's appeal is its simplicity: diagnose readiness for this task, then choose the style. The Australian Army's leadership resources, for example, present it as a guide to matching style to readiness, as with a new hire who needs explicit instructions (S1) and later becomes a trusted owner of the work (S4).[^s2] Hersey and Blanchard also applied it to organizational design, arguing that span of control should depend on subordinates' maturity rather than hierarchical level, since mature subordinates let a manager supervise more people.[^s1]

## Connections

The model sits under **leadership** and **management theory** as a situational approach.[^s1] It was **created by Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard** and **influenced by Chris Argyris** and the **Ohio State leadership studies**, whose two behavior dimensions form its grid.[^s1][^s2] It **contrasts with the Managerial Grid**, which favors one style, and is a sibling of Fiedler's **contingency theory of leadership**, which the authors cited as support.[^s1] It also resembles **path-goal theory**, in which leaders likewise diagnose the situation and flex among several styles.[^s7] Its maturity argument links it to **span of control**.[^s1]
