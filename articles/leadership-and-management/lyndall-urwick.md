# Lyndall Urwick

Lyndall Fownes Urwick (1891-1983) was a British consultant, author and organizer of the interwar management movement, and the most persistent twentieth-century champion of a "science" of administration. Born in Malvern and educated at Repton and New College, Oxford, he joined his family's glove maker, Fownes Brothers, in 1913 and became a partner in 1916.[^s1] Serving as an officer on the Western Front, where he won the Military Cross and DSO, he read Frederick Taylor's *Shop Management* and resolved to devote his career to management.[^s1]

## Career

After the war Urwick worked briefly in the glove trade and with the National Institute of Industrial Psychology, then in 1922 joined Rowntree & Co. in York to reorganize its sales and office administration. There he met Oliver Sheldon and did most of the work of setting up Rowntree's six Management Research Groups (1926-28), forums where firms compared practice.[^s1] From 1928 until it closed for lack of money in 1933 he directed the International Management Institute in Geneva, and he was general secretary of the International Committee for Scientific Management from 1932 to 1935.[^s1] In 1934 he founded the London consultancy Urwick Orr & Partners, remaining senior partner until 1965; one historian characterizes its approach as adapting Taylorism to British conditions with an emphasis on joint consultation and profit-sharing.[^s1] He co-founded the British Management Council in 1937, advised the Treasury during the Second World War and sat on the council of the British Institute of Management, part of a wave of postwar British management institutions in most of which, according to his 2010 Oxford biography, he played a key role.[^s1][^s6]

## Ideas

Urwick's books include *Papers on the Science of Administration* (1937, co-edited with the American public administration scholar Luther Gulick), *The Elements of Administration* (1944), *The Making of Scientific Management* (three volumes, 1947-49, with E. F. L. Brech) and *The Golden Book of Management* (1956), which profiled some seventy management pioneers.[^s1] His central project was integration. Parker and Ritson, reviewing sixty years of his writing, show that he defined scientific management far more broadly than most contemporaries, as fact-based analysis of the whole enterprise, and used that definition to fold Taylor's methods into the newer schools of thought, while comparing his position with Henri Fayol's.[^s2] He also drew heavily on Mary Parker Follett and argued that management should rest on research into facts rather than opinion, that training mattered and that firms should adopt a marketing point of view built on discovering what customers need.[^s1]

His influence on organization design came largely through principles. *Papers on the Science of Administration* collected his essay "Organization as a Technical Problem" alongside Gulick's work and reprinted V. A. Graicunas's 1933 analysis of superior-subordinate relationships, first published in the bulletin of the Geneva institute Urwick then directed.[^s1][^s4][^s5] In a 1956 Harvard Business Review article, "The Manager's Span of Control," he defended the rule that no superior can directly supervise more than five or, at most, six subordinates whose work interlocks, stressing that the limit applies only when subordinates' work is interdependent.[^s4] American public administration writers of the 1940s, such as Norman Pearson, drew on Urwick when arguing that Fayol's administrative principles, including unity of command, were the necessary complement to Taylor's shop-floor methods.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Historians credit Urwick with making Fayol central to British management theory: Smith and Boyns identify him and the British neoclassical school as the main channel for Fayol's ideas from the 1920s to the 1970s.[^s3] Their archival study of engineering firms, however, finds that this theoretical influence translated into much more limited change in actual company practice.[^s3] His span rule also drew qualification: a 1966 study of organization size noted that interlocks among a manager's reports are often infrequent in practice, as with an actuary and a controller reporting to the same executive, which weakens any fixed numerical limit.[^s4] Parker and Ritson argue that he is often stereotyped as a narrow Taylorist and that his legacy is better understood through his unusually broad and evolving definition of the field; they urge readers to consult his original writing rather than convenient labels.[^s2] He is also regarded as the founder of management history in Britain, notably for giving European pioneers equal credit with Americans.[^s1]

## Connections

Urwick belongs under **management theory** as the leading British voice of its classical, administrative school. He was **influenced by Henri Fayol**, whose principles he spread in Britain, by **Frederick Winslow Taylor** and **scientific management**, which first drew him to the field, and by **Mary Parker Follett**.[^s1][^s2][^s3] He **influenced** the classical doctrine of **span of control**, alongside **V. A. Graicunas** and **Luther Gulick**, his co-editor.[^s4] As founder of Urwick Orr & Partners he is also a figure in the history of **management consulting**.[^s1]
