# Henri Fayol

Henri Fayol (1841-1925) was a French mining engineer and executive whose late-career writings gave management its first general theory written from the top of the organisation. Born into a lower-middle-class family, he graduated as a mining engineer from the National School of Mines at Saint-Étienne at 19 and joined the Commentry-Fourchambault company (Comambault) in 1860, staying until he retired in 1918.[^s1] OpenStax groups him with the administrative school: where Frederick Taylor studied the shop floor, Fayol studied how the whole enterprise is run by its senior managers.[^s2]

## Career and the 1916 treatise

At Comambault Fayol first worked on mine fires and explosions, then directed a group of coal mines from 1872 to 1888. He was named director-general in 1888, when the firm was close to bankruptcy, and by his retirement thirty years later it was financially strong and one of Europe's largest industrial combines.[^s1] His monograph *Administration industrielle et générale* appeared in 1916 in the *Bulletin de la Société de l'Industrie Minérale*, was issued as a book in France in 1925, and reached most English-speaking readers through Constance Storrs's 1949 translation, *General and Industrial Management*.[^s1] After retiring he founded and chaired a Centre of Administrative Studies, which in 1924 merged into the Comité National de l'Organisation Française.[^s1]

Peaucelle and Guthrie, reconstructing his private life from French civil, military and school archives, note that he published his theory at age 75 and argue that the motive was probably not money: his father's career setbacks and his own schooling shaped his wish to found a science of management and his views on how mathematics should feature in managerial training.[^s4]

## Core ideas

Fayol divided the work of any industrial undertaking into six activities: technical, commercial, financial, security, accounting and managerial.[^s1] Management itself he described through five elements: forecasting and planning, organising, commanding, coordinating and controlling.[^s1][^s3] He paired these with fourteen principles: division of work, authority, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, subordination of individual interest, remuneration, centralisation, the scalar chain, order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative and esprit de corps.[^s2] OpenStax singles out unity of command, esprit de corps built through communication, and fairness in organisational decisions as his signature themes.[^s2]

Modern textbooks often restate his elements in revised form; OpenStax, for example, lists planning, organising, staffing, controlling and directing, so readers should distinguish Fayol's original wording from later adaptations.[^s2][^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Fayol's elements are frequently dismissed as dated or as having been displaced by later observational studies of what managers actually do.[^s3] The most influential of those studies is Henry Mintzberg's 1975 *Harvard Business Review* article, which, based on observing five chief executives and other managers, described the job as a set of interpersonal, informational and decisional roles rather than a tidy planning-and-control cycle.[^s6] Fells replies that Fayol's own framework was itself grounded in observation, and that when compared with the models of Hales, Kotter and Mintzberg, the later findings reinforce rather than refute the five elements.[^s3]

A second line of evidence concerns practice. Smith and Boyns trace how Fayol's ideas fed British management thought from the 1920s into the 1960s and 1970s, including through Lyndall Urwick and the British neoclassical school, but archival records from engineering firms suggest his effect on actual company practice was much more limited than his theoretical influence.[^s5]

## In practice

Fayol's own turnaround of Comambault is the case he drew on: an engineer promoted to general manager of a near-bankrupt combine who rebuilt it into a leading European industrial group.[^s1] His framework still structures how management is taught: introductory textbooks present his functions and principles, such as unity of command, as the starting vocabulary of organising,[^s2] and Fells argues the five elements remain a valid description of managerial work.[^s3]

## Connections

Fayol sits under **management theory** as the founder of its administrative school.[^s2] He **contrasts with Frederick Winslow Taylor**, whose **scientific management** optimised individual tasks while Fayol addressed the whole enterprise.[^s2] He **influenced** the principle of **unity of command**, one of his fourteen principles,[^s2] and **Lyndall Urwick**, through whom his ideas shaped British management theory.[^s5] **Henry Mintzberg** is the classic counterpoint, whose role-based account of managerial work is often set against Fayol's elements.[^s6][^s3]
