# Hoshin Kanri

**Hoshin kanri**, usually rendered in English as *policy deployment* or *strategy deployment*, is a management system for planning and executing a small number of critical strategic objectives across an entire organization. It applies the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle to strategy: senior leaders set a few breakthrough objectives, those objectives are translated into targets and plans at every level, progress is reviewed at regular intervals, and the planning process itself is reviewed and improved each year.[^s1] Darden faculty describe it as a practice common in successful Lean organizations for linking strategic direction to day-to-day operations and improvement plans.[^s4]

## Origin

The method grew up in Japan in the 1960s alongside company-wide quality control, the approach later called total quality management.[^s3] Accounts of its first documentation differ: Barry Witcher traces the term to a 1962 internal Bridgestone Tire document, Ćwiklicki and Obora point to a 1965 report on the planning practices of Deming Prize winners, and a 2022 review credits a 1968 Bridgestone study of prize winners, Toyota among them, with naming the approach.[^s1][^s3][^s2] It spread through Japanese industry after 1970 and reached Western firms in the 1980s.[^s2] The US nonprofit GOAL/QPC published the first English-language report in 1989, and Akao's 1991 English-language book made the method widely known.[^s1][^s3] Western adopters often renamed it: AT&T and Lucent used "policy deployment", Xerox "control for results", and Hewlett-Packard "hoshin planning".[^s2]

## How it works

A typical cycle runs from a vision and a three-to-five-year plan to annual objectives, which are cascaded across departments, implemented, reviewed periodically and evaluated at year-end.[^s1] Hoshin kanri separates *breakthrough* objectives, which need cross-functional change, from *daily management* of routine key performance indicators, often grouped as quality, cost, delivery and employees.[^s1] One synthesis frames the logic as Focus, Alignment, Integration and Review: pick a few priorities, deploy them, fold them into daily work, and diagnose results annually at the top.[^s2]

The distinctive mechanism is **catchball**: draft goals are passed down, across and back up the organization so that each level negotiates what it can achieve and what resources it needs, and goals may change as a result.[^s2][^s5] Practitioners commonly run catchball with an **X-matrix**, a correlation matrix linking long-range objectives, annual objectives, improvement priorities, metrics and owners, together with one-page A3 plans.[^s2][^s5] Some firms add a top-executive audit in which senior managers visit work areas to review progress directly; Nissan used such audits extensively.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence base consists mostly of case studies. A 2022 bibliometric review of 26 journal articles reports, among others, a Land Rover programme whose lead time beat the European average by 19%, a 6% labour-productivity gain over two years at a Taiwanese semiconductor firm, and a healthcare provider that cut urgent waiting times from seven days to three.[^s2] The same review records mixed results: in a Swedish project only one of five small manufacturers improved financially, and catchball at Land Rover involving 700 managers took 12 months.[^s2] Other failures came from overloading staff with too many goals or abandoning reviews after a leadership change.[^s2]

Witcher notes that the literature sits mainly in quality and operations management and is almost absent from mainstream strategy texts, partly because outsiders see the method as mechanistic and tool-heavy and because its inside-out, process-driven logic sits awkwardly with market-positioning views of strategy.[^s1] Ćwiklicki and Obora found that heavily documented versions came to be perceived as bureaucratic, and that in Japanese subsidiaries in Poland, local staff had little say in objectives set by head offices.[^s3]

## In practice

Hewlett-Packard, Florida Power & Light, Xerox UK, Nissan and Tata group companies are frequently cited adopters.[^s1] In Japanese subsidiaries studied in Poland, hoshin kanri was combined with kaizen, quality circles, A3 reporting and individual management-by-objectives targets, with monthly reviews focused on strategic objectives.[^s3]

## Connections

Hoshin kanri is a method of **strategy execution** and **performance management**.[^s4] It is **influenced by management by objectives**, which it extends with PDCA review and TQM tools; one firm studied treated MBO as the individual-level layer of its hoshin system.[^s5][^s3] It **depends on the PDCA cycle** and belongs to the **total quality management** and **lean manufacturing** traditions.[^s1][^s4] It is often compared with the **balanced scorecard**, which Witcher argues says what to measure but little about how to deploy it, and it **contrasts with OKRs**, which also descend from MBO but use quarterly, often aspirational targets rather than an annual negotiated cascade.[^s1] **Hewlett-Packard** and **Xerox** exemplify Western adoption.[^s1][^s2]
