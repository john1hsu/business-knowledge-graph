# Frederick Winslow Taylor

Frederick Winslow Taylor (1856-1915) was an American mechanical engineer, inventor and the first management consultant of note, remembered as the father of scientific management.[^s1] His career ran from the machine shop to the lecture hall: he held more than 40 patents, led the profession's main engineering society, and spent his final decade promoting a system that he believed could raise both wages and profits.[^s1] This node covers the man and his career; the system itself is described under scientific management.

## Early life and training

Taylor was born in Philadelphia on 20 March 1856, the son of a lawyer.[^s1] He entered Phillips Exeter Academy in 1872, led his class, and passed the Harvard entrance examination, but gave up college after night study damaged his eyesight.[^s1] Instead, in 1875 he apprenticed as a patternmaker and machinist at the Enterprise Hydraulic Works in Philadelphia, and he later earned a mechanical engineering degree from Stevens Institute of Technology in 1883 while studying at night.[^s1] He was also a serious sportsman: Stevens' archive records that he and his cousin C. M. Clark won the national lawn-tennis doubles championship.[^s3]

## Midvale and the science of metal cutting

Taylor joined the Midvale Steel Company in 1878 as a machine-shop laborer and within about six years was chief engineer.[^s2] There, beginning in 1881, he timed shop tasks to set standards for each job, the seed of his later theories, and he designed and built a new machine shop.[^s1] He also built what EBSCO describes as the largest steam hammer then made in the United States.[^s2]

The engineering question that absorbed him longest was how fast and how deep a tool could cut steel. His 1906 ASME presidential address, "On the Art of Cutting Metals", distilled some twenty-six years of experiments on the subject.[^s3] An open operations-management textbook puts the experimental base at more than 30,000 trials.[^s5]

## Consultant and inventor

Taylor left Midvale in 1890 to manage paper mills for the Manufacturing Investment Company, and in 1893 opened a consulting practice that at first drew few clients.[^s2] In 1898 Bethlehem Steel retained him exclusively.[^s2] With metallurgist J. Maunsel White he developed the Taylor-White process for heat-treating tool steel, which EBSCO credits with raising cutting capacity by 200 to 300 percent.[^s2] The resulting high-speed tools won an award at the 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle, Taylor reported shaft speeds in a Bethlehem shop rising from 90 to 250 revolutions per minute, and in 1902 the Franklin Institute gave Taylor and White its Elliott Cresson Medal.[^s3]

Bethlehem dismissed him in 1901 after its president withdrew support for his demand for a free hand.[^s2] Letters recovered by historian Charles Wrege show Taylor recovering from a nervous breakdown after leaving, working only an hour or two a day while he paid engineer Sanford E. Thompson, under yearly contracts, to gather time-study data and help write books on construction trades.[^s4]

## Prophet of efficiency

Taylor retired at 45 but kept lecturing on his system at universities and professional societies.[^s1] He was elected ASME president in 1906, and the University of Pennsylvania gave him an honorary doctorate the same year.[^s1] In 1910 Louis Brandeis made Taylor's methods famous by invoking them before the Interstate Commerce Commission in the eastern railroad rate case, and in 1911 sympathetic engineers formed the society later renamed the Taylor Society.[^s2] The Army's adoption of his system at the Watertown Arsenal from 1909 fed labor conflict, and in January 1912 Taylor testified before a special House committee investigating his and other shop-management systems.[^s2][^s1] He died of pneumonia at his Philadelphia home on 21 March 1915.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Taylor's reputation is contested. EBSCO credits him with pioneering rational industrial management but faults his simplistic view of human motivation, which led him to underrate workers' attitudes.[^s2] Critics summarized in the open textbook argue that his approach encouraged authoritarian supervision and monotonous work.[^s5] Wrege's archival work complicates the lone-genius image, showing how much data gathering and writing Taylor delegated to collaborators such as Thompson, including covert timing of workers with stopwatches hidden in "watch books."[^s4] Even his engineering success had a managerial edge: high-speed steel made machines faster, which raised the payoff from planning and pacing shop work precisely.[^s3]

## Legacy in practice

His influence spread internationally, with translations into most major European languages, notable adoption in France after the First World War, and Lenin's call to apply it in Soviet industry.[^s2] His system shaped industrial engineering and production management worldwide, and his emphasis on standardized, specialized work fed into Henry Ford's assembly line.[^s1][^s5] For managers today, Taylor's career is a reminder that the measurement tools of operations management began as engineering experiments, and that their value depended on how workers were brought into them.[^s2]

## Connections

Taylor sits **broader** under **management theory** as the founder of its first systematic school, and is **related** to **scientific management**, the system he created and codified.[^s1] He **influenced** **industrial engineering** and **high-speed steel**, through his Bethlehem metallurgy, and the **Taylor Society** that carried on his work after his death.[^s1][^s3][^s2] His shop-floor, engineering route into management **contrasts with** that of **Henri Fayol**, the administrative-school founder, and he is **related** to the other classical and modern management writers in this domain, **Lyndall Urwick** and **Peter Drucker**.[^s1] His standardized, specialized work fed **mass production** through Ford's assembly line.[^s5]
