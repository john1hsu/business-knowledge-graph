# Strategy Execution

**Strategy execution** (or *strategy implementation*) is the work of turning a chosen strategy into results. The OpenStax *Principles of Management* text defines implementation as carrying out a strategy by assigning tasks to people so that the firm reaches its strategic goals, and stresses that the value-adding work happens far down the hierarchy, in research and development, manufacturing and marketing, rather than in the executive suite.[^s1] Where strategy formulation decides *what* to do, execution concerns how goals are cascaded, resourced, coordinated, monitored and revised over time.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Execution became a distinct management topic because formulation dominated the literature and business-school curricula. Lawrence Hrebiniak of Wharton argued in *Making Strategy Work* (2005) that making strategy work is harder than making strategy, and that MBA programmes trained managers to plan but not to execute.[^s2] Donald Sull, Rebecca Homkes and Charles Sull later observed in *Harvard Business Review* (2015) that writing on strategy outnumbered writing on execution by roughly an order of magnitude, and that in a survey of more than 400 global CEOs, executional excellence ranked first among some 80 challenges.[^s5]

## How it works

Textbook treatments describe a cascade. Executives set direction at the strategic level, division managers choose broad actions at the tactical level, and front-line plans cover daily work such as scheduling, inventory and sales goals; each plan becomes more specific as it moves down.[^s1] Goals are set (often to SMART criteria), plans are designed against the firm's resources and capabilities, plans are implemented, and results are reviewed in a repeating cycle.[^s1]

Hrebiniak frames execution as a process of integrated decisions and actions over time, not a single step, and names eight areas that must be managed together: a guiding model, the strategy itself, change management, power, structure, controls, culture and leadership.[^s2] Robert Kaplan and David Norton's *The Execution Premium* (2008) proposes a six-stage closed loop: develop the strategy, translate it into a strategy map and scorecard, align units and employees, plan operations and budgets, hold strategy review meetings, and test and adapt the strategy.[^s4] They recommend a small Office of Strategy Management to run this loop and to separate monthly strategy reviews from operational meetings, so that strategy is not crowded out by firefighting.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Survey evidence identifies the usual obstacles. In Hrebiniak's surveys of more than 400 managers (243 from a Wharton-Gartner panel and about 200 Wharton executive-education participants), inability to manage change and overcome resistance ranked first in both groups; conflicts with the power structure, poor information sharing across units, unclear accountability and vague strategy followed.[^s2] Sull and colleagues, drawing on a study of about 8,000 managers in 250 companies, reported that 84% of managers said they could rely on their boss and direct reports but only 9% could rely on colleagues in other units, and that only 55% of middle managers could name one of their company's top five priorities.[^s6] They argued that alignment, rigid adherence to plans, one-way communication and pure pay-for-performance are myths of execution, and that execution must also be driven from the middle of the organisation.[^s6]

The field's most quoted statistic is weaker than it looks. Cândido and Santos reviewed claims that 50–90% of strategic initiatives fail and concluded that the true failure rate is unknown, because the estimates rest on outdated, fragmentary or missing evidence; they caution against using such figures to justify changes in theory or practice.[^s3] Kaplan himself conceded that his framework underplayed risk management.[^s4]

## In practice

OpenStax illustrates the cascade with Tesla: a goal (as of the 2019 edition) of producing 500,000 cars a year required factories, parts, production workers and billions of dollars of funding to be coordinated, along with resources the company did not yet have.[^s1] Kaplan reports that every chief executive in the Balanced Scorecard Hall of Fame, which by 2008 held more than one hundred organisations, personally led the strategy process.[^s4]

## Connections

Strategy execution is the implementation half of **strategic management**.[^s1] **Management by objectives**, **hoshin kanri** and **OKRs** are methods for cascading strategic goals into unit and individual objectives, and Kaplan and Norton's **balanced scorecard** and strategy maps are the measurement system their execution loop relies on.[^s4] Because Hrebiniak's managers ranked resistance to change as the top obstacle, execution **depends on change management**, and he lists **organizational culture** among the levers that must fit the strategy.[^s2]
