# Leadership: Influencing People Toward Shared Goals

Leadership is one of the most studied and least agreed-upon ideas in management. Definitions emphasise different things: some treat it as a set of behaviors (such as giving a group structure so members know how to do a task), others as a matter of personality, persuasion or power, and others as the management of group processes such as forming and communicating a vision. OpenStax's *Principles of Management* settles on a working definition: leadership is an interpersonal influence relationship between two or more people who depend on each other to reach shared goals.[^s1] An effective leader attends to two kinds of group needs at once, *task* needs (progress toward the goal) and *maintenance* needs (members fitting together and sharing norms).[^s1]

## Leadership versus management

The best-known framing in business schools separates leading from managing. Abraham Zaleznik's 1977 *Harvard Business Review* article argued that the two are different in kind: managers approach goals out of necessity and keep daily work running by resolving conflicts, while leaders take a personal, active stance toward goals and energise creative work. He warned that a bureaucratic "managerial mystique" favoring routine and risk avoidance could stifle leader development.[^s4] John Kotter's 1990 HBR article "What Leaders Really Do" built on this by recasting the distinction as two complementary systems: management copes with complexity and brings order, while leadership copes with change. Kotter paired the processes: planning and budgeting versus setting direction; organizing and staffing versus aligning people; controlling and problem solving versus motivating and inspiring. He argued that many US corporations were overmanaged and underled, and rejected the notion that leadership requires charisma.[^s3]

The OpenStax text draws a related line in terms of authority. Managers are usually appointed and get compliance largely through role authority, rewards and sanctions. Leaders often emerge from within a group, depend on followers' acceptance, and draw power from followers' perceptions of their expertise, personality and relationship. The text stresses that good managers often need leadership skills and vice versa, so the roles overlap rather than being redundant.[^s1]

## How the theory developed

**Traits.** Early research asked who leaders are. Decades of trait studies linked drive, self-confidence, honesty and integrity to leader emergence and effectiveness, but traits explained only part of the variance.[^s2] The trait view was revived by a 2002 meta-analysis by Timothy Judge and colleagues, which organised 222 correlations from 73 samples around the Five-Factor Model. Extraversion (mean correlation .31) was the most consistent correlate across settings and across both leader emergence and effectiveness, followed by conscientiousness (.28), openness (.24) and low neuroticism (−.24); together the five traits had a multiple correlation of .48 with leadership.[^s5]

**Behaviors.** From the late 1940s, Ralph Stogdill's team at Ohio State identified two independent leader-behavior dimensions, *consideration* (supportive, relationship-oriented behavior) and *initiating structure* (task-oriented planning and coordination), while University of Michigan researchers independently found closely parallel employee-centered and job-centered dimensions.[^s2] Results were mixed: consideration tended to raise member satisfaction without a clear performance effect, and the payoff to initiating structure varied by organization. The conclusion that no single style is best everywhere pushed the field toward contingency theories.[^s2]

**Situations and relationships.** Later work treats leadership as a process involving the leader, followers, context and consequences, each shaping the others; leader-member exchange theory, for instance, models leadership as a set of dyadic relationships of varying quality.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Beyond the variance that traits and behaviors leave unexplained, a sharper critique concerns attribution. Meindl, Ehrlich and Dukerich's 1985 "romance of leadership" thesis holds that scholars, the media and the public systematically over-credit leaders for organizational success and failure, because leadership offers a reassuring but simplified explanation of complex events. A later *Organization Studies* paper argues that this critical insight seeded post-heroic theories of shared, distributed and follower-centred leadership, yet that many newer approaches, including authentic and spiritual leadership, still romanticise leaders.[^s6] The leader-manager split itself is also contested: the OpenStax treatment emphasises overlap, and Kotter frames the two as complements that an organization must combine rather than as types of people.[^s1][^s3]

## In practice

Kotter illustrated direction-setting with Jan Carlzon at Scandinavian Airlines, who committed the carrier to becoming the best airline for frequent business travelers, a simple focus no rival had pursued, and with Richard Nicolosi at Procter & Gamble, who used category teams and a message that every employee could lead to energize thousands of staff.[^s3] Practitioners therefore tend to develop leadership less as an innate gift and more as a repertoire of direction-setting, alignment and motivation skills used alongside managerial routines.[^s3]

## Connections

Leadership sits under organizational behavior and management theory, and it is the parent of specific models in this graph: the Ohio State studies supplied the task/relationship dimensions that contingency theory of leadership and the Hersey-Blanchard situational leadership model build on.[^s2] It contrasts with management, the planning, organizing and controlling function from which Zaleznik and Kotter distinguished it.[^s1][^s4] Kotter's view of leadership as coping with change links it directly to change management, and transformational leadership extends its vision-and-inspiration strand.[^s3]
