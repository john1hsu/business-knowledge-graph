# Management by Objectives

**Management by objectives (MBO)** is a practice in which managers and the people who report to them agree on a small set of results each person is accountable for, derive those results from the organization's goals, and then use the agreed objectives both to steer day-to-day work and to judge performance. Textbooks describe it simultaneously as a management philosophy, a planning and control technique, and a program for employee involvement.[^s1] Britannica characterizes it as an approach stressing clearly defined objectives for individual managers and lists it among Peter Drucker's contributions.[^s5]

## Origin

Peter Drucker first set out the idea in *The Practice of Management* (1954). His argument was that effective managers concentrate on results rather than activity and delegate by negotiating an agreement on objectives with each subordinate.[^s2] The philosophy behind MBO is usually linked to the human-resource model of management: Douglas McGregor's Theory Y assumption that employees can direct and control their own work, and Maslow's view that work can satisfy higher-order needs such as self-esteem.[^s1]

## How it works

Standard textbook accounts break MBO into a repeating cycle. Organization-wide goals are set from strategy; units translate them into team and department goals; each manager and subordinate then jointly set individual objectives aligned with the level above; the employee builds an action plan; and progress is reviewed periodically, with goals revised before the cycle restarts.[^s2] Three features distinguish MBO from simply assigning targets: objectives are negotiated rather than dictated, employees play a major role in planning how to hit them, and employees design the controls, monitor their own performance and propose corrections when they drift off course.[^s1] OpenStax attributes MBO's expected effect to four ingredients drawn from goal-setting research: specific goals, goals realistic enough to be accepted, joint participation, and regular feedback.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The best-known evidence is Rodgers and Hunter's review of MBO programs: of 70 cases, 68 showed productivity gains and only 2 showed losses, with a mean gain above 40 percent. Gains were largest where top management was emotionally, intellectually and behaviorally committed to MBO and used it themselves, and smallest where leaders paid it lip service, which is why OpenStax concludes MBO should be launched from the top down.[^s1] A separate review of 185 studies found MBO effective only under some conditions, working better over horizons of under two years, in the private sector, and in units far from direct customer contact.[^s1]

Critiques arrived early. In a 1970 *Harvard Business Review* article, psychologist Harry Levinson argued that MBO assumes employees' personal aims already match corporate goals, ratchets up pressure on that assumption, and leaves individual needs out of performance measurement; he proposed starting from the employee's own objectives instead.[^s4] In a 1972 follow-up he added that MBO's emphasis on quantifiable results crowds out qualitative work, treats people as responding mainly to reward and punishment, rarely lets subordinates appraise superiors, and invites sacrificing long-run gains for short-run targets.[^s3] Steven Kerr's 1975 analysis of reward systems that pay for one behavior while hoping for another also took aim at the objective criteria typical of MBO plans.[^s2] Practical failure modes include half-hearted executive support, too little time to learn goal-writing and monitoring, poor integration between individual and unit goals, programs seen as a productivity gimmick, and no sharing of the economic gains with employees.[^s1] Formal MBO programs fell out of favor around the late 1980s and early 1990s, partly because they seemed disconnected from strategy, and Drucker himself later called MBO just one tool rather than a cure for poor management.[^s2]

## In practice

Hewlett-Packard is the classic adopter: the company's archives record that its emphasis on management by objective spread so widely that cufflinks were made bearing the company objectives.[^s6] Elsewhere, MBO survives less as a branded program than as a pattern embedded in later systems: performance-review cycles built around agreed individual objectives, and frameworks that tie those objectives more explicitly to strategy.[^s2]

## Connections

MBO is a form of **performance management** and **strategy execution**, created by Peter Drucker.[^s2] Its theoretical underpinnings run through **goal-setting theory** and McGregor's **Theory Y**.[^s1] The **balanced scorecard**, developed by Kaplan and Norton in 1992, was in part a response to MBO's weak link to strategy, adding explicit ties to vision and measures beyond finance.[^s2] **Objectives and key results (OKRs)** and **hoshin kanri** are descendants or relatives that keep the cascade of objectives while changing cadence, measurement and how goals are negotiated. Britannica contrasts MBO with **total quality management**, which centers on quality across all processes rather than on individual managers' objectives.[^s5]
