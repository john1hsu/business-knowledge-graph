# Ishikawa Diagram

An **Ishikawa diagram**, also called a **fishbone diagram** or **cause-and-effect diagram**, is a chart that helps a team list the many possible causes of a single problem and sort them into categories. The problem statement sits in a box at the "head" of a horizontal arrow (the spine), and cause categories branch off the spine like ribs, so the finished chart looks like a fish skeleton.[^s1] The Asian Productivity Organization credits its invention to Kaoru Ishikawa, a Japanese quality-control statistician, and describes it as a tool for examining effects and their causes systematically.[^s2]

## Origin

Course materials from the University of Alberta date the technique to 1943, when Ishikawa, then at the University of Tokyo, developed it; the diagram took his name and its nickname from its shape.[^s3] It later became one of the "seven basic quality tools" of Japanese quality control, a set that also includes the Pareto chart, histogram, scatter plot and control chart, and it remains a standard element of Kaizen, lean and Six Sigma improvement work.[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## How it works

The American Society for Quality (ASQ) describes a team-based procedure. A group that knows the process agrees on a precise problem statement and writes it at the head; it then draws the main cause categories as branches; members ask "why does this happen?" to brainstorm causes under each category, and keep asking "why" to add sub-causes as smaller branches. A cause may appear under more than one category. When ideas dry up, the team looks at thin branches, then analyses the whole chart to choose which causes to tackle, aiming at root causes rather than symptoms.[^s1]

Manufacturing teams often start from the "6 Ms": materials, machinery, methods, measurement, manpower and "Mother Nature" (environment), with money sometimes added as a seventh; Ishikawa himself encouraged users to rename categories to fit their setting.[^s1] Service and public-sector teams commonly substitute headings such as equipment and supplies, environment, policies and procedures, and people.[^s6] Two construction styles are distinguished: *dispersion analysis*, which starts from broad cause areas and drills down, and *cause enumeration*, which brainstorms freely and groups the results afterwards; a *process-classification* variant instead lays the spine out along the sequence of process steps.[^s3] ASQ also lists variants such as the reverse fishbone, the desired-result fishbone and the cause-and-effect diagram with cards (CEDAC).[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The tool is popular with practitioners. In a 2023 survey of quality professionals at a global manufacturer, Matthew Barsalou found that the cause-and-effect diagram was the classic quality tool used most often during root-cause analysis, well ahead of Pareto and control charts.[^s4] Researchers have also used completed fishbones as data: Suárez-Barraza and Rodríguez-González analysed the diagrams and interviews from improvement projects at 40 Mexican companies and identified recurring "cornerstone" root causes that cut across sectors, while cautioning that a qualitative sample of that size cannot support generalization.[^s5]

Its main weakness is that it organizes hypotheses rather than testing them. A fishbone records what participants believe the causes are; public-health guidance warns that teams can drift into listing symptoms, and that leaving key stakeholders out of the session lets a team assume it understands the problem without confirming it.[^s6] For that reason guidance pairs it with the "5 Whys" to push each branch deeper,[^s6] and practitioners draw on the other classic tools, such as Pareto and control charts, when they need data to judge which candidate causes actually matter.[^s4]

## In practice

A Los Angeles County public-health quality-improvement guide, for example, teaches staff to write the problem at the fish's "mouth", draw branches for equipment, environment, rules and people, brainstorm causes for each, and then apply successive "why" questions to reach underlying causes.[^s6] ASQ recommends the chart chiefly for structuring a team brainstorm; on small or urgent problems it may be the only tool needed to find root causes and guide action, while on complex problems it is combined with action-planning and responsibility matrices that assign an owner to each cause.[^s1]

## Connections

The Ishikawa diagram is a technique of **root-cause analysis** and, more broadly, of **structured problem solving**.[^s1] It is a close relative of the **issue tree**: both break a problem into branches and sub-branches, but the fishbone starts from one observed effect and groups candidate causes under broad headings such as the 6 Ms or the steps of a process.[^s1][^s3] It belongs to the **seven basic quality tools** created or popularized by **Kaoru Ishikawa** within **total quality management**, and is typically followed by a Pareto analysis (the **80/20 rule**) to rank causes and by the **five whys** to deepen each branch.[^s4][^s6] The graph also contrasts it with **fault tree analysis**, a more formal top-down logic method for the same diagnostic task.
