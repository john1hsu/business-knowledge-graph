# MECE Principle

The **MECE principle** (pronounced "mee-see" at McKinsey, though its originator says "meece") is the rule that the items in any grouping should be **mutually exclusive** and **collectively exhaustive**: no item overlaps another, and together the items cover the whole of the thing being divided.[^s1][^s2] It is one of the most widely taught habits of management consulting, applied when breaking a question into sub-questions, dividing a market or cost base into categories, or grouping the supporting points of an argument.[^s2][^s5] A breakdown that violates the first half double-counts; one that violates the second leaves gaps.[^s2]

## Origin

The acronym is credited to Barbara Minto, who joined McKinsey in 1963 after graduating from Harvard Business School and was the firm's first female MBA professional hire.[^s1] While editing consultants' reports in Cleveland and later London, she found herself repeatedly reorganising ideas into a pyramid shape and distilled three rules: a point must summarise the points beneath it, the ideas in a group must be logically of the same kind, and they must sit in a logical order.[^s1] MECE was her shorthand for how such groupings should be formed. Minto does not claim the underlying idea, which she attributes to Aristotle, but says she was the first to abbreviate it and apply it this way.[^s1] The rule was set out in *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* (1985), revised by Minto in 1996 with an added problem-solving section, and spread through her writing courses for McKinsey, Accenture, Bain and other firms after she left McKinsey in 1973.[^s1]

## How it works

The two conditions have a precise counterpart in probability. Events are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur together, so the probability of both is zero and the probability of either is the simple sum of the two; when events also make up the whole sample space, nothing is left uncounted.[^s3] MECE carries that logic into business reasoning, where adding up the parts of a correctly partitioned whole, such as revenue by region, returns the total without double-counting.[^s3][^s5]

In practice the rule is applied through issue trees. A UK government training guide on issue trees advises starting from a carefully chosen primary question, splitting it into sub-questions that do not overlap and together cover all of it, and repeating until each end question is simple enough, ideally yes or no, to answer with a short analysis.[^s2] The guide then has teams test the tree with stakeholders and plan the evidence each end question needs.[^s2] A common consulting example is the profit tree, which splits profit into revenue (price times quantity) and costs (variable and fixed), a breakdown whose branches are exclusive and exhaustive by construction.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

MECE's support rests on logic rather than on controlled studies: its guarantees follow from the same addition rules that make mutually exclusive probabilities sum cleanly.[^s3] Its reach now extends beyond consulting; for instance, a 2024 peer-reviewed study of disruptions in home-care patient journeys coded its interview data into categories designed to be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive to avoid overlap.[^s4]

The principle also has well-known limits. A MECE breakdown is not unique: former McKinsey partner Charles Conn recommends making two or three different cuts of the same problem, because each surfaces different insights.[^s5] Exhaustiveness says nothing about importance, and McKinsey's Hugo Sarrazin warns that branches do not carry equal weight merely because they take up equal space on the page, so trees must still be prioritised toward the levers that matter and can be changed.[^s5] And a perfectly MECE tree is worthless if the root question is wrong, the failure the UK guide calls "barking up the wrong (issue) tree."[^s2] Minto's own account also cautions that the idea itself is old, since she attributes it to Aristotle; the novelty was in the label and its application to business writing.[^s1]

## In practice

The UK guide illustrates the method with a deliberately fictional ministry's target to double the number of offenders captured, split into raising the productivity of existing officers and employing more of them, with the hiring branch further divided into training new staff or recruiting already-trained ones.[^s2] Conn applied the same disaggregation to a long-running salmon-conservation programme funded by the Gordon Moore Foundation, focusing effort on habitat and harvesting branches rather than ocean conditions that no one could change.[^s5]

## Connections

MECE is a core rule of **structured problem solving** and sits inside Minto's **Pyramid Principle**, which is its original home, and it is credited to **Barbara Minto**, who traces it to **Aristotle**.[^s1] It is the construction rule for **issue trees** and shapes **hypothesis-driven problem solving**, in which a tree of exclusive, exhaustive sub-questions is tested branch by branch.[^s2][^s5] It depends on the logic of **mutually exclusive events** in probability, and the same discipline applies to **segmentation**, where every customer should fall into exactly one segment.[^s3]
