# A3 Report

An **A3 report** is a one-page document, named after the international A3 paper size (the metric counterpart of an 11 x 17 inch sheet), on which a Toyota employee lays out a problem, its causes, the proposed countermeasures and a plan to check results.[^s4] Its layout of boxes and simple graphics looks modest, but practitioners stress that the value lies in the rigorous thinking the format imposes and in the mentoring conversations it structures, often called "A3 thinking."[^s1] Sobek and Smalley describe the report as one component of Toyota's Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) management system, and argue that the culture and process around writing and reviewing A3s matter as much as the paper itself.[^s3]

## Origin

The format grew out of Toyota's quality movement. Toyota built total quality control (TQC) routines in the 1960s, and in the 1970s, worried that fast growth was leaving younger staff under-developed, it ran a two-year cross-functional manager-development programme in which A3s served as learning and presentation documents; elements of that programme were later folded into Toyota's standard manager training and annual hoshin planning.[^s6] John Shook, who learned the method after joining Toyota in 1983 and became the company's first American manager (*kacho*) in Japan, popularised it in the West through his book *Managing to Learn* and a 2009 *MIT Sloan Management Review* article.[^s1][^s2] At Toyota, new college hires learned their jobs by working an A3 under a mentor.[^s2]

## How it works

Sobek and Smalley distinguish three main kinds of A3: problem-solving reports, proposal reports and status reports.[^s3] The problem-solving version, as adapted by Sobek and Jimmerson for hospitals, runs down the left side of the sheet through an issue statement, background, current condition (drawn from direct observation and data) and cause analysis, then down the right side through a target condition with countermeasures, an implementation plan with owners and dates, a follow-up plan and, finally, results.[^s4] The steps map onto PDCA: everything through stakeholder consensus and approval is "Plan," implementation is "Do," follow-up is "Check," and any shortfall becomes the next problem to "Act" on.[^s4] Root causes are usually traced with the Five Whys.[^s4]

Sobek and Smalley list seven elements of A3 thinking: a logical thinking process, objectivity, attention to both results and process, synthesis and visualization, alignment, coherence within and consistency across reports, and a systems viewpoint.[^s3] The one-page limit acts as a forcing device: rather than urging people to be focused and selective, it simply leaves no room for anything else, and it obliges authors to gather facts, seek feedback and name owners before acting.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Evidence for A3s is mostly case-based. Sobek and Jimmerson report that in a review of 18 hospital A3 cases, teams that completed every step consistently achieved strong results, while skipping even one step markedly lowered the odds of success; in one intensive-care example, seven incorrect IV infusion rates were documented in the three months before a standardized calculation method, and none in the three months after.[^s4] A peer-reviewed study at Seattle Children's research vivarium describes a 10-step A3 method aligned with PDCA; nine reports were completed in fiscal year 2012, and the two described in detail met measurable goals verified by audits.[^s5] These are descriptive single-site accounts written by the method's advocates, without control groups. Sobek and Jimmerson themselves note practical barriers: scarce staff time, management support that is verbal rather than active, a shortage of trained coaches, and authors who skip direct observation or stakeholder input.[^s4] The recurring warning is that copying the template without the PDCA mindset and coaching yields little.[^s3]

## In practice

Shook recounts how, when Toyota prepared its Georgetown, Kentucky plant in 1986 and needed thousands of pages of Japanese technical documents translated, he used an A3 to study earlier translation work for NUMMI, discovered that translators differed in skill type, and redesigned the work as a three-step process with a timing chart to catch stalled documents, using the sheet to gather feedback and build consensus.[^s2] He notes that working the A3 frequently reveals that the problem first stated was not the real one.[^s2]

## Connections

The A3 is a concrete vehicle for the **PDCA cycle** and sits within **structured problem solving** and the **Toyota Production System**; it draws on **Five Whys** and **root cause analysis** for its cause section.[^s4][^s3] Toyota's Kan-Pro programme linked A3s to **hoshin kanri** planning and to the TQC tradition of **total quality management**.[^s6] Shook contrasts the A3 with **management by objectives**, which sets direction but offers little guidance on how to reach it.[^s2] Its role in coaching ties it to **organizational learning**, and its hospital adaptations connect it to **patient safety** work.[^s1][^s4][^s5]
