# Structured Problem Solving: The Consultant's Stepwise Method

**Structured problem solving** is the practice of working a complex, ambiguous business question through a fixed sequence of explicit stages, so that the team agrees on what it is solving, breaks the question into manageable parts, spends effort only where it matters, and ends with a recommendation decision-makers can act on. Its best-known codification is the seven-step cycle that Charles Conn and Robert McLean, both McKinsey alumni, set out in *Bulletproof Problem Solving* (Wiley); the authors note that these building blocks are seldom taught as a systematic process even though they underpin good decisions in business, policy and personal life.[^s1] McKinsey describes the method as one it teaches new consultants by immersion.[^s2]

## Origin

Conn and McLean say they learned the logic-tree core of the approach at McKinsey, and the firm's own podcast host calls the cycle a very McKinsey process.[^s1][^s2] The approach generalizes the classic rational decision model taught in management courses: recognize that a decision is needed, generate alternatives, analyze them, choose, implement and evaluate.[^s3] What the consulting version adds is heavier emphasis on the early, framing stages and on decomposing the problem so that a team can divide the work.[^s1]

## How it works

The seven steps in Conn and McLean's cycle are:[^s1][^s2]

1. **Define the problem.** State a precise question with clear boundaries, and surface constraints, dependencies and stakeholders' views before any analysis.[^s1][^s2]
2. **Disaggregate.** Break the question into component parts, usually with an issue (logic) tree, such as a profit tree splitting revenue and cost drivers.[^s1][^s2]
3. **Prioritize.** Prune the tree to the branches that most affect the outcome and that the team can actually influence.[^s1][^s2]
4. **Plan the work.** Assign analyses, owners and deadlines along the critical path, matching precision to the stakes.[^s2]
5. **Analyze.** Start with simple estimates and rules of thumb before heavier modelling.[^s1][^s2]
6. **Synthesize.** Combine findings into conclusions rather than presenting the analysis as the answer.[^s1][^s2]
7. **Tell the story and drive action.** Communicate the answer so that the audience is persuaded to act.[^s1][^s2]

The cycle is meant to be iterative, not linear: teams keep a "one-day answer", their best current view of the solution, available at any point and refine it with each pass.[^s1] Conn argues that the structure exists to create room for creativity rather than rigidity, and warns that a long, fixed workplan signals the team has stopped iterating.[^s2] To limit bias inside the team he suggests letting the most junior members speak first and using contrasting teams to force divergent thinking.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Research on strategy supports the method's emphasis on the first step. Baer, Dirks and Nickerson argue that diverse teams facing complex, ill-structured problems tend to formulate them too narrowly, which in turn narrows the search for solutions and the value that can be created; they propose a "collaborative structured inquiry" process to broaden formulation.[^s4] The same theory implies a critique: a sequence of steps does not by itself prevent premature framing if step one is rushed.[^s4] Management textbooks add that analysis can be distorted by heuristics, satisficing and logical fallacies, and that consulting other people reduces bias only when those people hold different biases.[^s3] Practitioners also caution against treating the steps as a rigid, linear checklist, and against running off with half an idea of the problem.[^s2]

## In practice

Consulting teams use the cycle to scope engagements, divide workstreams by tree branch and report progress against an evolving answer.[^s2] Conn and McLean apply it to public as well as business questions, for example bounding a Sydney transport case to passenger capacity and examining US school outcomes.[^s1] Toyota's **A3 report** is a comparable discipline from lean management: a compact, fixed template that walks through background, current condition, goal, root-cause analysis, countermeasures, an action plan and follow-up, used chiefly to mentor people in scientific problem-solving.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Structured problem solving is a branch of managerial **decision-making** and the working method of **management consulting**, exemplified by **McKinsey & Company**.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It **has as parts** the **issue tree**, used to disaggregate the question, and **hypothesis-driven problem solving**, the "one-day answer" logic that steers which branches to test; trees are built under the **MECE principle**, and the prioritization step often leans on the **80/20 rule**.[^s1][^s2] It **depends on problem formulation**, the stage that research identifies as the main source of failure.[^s4] Its final step is the domain of the **Pyramid Principle**; diagnostic branches overlap with **root cause analysis**; group techniques such as the **nominal group technique** support the defining and prioritizing steps; and the A3 report is a lean **alternative** with the same stage logic.[^s1][^s5]
