# Stakeholder Analysis

**Stakeholder analysis** is a set of tools for finding out who has a stake in a decision, project or policy, what those actors want, how much influence and resources they can bring to bear, and how they relate to one another.[^s3] Where **stakeholder theory** is a normative and descriptive account of what a firm is and whom it should serve, stakeholder analysis is the practitioner's working method: a structured way of listing, sorting and mapping the people and groups whose support or opposition will decide whether a plan succeeds.[^s1][^s4] It is used in corporate strategy, public management, health policy, resource management and consulting, and the term means somewhat different things across those fields.[^s4]

## Origins

The starting point is R. Edward Freeman's broad 1984 definition of a stakeholder as any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of an organization's objectives.[^s1] That definition is so inclusive that it gives managers little guidance on whom to prioritise. Ronald Mitchell, Bradley Agle and Donna Wood addressed the gap in a 1997 *Academy of Management Review* article that proposed a theory of stakeholder identification and **salience**, which they defined as the degree to which managers give priority to competing stakeholder claims.[^s1] In parallel, the approach developed in development studies and health policy, where analysts used it to understand the politics of reform, and in natural resource management, where it became a basis for deciding who should participate in decisions.[^s3][^s4]

## How it works

Most applications follow three broad moves, which Mark Reed and colleagues formalised as a typology: identifying stakeholders, differentiating and categorising them, and investigating the relationships among them.[^s4] Zsuzsa Varvasovszky and Ruairí Brugha's practical guide adds preliminaries: define the purpose, time focus and level of the analysis, and decide whether one analyst or a team will do it.[^s3] Identification typically starts from documents and then "snowballs", with each interviewee asked to name other important actors; data come from semi-structured interviews, document review and group discussions, and are presented as matrices, position maps, force-field diagrams and network maps.[^s3]

Two categorisation tools dominate. The **salience model** scores each stakeholder on three attributes: *power* (the ability to make the firm do something it otherwise would not), *legitimacy* (a socially accepted claim) and *urgency* (how far the claim calls for immediate attention, combining time sensitivity and criticality).[^s1] Stakeholders holding one attribute are "latent" (dormant, discretionary or demanding), those with two are "expectant" (dominant, dangerous or dependent), and those with all three are "definitive" and command the most managerial attention.[^s1] Mitchell, Agle and Wood stress that these attributes are variable and socially constructed, so a stakeholder's salience can rise or fall as an issue evolves.[^s1]

The **power versus interest grid**, which John Bryson attributes to Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann, places stakeholders in four cells: *players* (high interest, high power), *subjects* (high interest, low power), *context setters* (high power, low interest) and the *crowd* (low on both).[^s2] Bryson's 2004 review catalogues fifteen techniques in four groups, from organising participation through developing proposals to planning implementation, including stakeholder influence diagrams, participation planning matrices and support-versus-opposition grids.[^s2] The output feeds engagement strategy: Varvasovszky and Brugha suggest collaborating with "mixed-blessing" actors, defending against non-supportive ones and monitoring marginal ones.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The case for the practice rests partly on failure studies. Bryson cites Paul Nutt's analysis of 400 strategic decisions, about half of which failed, with much of the failure traced to decision makers who ignored stakeholders' interests and information.[^s2] Yet Bryson also concedes that research has not established which techniques work best in which conditions, and that there is little systematic evidence that stakeholder analyses reliably improve outcomes.[^s2] Methodological critiques are substantial: an analysis is a snapshot that quickly dates in fast-moving politics; respondents' recollections are coloured by later events; stakeholders may support a policy in public while opposing it privately; analysts bring their own values and can become stakeholders themselves; and premature scoring can lend false precision.[^s3] Deciding who counts as "key" is itself a political and ethical choice, and Bryson warns that the techniques can be turned to ends that do not serve the common good.[^s2]

## In practice

Varvasovszky and Brugha illustrate the method with a study of alcohol policy in Hungary, in which snowball interviewing surfaced almost 30 stakeholders and their positions were mapped to understand the prospects for policy change.[^s3] In business and consulting, the same steps precede reorganisations, market entries and major system implementations: the team lists affected groups, plots them on a power-interest grid, and assigns engagement tactics and owners to each cell before the change is announced.[^s2]

## Connections

Stakeholder analysis is an application of **stakeholder theory**, translating its broad definition of the stakeholder into a prioritisation method; it is therefore `influencedBy` that node.[^s1] The **stakeholder salience model** and the **power-interest grid** are its two best-known component tools (`hasPart`).[^s1][^s2] It sits under **structured problem solving** and **strategic planning**, where Bryson situates it as a core step in public and nonprofit strategy work.[^s2] It is a routine input to **change management**, since mapping supporters and opponents precedes any engagement plan, and it connects to **public value**, because public managers use it to build the coalitions that legitimise programmes.[^s2] Its data-collection stage can borrow structured expert-elicitation tools such as the **Delphi method** to quantify stakeholder positions.[^s3]
