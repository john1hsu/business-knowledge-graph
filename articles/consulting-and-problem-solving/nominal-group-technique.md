# Nominal Group Technique

The **nominal group technique (NGT)** is a facilitated meeting format for generating and prioritising ideas. Its name reflects the fact that participants work as a group "in name only" during the critical idea-generation phase: they sit together but write their ideas silently and independently before any discussion begins. Andre Delbecq and Andrew Van de Ven devised the method around 1971, blending the idea volume of individual brainstorming with the commitment-building of a face-to-face meeting.[^s3] Their 1971 article in the *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* set it within a wider program-planning model that brings clients, experts, resource controllers and staff into structured meetings at different stages of program development.[^s1]

## How it works

Descriptions differ slightly, but the core sequence is consistent. A facilitator poses one clearly worded question; members silently write down as many responses as they can; each person then contributes one idea at a time in round-robin order while the facilitator records every item in view of the group; the group discusses the items only to clarify and evaluate them; and members finally vote or rank privately, with the group result computed arithmetically, sometimes followed by discussion and a second round of ranking.[^s3][^s4] A typical session involves a face-to-face meeting of roughly 5 to 12 participants, which distinguishes it from the remote, multi-round Delphi method.[^s4]

Practitioner guides add practical rules: label items with letters rather than numbers so the list order is not mistaken for a ranking, get the group's consent before rewording anyone's statement, and use reverse scoring (the top item gets the most points) so that items nobody ranked score zero. Variants include ranking only the top half of a long list and repeating, or weighted voting in which members spread a fixed budget of points, such as 100, across options.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Early experimental work favoured the technique for idea generation. In a 1974 *Academy of Management Journal* study on a fact-finding problem with no known answer, nominal and Delphi groups were about equally productive, and both clearly outperformed conventional interacting groups in the number of ideas generated; participant satisfaction was also measured.[^s2]

The picture for judgement accuracy is more mixed. A 1978 University of Illinois experiment using estimation tasks found nominal groups less accurate and more variable than Delphi groups or averaged individuals, even though nominal-group members rated their process the most satisfying and felt freest to contribute. The authors noted that confidence rose while accuracy did not, hinting at groupthink-like effects, and called for further research.[^s3] Other recurring limitations are that NGT usually involves fewer people than a Delphi study and that dominant participants can still sway the group during the discussion stage.[^s4]

A 2021 scoping review of 57 health-research studies found large variation in how NGT is run, from 1 to 41 groups of 2 to 30 participants each, and identified 30 design decision points; it urged researchers to justify their choices in advance to improve validity and transparency.[^s5] Practice has also moved online: in pilot searches for a 2023 review protocol, about 35% of pandemic-era studies examined used a virtual NGT component, against about 6% beforehand, with trade-offs such as easier access but weaker non-verbal cues and less interaction.[^s4]

## In practice

NGT is widely used in public-sector quality improvement and health research to set priorities. The Minnesota Department of Health, for example, recommends it when a team must rank issues quickly, when members are unused to participating, or when a topic is sensitive, because private written input gives quieter members an equal voice and makes disagreement visible for discussion.[^s6]

## Connections

NGT is a form of **group decision-making** and a building block of **structured problem solving**: it supplies a prioritised list of issues that can then be framed as hypotheses for **hypothesis-driven problem solving**.[^s1][^s6] It was created by **Andre Delbecq** and **Andrew Van de Ven** and introduced in their 1971 program-planning article.[^s1][^s3] It is an **alternative to the Delphi method**, which keeps participants apart across survey rounds, and to unstructured **brainstorming** in interacting groups, against which it was benchmarked.[^s2][^s4] By separating silent generation from discussion and using private votes, it aims to **mitigate groupthink** and the influence of dominant members, though evidence that it fully does so is limited.[^s3][^s4] Its emphasis on equal voice links it to **psychological safety** in teams.[^s6]
