# Management Consulting: The Business of Expert Advice to Client Organizations

**Management consulting** is the sale of expert advice on how an organization should be run. Consultants, whom the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics classifies as *management analysts*, recommend ways to improve a client's efficiency and help managers raise profits by lowering costs and increasing revenue.[^s1] A typical engagement involves gathering information about the problem, interviewing staff, analyzing financial and operating data, developing alternative solutions and presenting recommendations to management.[^s1] The field spans independent practitioners, elite strategy partnerships, accounting-linked advisory arms and in-house internal consulting groups.

## Origin and history

Business historian Christopher McKenna of Oxford's Saïd Business School argues that, although consulting has never become a formally recognized profession, the leading firms, such as McKinsey & Company and Booz Allen & Hamilton, have advised and reshaped the world's largest organizations since the 1920s.[^s2] In his account the elite firms grew rapidly after U.S. regulatory changes of the 1930s, with endorsers of the book crediting the Glass-Steagall banking legislation of 1933 with helping spawn the industry.[^s2][^s3] McKenna frames the early decades as a struggle with accountants for jurisdiction over advisory work, and devotes chapters to consultants "creating the contractor state" inside the U.S. federal government and reshaping nonprofits.[^s3] By the 1950s consultants were remaking large corporations, nonprofits and the state, and after 1960 they exported the American management model abroad, popularizing ideas such as decentralization and "corporate culture".[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

Consulting firms sell knowledge and judgment rather than a physical product; McKenna's opening chapter builds a theory of the industry around what he calls "economies of knowledge".[^s2][^s3] The analyst's job, as BLS describes it, is to collect information, interview staff, analyze data and present recommendations, so the core product is a diagnosis plus a proposed course of action.[^s1] The occupation is large: BLS counted about 1,077,100 management analyst jobs in 2025, with median pay of $101,860 in May 2025, and projects 10% employment growth from 2025 to 2035, much faster than the average for all occupations.[^s1] About 14% of analysts were self-employed, and the largest employers were professional, scientific and technical services firms (33%), government other than state and local education and hospitals (18%) and finance and insurance (13%).[^s1] Entry typically requires a bachelor's degree; some employers prefer an MBA, and certification is optional.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Consulting has attracted sustained criticism. Andrew Sturdy, writing in *Management Learning* in 2009, catalogued the recurring popular critiques: consultants portrayed as job-cutting rationalizers, as carriers of imported ideology, as wasters of resources who merely legitimize plans managers had already made, as promoters of insubstantial fads, as unaccountable actors who blur responsibility (including in the public sector), and as self-interested figures who take credit for clients' ideas.[^s4] Sturdy suggests consultants partly serve as a scapegoat for management in general, and notes that academic research rarely tests these popular claims directly.[^s4] McKenna likewise records criticism of consultants' role in the Enron crisis and in lending legitimacy to corporate blunders.[^s2]

A second debate concerns disruption. In 2013 Clayton Christensen and colleagues predicted that new, more modular models would catch some consulting incumbents off guard.[^s5] A decade later, Julian Birkinshaw and Dan Lancefield found that this had not happened: drawing on 25 executive interviews and case studies of about a dozen would-be disruptors, they report that challengers either failed or settled into niches, and none displaced the leaders.[^s5][^s6] Vault's top-10 consulting ranking changed only twice between 2010 and 2020 (excluding mergers), with McKinsey, Bain and BCG holding the top three positions.[^s5] They attribute this resilience to incumbents' ability to diagnose potential causes of disruption and adapt quickly.[^s5][^s6]

## In practice

Clients engage consultants for strategy reviews, operational improvement, organizational redesign, technology implementation and change programs. Governments are major buyers: government accounts for roughly 18% of management analyst employment, and McKenna traces how federal agencies came to rely on outside consultants from the mid-twentieth century.[^s1][^s3] McKenna's case study of Lukens Steel illustrates how consulting advice can alter a company's organization and direction.[^s3]

## Connections

Management consulting is a branch of **professional services** and is **exemplified by** firms such as **McKinsey & Company** and **Booz Allen Hamilton**, both central to McKenna's history.[^s2][^s3] Its working method is **structured problem solving**, and **benchmarking** against other organizations is a natural companion to the information-gathering and data-analysis steps of an engagement.[^s1] The field was **influenced by scientific management**, whose efficiency mandate survives in the BLS job description.[^s1] Its business model **depends on information asymmetry**: clients buy knowledge they lack, which is also why critics question its accountability.[^s4] Consultants helped codify and sell **organizational culture** as a management idea; their public-sector work ties the industry to **public procurement**.[^s2][^s3] The **Enron scandal** is a prominent episode in debates over consultants' legitimacy.[^s2]
