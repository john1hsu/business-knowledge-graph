# Kepner-Tregoe Method

The Kepner-Tregoe (KT) method is a family of four structured thinking processes for working through management problems: Situation Appraisal ("what's going on?"), Problem Analysis ("why did this happen?"), Decision Analysis ("which course of action should we take?") and Potential Problem (and Opportunity) Analysis ("what lies ahead?").[^s1] Its best-known component, the "is / is not" matrix used in Problem Analysis, is catalogued by the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality as a quality-improvement tool for locating the cause of a problem by contrasting where it occurs with where it plausibly could occur but does not.[^s2]

## Origin

Charles H. Kepner and Benjamin B. Tregoe developed the approach after studying breakdowns in decision making at the U.S. Air Force's Strategic Air Command in the late 1950s. They concluded that good decisions depended more on a disciplined sequence of reasoning than on a person's rank or career history, then studied the thinking habits of effective business managers and codified them as repeatable processes. They founded their firm in 1958 and published *The Rational Manager* in 1965; *The New Rational Manager* is the updated edition.[^s1] By 1962 the U.S. Department of Agriculture was already sending GS-12 to GS-15 managers to a five-day Kepner-Tregoe course in problem analysis and decision-making, citing a 1960 *Harvard Business Review* article by the two authors as background.[^s3]

## How it works

**Problem Analysis** treats a problem as a deviation from expected performance whose cause is unknown. The analyst states the deviation precisely, then specifies it along four dimensions (what, where, when and extent), recording for each what the problem *is* and what it *is not* even though it reasonably could be. The distinctions between the two columns, and the changes associated with those distinctions, generate candidate causes; each candidate is tested against every fact in the specification, and the most probable cause is the one that explains them all before it is verified in practice.[^s1] AHRQ's version adds a "who is involved" row, stresses recording who without assigning blame, and recommends an experiment to confirm the cause where possible.[^s2] The book's lead case, a leaking soybean-oil filter, was traced to a thinner, square-cornered gasket from a new supplier installed during maintenance, a cause the authors argue the method would have exposed within hours.[^s1]

**Decision Analysis** begins with a clear decision statement, because a badly framed question leads to the wrong choice. Objectives are split into MUSTs, which are mandatory and measurable so that any alternative failing one is dropped, and WANTs, which are weighted for importance (typically 1 to 10). Each surviving alternative is scored against each WANT, scores are multiplied by weights and summed, and the highest total becomes a tentative choice. Adverse consequences of that choice are then rated separately for probability and seriousness; the authors warn against multiplying the two ratings, which can hide a grave but unlikely risk.[^s1]

**Potential Problem Analysis** protects a chosen plan: state the action, list what could go wrong one item at a time, identify likely causes, take preventive actions against those causes, prepare contingent actions to limit damage, and set triggers that tell the team when to act. Preventive spending should be proportionate to the probability and seriousness of the threat.[^s1] **Situation Appraisal** sits in front of the other three, sorting and prioritising a tangled situation so that each concern is routed to the right process.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence base is thinner than the method's long commercial history suggests. The 1963 USDA evaluation of 53 trainees found that the share who could name specific on-the-job applications rose from 26% before the course to 46% after, while those seeing no use fell from 34% to 14%; but the data were self-reported questionnaires from a small sample, and the author called the study preliminary pending a follow-up.[^s3] Writing in *California Management Review* in 1978, James Guyot questioned evaluations of Kepner-Tregoe and similar packaged programs, arguing that they relied largely on participants' self-reports, which reflect intentions more than behaviour, and that they rarely showed benefits large enough to matter.[^s4] AHRQ's main practical caveat is that the matrix is time-consuming, though it requires little training.[^s2] The authors themselves concede that Problem Analysis fails without the key facts, so it suits deviations with observable evidence better than ill-defined strategic puzzles.[^s1]

## In practice

In health care, AHRQ positions the matrix for quality problems at any stage of improvement work, for both discovering and resolving a problem, and also lists it as an aid to brainstorming causes.[^s2] The book's case histories span plant maintenance, mining, distribution and insurance, including a records-centralisation decision that saved more than $400,000 in its first year.[^s1] Kepner-Tregoe's own materials report that NASA mission-control managers used the processes during the Apollo era, and include Apollo XIII among their case histories.[^s1]

## Connections

Kepner-Tregoe is a form of **structured problem solving** and a prescriptive approach to **decision making**. Its Problem Analysis is a cause-finding technique within the **root-cause analysis** toolkit, an evidence-driven alternative to the brainstormed cause categories of an **Ishikawa diagram** and to the iterative questioning of **five whys**.[^s2] Its test-every-cause-against-the-facts logic parallels **hypothesis-driven problem solving**, and the method is credited to Charles Kepner and Benjamin Tregoe through *The Rational Manager* (1965).[^s1]
