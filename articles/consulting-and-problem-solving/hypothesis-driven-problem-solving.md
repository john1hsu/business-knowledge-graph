# Hypothesis-Driven Problem Solving

**Hypothesis-driven problem solving** is the practice of proposing a plausible answer to a business question at the start of the work and then using data to validate or refute it, rather than collecting every available fact and hoping an answer emerges. Jeanne Liedtka's 2006 Darden School teaching note describes it as an application of the scientific method built around generating alternative hypotheses and testing them, and illustrates it with examples from strategy consulting, medicine and architecture.[^s1] The approach is associated above all with strategy consulting, and since the late 2010s strategy and entrepreneurship researchers have argued that managers and founders should work the same way.[^s1][^s4]

## How it works

Liedtka frames the method as a repeating two-step cycle. Hypothesis **generation** is a creative act that asks "what if…?"; hypothesis **testing** is analytical and takes an "if…, then…" form, specifying which data would support or undermine the idea.[^s1] Running the cycle repeatedly lets a team sharpen its hypotheses while still leaving room to explore new ones.[^s1] In practice a team typically:

1. Frames the key question, often the first step of a broader frame–diagnose–solve–implement sequence such as the one Arnaud Chevallier teaches at IMD.[^s6]
2. Breaks the question into sub-questions with an issue map or tree, and attaches a candidate answer to each branch.[^s6]
3. Lists the analyses that would prove each hypothesis wrong, runs only those, and drops or revises hypotheses that fail.[^s1]
4. Synthesises the surviving hypotheses into a recommendation.

The efficiency argument is that the hypothesis tells the team which analyses matter, so effort goes where the answer is likely to be found instead of being spread evenly across all possible data.[^s1]

## Evidence

The strongest evidence comes from entrepreneurship rather than consulting. In a randomized controlled trial run by Arnaldo Camuffo, Alessandro Cordova and Alfonso Gambardella, 116 Italian startups were tracked over roughly a year across 16 data points; both arms received ten sessions of general training on gathering market feedback, but only the treated group was taught to build theories of how their idea would perform and to test hypotheses rigorously.[^s2] The treated firms performed better early on and were more likely to pivot to a different idea, though they did not drop out less often than the control group.[^s2] Bocconi University's summary of the study (accepted for publication in *Management Science*, with Chiara Spina added as a co-author) reports that the scientific approach helped founders read market signals with less bias, reducing false positives and false negatives.[^s3]

At the level of strategy theory, Teppo Felin and Todd Zenger's **theory-based view** (2017) argues that distinctive strategies originate in managers' firm-specific theories and hypotheses about which assets to acquire and how value will be created, using examples such as Apple, Disney, Walmart and Airbnb.[^s4]

## Critiques and pitfalls

The main risk is **confirmation bias**. Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review defines it as seeking or interpreting evidence in ways partial to existing beliefs, and shows that people testing a favoured hypothesis tend to choose tests that can only return "yes", as in Peter Wason's 2-4-6 rule-discovery task.[^s5] Nickerson also describes how early opinions colour later evidence, how contrary data gets explained away, and how searching only for support breeds overconfidence — all hazards for a team that has committed early to an answer.[^s5] Liedtka's emphasis on alternative hypotheses and on tests capable of refutation is the standard remedy.[^s1] A second limitation is that a strong early hypothesis can narrow the search, which is why Chevallier puts a diagnostic step, asking why the problem exists, before solutions are chosen.[^s6]

## In practice

An illustrative consulting case, following the generate-and-test cycle Liedtka describes: asked why a client's profits have fallen, a team might hypothesise that a new low-cost entrant has taken share in one segment, then check segment-level share and price data within days; if share is stable, the hypothesis is discarded and the next branch of the tree is tested.[^s1][^s6] The same discipline in a startup means treating a business idea as a set of falsifiable claims about customers and testing each before scaling, which is the behaviour the Bocconi trial trained.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

Hypothesis-driven problem solving is a branch of **structured problem solving** and is **influenced by the scientific method**.[^s1] It **depends on the issue tree**, which supplies the branches that hypotheses are attached to, and on the **MECE principle** that keeps those branches clean; and its conclusions are commonly communicated answer-first, as in the **Pyramid Principle**. It **contrasts with trial-and-error search**, the heuristic the Camuffo trial's control group relied on,[^s2] and it is **related to confirmation bias**, the error its emphasis on falsification is meant to guard against.[^s5] In strategy research the **theory-based view** offers a parallel, firm-level version of the same idea,[^s4] and it shares its test-and-pivot logic with the **lean startup**.[^s3]
