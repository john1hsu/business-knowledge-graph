# Wicked Problems: Rittel and Webber's Tame-versus-Wicked Distinction

A **wicked problem** is an ill-defined problem whose very formulation is contested, whose resolution rests on political and value judgments rather than technical proof, and which is never finally solved, only re-solved. Design theorist Horst Rittel and urban planner Melvin Webber introduced the term in "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning", published in *Policy Sciences* in 1973.[^s1] They contrasted wicked problems with **tame** problems such as solving an equation or achieving checkmate, where the goal is clear and one can tell when the problem has been solved.[^s1]

## Origin and the ten properties

Rittel and Webber wrote at a moment when planning professions, modeled on science and engineering, had organized themselves around efficiency, an approach that worked while problems were easy to define and broadly agreed upon.[^s1] They argued that this "first-generation" systems approach, which moves in phases from understanding the problem to gathering data to devising a solution, breaks down for open societal problems because the problem cannot be understood apart from some idea of its solution; operations-research tools only become useful once a problem has already been tamed.[^s1] They listed ten distinguishing properties:[^s1]

1. There is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem.
2. There is no stopping rule; work ends when time, money or patience runs out.
3. Solutions are better or worse, not true or false.
4. There is no immediate or ultimate test of a solution.
5. Every solution is a one-shot operation, so trial and error is costly.
6. The set of possible solutions cannot be exhaustively described.
7. Every wicked problem is essentially unique.
8. Every wicked problem can be seen as a symptom of another problem.
9. The chosen explanation of the problem determines the resolution.
10. The planner has no right to be wrong, because actions affect real people.[^s1]

As an alternative, they proposed a "second-generation" approach: an argumentative process in which participants gradually build a shared picture of the problem and its resolution through critical judgment.[^s1]

## How organizations apply it

The concept spread from planning into public administration and management. A 2007 discussion paper from the Australian Public Service Commission described wicked policy problems as having multiple causes, conflicting goals and stakeholders who disagree about what the problem is, and often requiring changes in citizen behavior; its examples were climate change, obesity, Indigenous disadvantage and land degradation.[^s3] It recommended holistic rather than linear thinking, collaboration across agency boundaries, stakeholder engagement, iterative trials and tolerance of uncertainty, and warned against "taming" a problem by tackling only a tractable sub-problem.[^s3] It also cited tobacco control as an issue that moved from wicked toward tame as evidence and consensus accumulated.[^s3]

Keith Grint of Warwick Business School links problem types to modes of authority: tame problems call for **management** using known techniques, critical problems (crises) call for decisive command, and wicked problems call for **leadership** that asks questions and mobilizes the collective rather than supplying answers.[^s4] He recommends "clumsy solutions": pragmatic, reversible experiments sustained long enough to evaluate, a just culture that reports errors openly, and drawing on those closest to the problem.[^s4]

In strategy, John Camillus argued in *Harvard Business Review* in 2008 that many strategic issues are wicked: they have innumerable causes, are hard to describe and have no right answer, and conventional planning methods such as gathering more data or decomposing the issue can make them worse.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The main scholarly critique is that the binary split between tame and wicked is too blunt. Brian Head and John Alford, writing in *Administration & Society* in 2015, argue that wickedness is a matter of degree along two dimensions, complexity and uncertainty on one hand and divergence of stakeholder values on the other, and that more of either makes a problem more wicked.[^s2] They also judge Rittel and Webber's pessimism too strong, holding that adaptive management and democratic debate can yield learning and partial progress.[^s2] They note that how a problem is framed, as a knowledge gap or as a values conflict, already implies a preferred remedy, so responses should be treated as provisional, and that hierarchy, silos and New Public Management contracting make wicked problems harder to address.[^s2] Their recommended responses are collaboration, systems thinking and causal mapping, adaptive and collaborative leadership, and more flexible budgets and performance measures.[^s2]

## Connections

Wicked problems sit under **structured problem solving** and **problem formulation** as the class of problems where formulation is itself the hard part.[^s1] The concept **contrasts with** **hypothesis-driven problem solving** and **operations research**, which presume a stable question that can be decomposed and tested; Rittel and Webber held that such tools apply only after taming.[^s1] Rittel's work shaped **design thinking**, which treats problem definition as provisional, and the stakeholder-heavy prescriptions of the APSC and Head and Alford tie it to **stakeholder analysis** and **public value**.[^s2][^s3] Camillus connects it to **strategic planning**, and its emphasis on unstable futures relates it to **scenario planning**; Grint's **clumsy solutions** build directly on the concept.[^s4][^s5]
