# Benchmarking

**Benchmarking** is the disciplined comparison of an organization's products, services and practices against those of strong competitors or recognized leaders, with the aim of setting performance goals grounded in demonstrated best practice rather than in internal guesswork.[^s1] Xerox, the company most associated with the method, framed it as a continuous search for the best practices that produce superior performance, and stressed that it is an ongoing process rather than a one-off study.[^s1][^s3] In consulting and problem solving it is a standard way to size a performance gap, test whether a target is realistic, and find proven solutions to borrow.

## Origin

The practice is credited to Robert C. Camp, whose book on the subject is often regarded as its founding text; it set out a ten-step process Camp developed at Xerox and linked that work to the company's recovery after the difficult period around 1979.[^s2] Camp's book also described applications beyond Xerox, most famously at the retailer L.L. Bean, and it helped carry benchmarking into the Total Quality Management movement around 1989.[^s2][^s3] A 1996 government laboratory guide lists Xerox, L.L. Bean, IBM, Boeing and Johnson & Johnson among early adopters, and portrays Xerox as the leader in the method by that point.[^s3]

## Types

The Cambridge Institute for Manufacturing distinguishes four classic types by the choice of comparison partner.[^s1]

- **Internal** benchmarking compares similar operations in different units of the same organization.[^s1]
- **Competitive** benchmarking compares against the best direct rivals.[^s1]
- **Functional** benchmarking compares a function, such as logistics or billing, with firms in other industries that run similar processes.[^s1]
- **Generic process** benchmarking studies any organization known for an exemplary or innovative way of doing a piece of work.[^s1]

Moving outward from internal to generic partners raises the potential for breakthrough ideas, but also the difficulty of getting access and of translating what is seen.[^s1][^s3]

## How it works

Camp's ten steps, as reflected in his book's structure, run from deciding what to benchmark and choosing partners, through data collection and measuring the current gap, to projecting future performance, communicating findings, setting functional goals, building action plans, implementing and monitoring, and finally recalibrating the benchmarks.[^s2] Practitioners often compress this into four phases: an internal assessment that sets a baseline; planning and data gathering, including the selection of partners such as award winners; analysis of the gap and of what drives the leader's results; and implementation with monitoring and repetition of the cycle.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Benchmarking is widely accepted as a core tool for continuous quality improvement, and its literature grew large enough by the early 2000s to need a formal classification scheme.[^s5] Yet a later review in the *International Journal of Management Reviews* found that this literature is mostly descriptive or prescriptive, with few critical studies, and that long-term effects and the views of different stakeholders are largely neglected; the authors called for stronger theory and proper impact evaluation.[^s4] Practitioner guidance flags recurring failure modes: visiting partners before the organization is ready, treating comparison alone as benchmarking, scoping too broadly, skipping internal benchmarks, and weak management commitment to the resulting changes.[^s3]

The sharpest strategic critique comes from Michael Porter, who argued that benchmarking improves operational effectiveness but cannot by itself create a sustainable advantage, because best practices diffuse quickly and are easily imitated.[^s6] In his account, heavy benchmarking makes rivals look more alike, a process he called competitive convergence, and so managers must keep operational improvement separate from choosing a distinctive strategic position.[^s6]

## In practice

Xerox is the canonical example: its benchmarking programme is credited with reviving the company's competitiveness, and adopters such as L.L. Bean, IBM and Boeing followed.[^s2][^s3] In a typical engagement, a team first benchmarks internally to establish a common baseline measure, then selects external partners, often award winners, and studies how they achieve their results; guidance warns that comparison of numbers without this process learning does not by itself drive change.[^s3]

## Connections

Benchmarking sits under **total quality management** and **performance management**, the movements that adopted it as a standard tool.[^s3][^s5] It is credited to **Robert C. Camp** and exemplified by **Xerox**.[^s2] It relates to **operational effectiveness** and contrasts with **strategic positioning** in Porter's framework, which treats the two as separate agendas.[^s6] Award programmes such as the **Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award** steer benchmarkers toward exemplary partners, and lean systems such as the **Toyota Production System** are frequent targets of process benchmarking.[^s3]
