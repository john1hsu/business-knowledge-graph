# Design Thinking: Human-Centered Innovation Through Empathy, Ideation and Prototyping

**Design thinking** is an approach to problem solving and innovation that borrows the working methods of professional designers and applies them to products, services, processes and strategy. In the formulation that made it popular with managers, IDEO chief executive Tim Brown described it in a June 2008 *Harvard Business Review* article as using a designer's sensibility to match what people need with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business strategy can turn into customer value.[^s1] Its defining habits are direct observation of users, reframing of ill-defined problems, and fast, cheap prototyping that lets teams learn before they commit.[^s1][^s3]

## Origins

The intellectual roots are older than the management fad. In a 1992 *Design Issues* article, design theorist Richard Buchanan argued that design problems are "wicked" in the sense developed by Horst Rittel in the 1960s: they have no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and solutions that are better or worse rather than true or false.[^s2] Rittel proposed this view as an alternative to a linear model that separates problem definition from problem solution, and Buchanan concluded that design lacks a fixed subject matter, so designers must construct the problem anew from each situation.[^s2] He described design as a new liberal art of technological culture that integrates knowledge from many fields toward practical ends.[^s2]

The managerial version grew out of Stanford University and the design firm IDEO. Stanford mechanical engineering professor David Kelley, a School of Engineering alumnus, championed design thinking and became the first director of the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design (the "d.school"), which opened in fall 2005 with a curriculum built on observing users and then prototyping iteratively.[^s5] Brown's 2008 article traced a precursor to Thomas Edison, who, Brown argued, designed a whole electricity system around the light bulb and organized team-based experimentation.[^s1]

## How it works

Brown described projects as passing through three overlapping "spaces" rather than a linear sequence of steps, often looping back as teams learn:[^s1]

1. **Inspiration**: understanding the problem or opportunity, its constraints, and how people actually behave, largely through field observation.[^s1]
2. **Ideation**: generating, developing and testing ideas through brainstorming, sketching and rough prototypes.[^s1]
3. **Implementation**: charting a path to market and building the capabilities needed to deliver.[^s1]

He associated the method with traits such as empathy, integrative thinking, optimism, experimentalism and cross-disciplinary collaboration.[^s1] Other authors break the process into finer stages. Darden School professor Jeanne Liedtka, for example, describes seven steps running from immersion in the customer's experience through sense-making, alignment, idea emergence, articulating the assumptions behind each idea, low-fidelity "pre-experience" tests, and learning through real-world experiments.[^s4] Darden presents the structure as a way to push teams past status-quo thinking and confirmation bias, and argues that a clear sequence of steps gives participants the psychological safety to act.[^s4]

A systematic review by Pietro Micheli and colleagues, which screened 525 articles and retained 104 for analysis, distilled ten recurring attributes: abductive reasoning, visualization, blending analysis with intuition, creativity, a holistic (gestalt) view, interdisciplinary collaboration, iteration and experimentation, a focus on ill-structured problems, tolerance for ambiguity and failure, and user-centeredness.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Evidence for performance effects is thin relative to the method's popularity. Micheli and co-authors found that most of the literature is descriptive or normative, often resting on single cases or firm anecdotes, with few rigorous quantitative studies, so its impact on organizational performance remains largely unproven.[^s3] They also warned that the term is so loosely used that it risks "construct collapse" into its component tools, noted scholarly concern about overblown claims, and observed that critics see a drift from a designerly way of thinking toward a linear, checklist process.[^s3] A further unresolved debate is whether design thinking is a professional designer's skill that non-designers cannot easily acquire, or a transferable mindset.[^s3] Darden's practitioner material, by contrast, likens its potential impact to that of total quality management in the 1980s but offers no data to support the comparison.[^s4]

## In practice

Brown's article gives several cases. At Kaiser Permanente, nurses spent roughly 45 minutes piecing together patient information at each shift change; a cross-functional team prototyped a bedside handoff with simple software in about a week, and the interval before a nurse first saw a patient was more than halved.[^s1] For bicycle-component maker Shimano, IDEO's observation that many Americans found cycling intimidating led to "Coasting" bikes with automatic shifting and backpedal brakes, which Trek, Raleigh and Giant produced.[^s1] Bank of America's "Keep the Change" program, which rounds up debit-card purchases into savings, signed up 2.5 million customers in less than a year, according to Brown (figures as reported in 2008).[^s1] Consultancies and internal innovation units now use the method mainly at the "fuzzy front end" of innovation, where Micheli and colleagues suggest its fit is strongest and most worth studying.[^s3]

## Connections

Design thinking is a branch of **structured problem solving** and a tool of **innovation management**, but it differs from consulting's **hypothesis-driven problem solving**: rather than decomposing a defined question and testing an answer with data, it treats the problem definition itself as provisional and reframes it through user observation, reflecting the **wicked problems** tradition Buchanan applied to design and its reliance on **abductive reasoning**.[^s2][^s3] **Prototyping** and **brainstorming** are its core component techniques, and **IDEO**, led by **Tim Brown**, is its best-known exemplar.[^s1] Micheli and colleagues flag its unclear boundaries with **agile software development** and the **lean startup**, which share iteration and experimentation; the **customer development** and **jobs-to-be-done** approaches share its insistence on understanding customers before building.[^s3] Liedtka's account ties its effectiveness to **psychological safety** within teams.[^s4]
