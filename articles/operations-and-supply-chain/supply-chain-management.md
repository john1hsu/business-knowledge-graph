# Supply Chain Management: Integrating Sourcing, Production and Distribution Across Firms

Supply chain management (SCM) is the discipline of coordinating the flows of goods, services, finance and information that run from raw-material suppliers through manufacturers and distributors to the final customer. In the most widely cited scholarly definition, Mentzer and colleagues describe a supply chain as three or more organizations directly linked by such upstream and downstream flows, and SCM as the systemic, strategic coordination of traditional business functions both inside a company and across the businesses in its chain, aimed at improving the long-term performance of each firm and of the chain as a whole.[^s1] A key point of that definition is that a supply chain exists whether or not anyone manages it; SCM is the deliberate act of managing it.[^s1]

## Origin

The term is credited to Keith Oliver, a consultant at Booz Allen Hamilton. Working in the late 1970s with clients such as Philips, Oliver's team first called its approach "integrated inventory management"; a Philips manager suggested "total supply chain management" instead, and the phrase first reached print in a 1982 *Financial Times* article about Oliver.[^s2] The core idea was to run production, sales, marketing, finance and distribution as though they formed a single entity, rather than letting each function optimize its own inventory and capacity decisions.[^s3] Academic SCM grew out of the logistics literature and older systems thinking, notably Jay Forrester's 1958 work on industrial dynamics, and the term gained wide academic currency in the 1990s.[^s1] Textbooks such as Handfield and Nichols's 1998 *Introduction to Supply Chain Management* helped turn it into a field with its own departments, journals and software industry.[^s3]

## How it works

Mentzer et al. distinguish a *supply chain orientation*, a single firm's recognition that flows across its suppliers and customers have strategic implications, from SCM proper, which requires several directly connected firms sharing that orientation and acting on it together.[^s1] They also classify chains by reach: a *direct* chain covers a focal firm with one supplier and one customer; an *extended* chain adds suppliers' suppliers and customers' customers; and an *ultimate* chain spans every organization from the original source to the final consumer.[^s1] The expected payoff is lower total cost, higher customer value and satisfaction, and with them competitive advantage and profitability.[^s1]

Laseter and Oliver argue that three principles have stayed constant since the field began: set supply chain policy strategically, weigh trade-offs holistically across functions and firms, and support decisions with cross-functional systems.[^s2] Over time the scope widened from coordinating a company's own finished-goods flows to strategic sourcing and supplier involvement in product development from the late 1990s.[^s2]

## Matching the chain to the product

Marshall Fisher's 1997 *Harvard Business Review* article added an influential design rule. *Functional* products such as soup or paper towels have stable demand and thin margins and need a physically efficient chain that minimizes production, transport and storage cost. *Innovative* products such as fashion apparel have volatile demand, short lives and high margins and need a market-responsive chain built on short lead times and buffers of inventory or capacity.[^s4] Many failures, he argued, come from mismatches, most often running innovative products through cost-focused chains.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Fisher's examples show the gains from coordination. Campbell Soup's continuous-replenishment program, in which retailers reported demand electronically every day, roughly halved retailer inventories from about four weeks to two, and sales through participating retailers grew twice as fast as elsewhere.[^s4] Skiwear maker Sport Obermeyer gathered early orders from large retailers and cut forecast error from as much as 200% to about 10%.[^s4] Fisher also cited an estimate that poor coordination among U.S. food-industry partners wasted about $30 billion a year in the 1990s.[^s4]

Critics note that SCM often underdelivers. Laseter and Oliver, writing in 2003, attributed disappointing results to firms compromising on trade-offs instead of applying the field's principles.[^s2] Cost-driven practices can also create fragility: the U.S. government's June 2021 100-day supply chain reviews blamed a focus on low-cost labor, just-in-time production, consolidation and short-term returns for vulnerabilities in semiconductors, large-capacity batteries, critical minerals and pharmaceuticals, noting for example that Taiwan then produced 92% of leading-edge logic chips.[^s5]

## Connections

SCM extends its broader field, **operations management**, beyond the boundary of a single firm, and it **has as parts** activities such as **inventory management** and **logistics**, from whose literature it grew.[^s1] The term was **created by** **Keith Oliver**,[^s2] and the field was **influenced by** Forrester's **industrial dynamics**.[^s1] The **bullwhip effect** is a classic coordination failure inside supply chains, while **just-in-time** and **enterprise resource planning** are common operating tools; **transaction cost economics** and the **business ecosystem** idea frame which activities a firm keeps in-house and how it orchestrates partners. SCM is **exemplified by** Campbell Soup's continuous replenishment program.[^s4] The 2021 U.S. reviews link the field to the growing concern with **supply chain resilience**.[^s5]
