# W. Edwards Deming

William Edwards Deming (1900-1993) was an American statistician whose teaching on statistical quality control shaped Japan's postwar manufacturing and, decades later, the quality movement in the United States. Trained as a physicist, with a Yale PhD awarded in 1928, he argued that most problems in an organization come from the system that management designs rather than from the workers inside it, so the remedy is to change the system instead of blaming people.[^s1] Columbia's Deming Center captures the idea with his musical metaphor: when a performance goes badly, rewrite the score rather than fault the singers.[^s1]

## Life and career

Born in Sioux City, Iowa, Deming studied engineering at the University of Wyoming and physics and mathematics at the University of Colorado. In 1927 he met Walter Shewhart, the inventor of the statistical control chart, and in 1937 he sponsored Shewhart's lectures on statistical quality control at the USDA Graduate School.[^s2] From 1939 to 1946 he advised the U.S. Census Bureau, bringing statistical sampling into the 1940 census, and in 1946 he helped form the American Society for Quality Control and became a statistics professor at New York University's business school.[^s2][^s1]

In 1947 U.S. occupation authorities sent him to Japan to help prepare its 1951 census. In 1950 the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE) invited him to lecture on statistical methods for quality improvement, and within a decade he had trained roughly 20,000 Japanese engineers in basic statistical methods.[^s1][^s2] Japan created the Deming Prize in 1951; it is awarded annually to organizations that pass a demanding quality-control examination.[^s3][^s1]

Deming remained little known at home until NBC broadcast the documentary "If Japan Can, Why Can't We?" on 24 June 1980.[^s2] He then consulted widely, including for Ford, which the Deming Institute credits with his help in making the Taurus the best-selling U.S. car by 1992.[^s2] President Reagan awarded him the National Medal of Technology in 1987; he joined Columbia Business School in 1988 and died in Washington, DC, in December 1993.[^s2][^s1]

## Key ideas

**The Fourteen Points.** Deming's management prescriptions appeared in *Quality, Productivity, and Competitive Position* and *Out of the Crisis* (Columbia dates them 1982 and 1986; the Deming Institute dates *Out of the Crisis* to 1982).[^s1][^s2] They include constancy of purpose toward improvement, ceasing dependence on mass inspection, not awarding business on price alone, driving out fear, breaking down barriers between departments, and eliminating slogans, numerical quotas and work standards for the workforce, all led by daily top-management commitment.[^s1]

**The System of Profound Knowledge.** In *The New Economics* (1993) Deming framed the theory behind the points as a system of profound knowledge, the lens through which managers should understand their organizations.[^s2]

**The improvement cycle.** Deming built on a 1939 Shewhart cycle linking specification, production and inspection to the scientific method. At his 1950 JUSE seminar he presented a design-make-sell-redesign wheel, which Japanese executives recast as Plan-Do-Check-Act.[^s4] Deming disliked that version: he renamed the loop Plan-Do-Study-Act in 1993 and in a 1990 letter called PDCA a corruption of the idea, worrying that "check" implies holding back rather than learning.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

For years, support for the Deming management method rested mostly on anecdotes and case studies, partly because no explicit theory existed to test. Anderson and colleagues articulated one and, using plant-level data from the University of Minnesota and Iowa State World-Class Manufacturing project, found path-analytic support for several of its proposed relationships while also proposing new ones.[^s5] A 2005 retest on more than 100 U.S. and Canadian manufacturing and service firms strongly supported the model except for one construct, employee fulfilment, and concluded that continuous-improvement drives launched without visionary leadership, cooperation, learning and process management are likely to fail.[^s6] These studies test a theory distilled from the method rather than each of the fourteen points in isolation, so prescriptions such as abolishing numerical quotas and work standards for the workforce remain more contested than the broad emphasis on leadership and process management.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Deming's methods of counting defects, tracing their causes and tracking the effect of corrections underpin total quality management, his broader parent node; Six Sigma's focus on reducing process variation is a later descendant of the same statistical tradition.[^s3] His method rests on Walter Shewhart's statistical process control and control chart, and the PDCA/PDSA cycle descends from his 1950 reworking of Shewhart's loop.[^s2][^s4] The Deming Prize carries his name into Japanese quality practice.[^s3] He is linked to Joseph Juran as a fellow quality pioneer, and his call to eliminate numerical goals and quotas for the workforce sets him against goal-setting traditions in performance management and Peter Drucker's management by objectives.[^s1]
