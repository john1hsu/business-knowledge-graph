# Toyota Production System (TPS)

The Toyota Production System is the way Toyota Motor Corporation organizes manufacturing so that it can deliver vehicles quickly, cheaply and with high quality by thoroughly eliminating waste and shortening lead times.[^s1] It is usually drawn as a house with two pillars, just-in-time and jidoka, standing on a foundation of standardized work and kaizen (continuous improvement) carried out through plan-do-check-act cycles.[^s5] Western researchers studying Toyota later coined the term "lean production" to describe the approach, so TPS is the concrete system from which the generic lean model was abstracted.[^s5][^s3]

## Origin

The two pillars come from two generations of the Toyoda family. Toyota traces jidoka to Sakichi Toyoda's automatic looms, which stopped themselves when a thread broke; the company cites a weft-breakage stopping device of 1896 and the Type-G automatic loom of 1924.[^s1] Just-in-time is credited to Kiichiro Toyoda, who installed chain-conveyor flow production on a loom assembly line in 1927 and carried the method to body production at the Koromo plant in 1938.[^s1] After the Second World War, production chief Taiichi Ohno, backed by Eiji Toyoda, turned these ideas into a working system, starting in machining in the 1950s and 1960s and extending it to suppliers in the 1960s and 1970s.[^s1][^s5]

## How it works

Just-in-time means making only what is needed, when it is needed and in the amount needed, with every plant and process synchronized in continuous flow; Toyota frames this as producing to customer need, never letting goods or information stall, and producing at the pace of sales.[^s1] Synchronization matters because a car contains more than 30,000 parts, many made by partner firms.[^s1] Pull signals such as kanban cards, small-lot production and production leveling keep material moving only in response to downstream consumption.[^s3][^s5] Jidoka, glossed as automation with a human touch, means a machine or worker stops the line as soon as an abnormality appears, so defects never travel downstream; andon lights and stop cords make the problem visible.[^s1] Work is first refined by hand to remove *muda* (waste), *mura* (unevenness) and *muri* (overburden) before abnormality detection is built into equipment.[^s1]

Steven Spear and Kent Bowen, after a four-year study of more than 40 plants, argued that TPS was never formally written down and that its real engine is tacit: four rules governing how work is specified, how people connect, how production paths are designed and how improvement is learned.[^s2] In their account each specification functions as a hypothesis, so the apparent rigidity of scripted work is precisely what lets problems surface and be fixed, making the system flexible.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Michael Cusumano's comparisons found Toyota producing more than twice as many vehicles per worker as GM, Ford and Chrysler in the 1970s and 1980s after adjusting for vertical integration, and turning inventory 36 times in 1983 against roughly 11 to 12 for the U.S. makers.[^s3] Because Toyota was by then more capital-intensive per vehicle, the gap was attributed to the system as a whole rather than to cheap labor or low investment.[^s3]

Critics stress the limits. In 1994 Cusumano noted that in Japan just-in-time deliveries had worsened road congestion, while firms faced blue-collar labor shortages, excessive product variety and overstretched suppliers.[^s4] Later scholars warn that zero buffer inventory is dangerous in volatile environments, that equating TPS with efficiency neglects unevenness, overburden and cultural prerequisites, and that copying tools without the underlying thinking tends to produce unsustainable results.[^s3]

## In practice

Outside Japan, serious adoption began with NUMMI, the 1984 Toyota-General Motors joint venture in California, and spread widely after the 1990 MIT study *The Machine That Changed the World* popularized it.[^s5] The tools have since been carried into product development, services and software, though scholars caution that "lean" is often used loosely in those settings.[^s3][^s5]

## Connections

TPS was built by Taiichi Ohno and is exemplified by Toyota Motor Corporation; its parts include just-in-time, jidoka and the pull-signal practice kanban.[^s1][^s3] It sits within operations management and was defined against Ford-style mass production, whose large batches and buffers it treats as waste.[^s5] Its generalization, lean manufacturing, is what most firms adopt today, while its emphasis on building in quality parallels total quality management and its standardized work echoes scientific management, with the difference that workers themselves improve the standard.[^s2][^s3]
