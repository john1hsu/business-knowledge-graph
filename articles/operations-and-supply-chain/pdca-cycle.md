# PDCA Cycle: Plan-Do-Check-Act Iterative Improvement

The **PDCA cycle** (plan-do-check-act), also called the Deming cycle or Deming wheel and, in its later form, **PDSA** (plan-do-study-act), is a four-step loop for improving work through repeated small experiments. OpenStax's management textbook defines it as a planning model aimed at continuous improvement by building organizational learning into planning.[^s4] The Lean Enterprise Institute calls it the foundation of continuous improvement, or kaizen: a team plans a change against a stable baseline, implements it, evaluates the result, and then either standardizes the gain or starts the loop again.[^s5]

## The four steps

ISO's guidance on its 2015 quality-management standard gives a compact version of the steps:[^s3]

- **Plan:** set objectives and design the processes and changes needed to reach them.[^s3]
- **Do:** carry out what was planned, ideally as a limited trial that is scaled up over later cycles.[^s3][^s2]
- **Check (or Study):** monitor and measure the results and compare them with the objectives and predictions.[^s3][^s1]
- **Act:** standardize the change if it worked, or adjust and run another cycle if it did not.[^s5]

The cycle's value lies in repetition. Each turn produces knowledge that sharpens the next plan, so a chain of small, linked cycles is meant to ratchet performance upward while each new standard becomes the baseline for further improvement.[^s5][^s1]

## Origin and evolution

Ronald Moen and Clifford Norman, of the consulting group Associates in Process Improvement, traced the lineage in a 2010 *Quality Progress* article.[^s1] Walter Shewhart's 1939 lectures, which Deming edited, laid out specification, production and inspection as a straight-line sequence that Shewhart then recast as a cycle.[^s1] At an eight-day seminar sponsored by the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers (JUSE) in 1950, Deming presented a wheel of design, production, sales and research, rotating continuously with quality as its aim.[^s1] Japanese executives turned this wheel into plan-do-check-act around 1951; Moen and Norman note that no individual has claimed authorship of that revision, while the Lean Enterprise Institute attributes it to JUSE.[^s1][^s5] Kaoru Ishikawa later broadened the plan step to include goal setting and added training to the do step.[^s1]

Deming himself disliked the Japanese wording. He argued that "check" suggests holding back, and in a 1990 letter to Moen he urged that the method be called PDSA rather than the "corruption PDCA."[^s1] By 1993 he labelled it the Shewhart Cycle for Learning and Improvement.[^s1] In 1991 Moen, Thomas Nolan and Lloyd Provost added an explicit prediction to the plan step so that the study step compares outcomes with expectations, turning each cycle into a test of a theory; in 1994 Langley and colleagues wrapped PDSA in the Model for Improvement's three questions about aims, measures and candidate changes.[^s1] The two names now coexist, and many practitioners treat them as interchangeable.[^s5]

## In practice

The cycle is embedded in management systems and tools. ISO's guidance presents PDCA, together with risk-based thinking, as the way to manage and improve individual processes and the quality management system as a whole under ISO 9001:2015.[^s3] At Toyota, John Shook explains, the one-page **A3 report** is structured around PDCA: it records the problem and its causes, proposes countermeasures and an action plan, and schedules a follow-up review, and new hires learned their jobs by working through mentored A3s.[^s6] The Lean Enterprise Institute also links PDCA to the Build-Measure-Learn loop of the lean startup, which it treats as essentially the same logic applied to new ventures.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The main criticism is that PDCA is easy to name and hard to do well. A 2014 systematic review in *BMJ Quality & Safety* by Taylor and colleagues examined 73 peer-reviewed healthcare improvement studies that reported using the method.[^s2] Only 19.2% used linked iterative cycles, 8.5% of those analysed in detail stated an explicit prediction, 14.9% collected data at monthly or more frequent intervals, and just 2 of 73 met all five fidelity principles the authors assessed.[^s2] Studies labelled PDSA used iterative cycles more often than those labelled PDCA (44% versus 13.6%).[^s2] The authors warned against treating the method as a black box when judging whether quality improvement works, and noted that the review did not test whether faithful use yields better outcomes.[^s2] Deming's own objection, that "check" invites inspection rather than learning, is a conceptual version of the same concern.[^s1]

## Connections

PDCA is the core loop of **continuous improvement** and **total quality management**, and the practical engine of **kaizen**.[^s5][^s4] It is **influenced by Walter Shewhart** and **W. Edwards Deming**, whose cycles it adapts.[^s1] The **A3 report** is its documentary form at Toyota, and **root-cause analysis** supplies the plan step's diagnosis.[^s6] Its emphasis on prediction and study ties it to **organizational learning**, and its build-measure-learn echo links it to the **lean startup**.[^s1][^s5] It **applies to ISO 9001**, which builds the cycle into its process approach.[^s3]
