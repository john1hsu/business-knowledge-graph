# Operations Management

Operations management (OM) is the management of the conversion process by which a firm turns inputs (natural resources, raw materials, people and capital) into the products and services it sells.[^s1] Encyclopaedia Britannica treats "production management" and "operations management" as the same field, defined as planning and controlling industrial processes so they run smoothly at the required level of output, in services as well as manufacturing.[^s3] Because operations managers oversee inventories, wages and benefits, they control a large share of a typical firm's assets.[^s1]

## Origin

The field's analytical roots lie in the work measurement and time-study methods of Frederick W. Taylor and Frank and Lillian Gilbreth, which also gave rise to incentive pay schemes tied to output.[^s3] Later, quantitative tools from industrial engineering, operations research and systems engineering, increasingly run on computers, became the field's core toolkit.[^s3] A strategic turn came in 1969, when Harvard professor Wickham Skinner argued in *Harvard Business Review* that manufacturing was a neglected "missing link" in corporate strategy; he stressed that production systems force trade-offs and identified decision areas where those trade-offs must be resolved, laying the groundwork for manufacturing strategy and, later, operations strategy.[^s6]

## How it works

OpenStax describes three ongoing, overlapping responsibilities: production planning (where, when and how to produce, including site selection and acquiring resources); production control (quality, cost, scheduling and day-to-day running of a plant or service facility); and improvement (finding more efficient ways to produce).[^s1] Planning runs on three horizons: long-term decisions of three to five years about what to make, how much and where; medium-term decisions of about two years on layout and resources; and short-term plans within a year covering specific production and materials.[^s1]

Britannica frames the manager's levers as the "five M's" of men, machines, methods, materials and money, and highlights the problem of smoothing output against fluctuating demand. Inventory control is central because too little stock causes delays while too much ties up capital; at some firms inventory exceeds half of total assets.[^s3]

Process choice is a core design decision. Mass production uses standardized, repetitive steps to keep costs low; mass customization adds individual features late in a standard process; and customization builds each order to specification. Continuous processes run for days or months on high-volume, low-variety output, while intermittent processes use short batch runs for low-volume, high-variety work.[^s2] In services the transformation is less visible, the customer often participates in it, and most processes are intermittent because each "run" may be a single appointment.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

OM's practical stakes became clear in the 1980s, when many U.S. automotive, steel and electronics firms lost customers to foreign rivals whose production systems delivered better quality at the price customers wanted.[^s1] Academic OM has been criticized for drifting away from practice: a content analysis of 593 articles in five OM journals found research from 1977 to 2003 moving away from rationalistic, axiomatic models of artificial reconstructions of reality toward interpretive work grounded in observation, a shift the authors argue should make findings more relevant to managers.[^s4] A second debate concerns Skinner's trade-off logic: focused plants built around a few competitive priorities are widely held to outperform unfocused ones, yet managers facing constant disruption increasingly fear that focus undermines agility.[^s5]

## In practice

A study co-authored by Skinner traced Hewlett-Packard's Herrenberg server plant in Germany from roughly 1990 to 2005 as it moved from an R&D-linked innovation factory, to a mass-production operational-excellence factory, to a solutions factory that outsourced low-end parts. The authors found that annual manufacturing policy reviews kept operations aligned with corporate strategy, and that shifting the workforce, not the equipment, was the hardest part of each change of focus.[^s5]

## Connections

OM is a branch of general **management** and grew out of **scientific management** and **operations research**.[^s3] Its parts include **production planning and control**, **inventory management** and **operations strategy**, the strategic layer Skinner opened up.[^s1][^s6] Improvement philosophies such as **lean manufacturing** and **total quality management**, and planning systems such as **enterprise resource planning**, are tools within the field, while **supply chain management** extends its logic across firm boundaries. The **focused factory** applies operations strategy at plant level,[^s5] and **services marketing** shares OM's concern with customer participation in service delivery.[^s2]
