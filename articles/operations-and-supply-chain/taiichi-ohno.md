# Taiichi Ohno

Taiichi Ohno (1912-1990) was the Toyota engineer and executive who conceived and put into operation the Toyota Production System (TPS), a way of making high-quality goods as quickly and efficiently as possible.[^s2] He was born in Dairen (Dalian), Manchuria, in February 1912, graduated in mechanical engineering from Nagoya Technical High School in 1932 and joined Toyoda Spinning and Weaving before moving to Toyota Motor Company in 1943.[^s1] Operations scholars regard him as the central figure in the Toyota story, and his core idea as the relentless reduction of waste (muda) in production.[^s4]

## Career at Toyota

Ohno rose from the shop floor. In 1946 he was put in charge of machine shops at the Koromo Plant, where he began developing what became TPS, and from 1948 he worked with Eiji Toyoda on raising productivity and cutting wasted resources.[^s2] He became machine shop manager in 1949, a director in 1954, managing director in 1964, senior managing director in 1970 and executive vice president in 1975, retiring from Toyota's executive ranks in 1978 while remaining chairman of Toyoda Spinning and Weaving.[^s1] He stayed on as a consultant until 1982 and spent much of his career personally training Toyota employees and suppliers in the system.[^s2]

## Contributions

Ohno did not invent just-in-time from nothing. Toyota's own corporate history credits founder Kiichiro Toyoda with proposing just-in-time production when the Koromo Plant opened, an effort suspended under wartime materials rationing from 1939.[^s3] The concept was only fully realized around 1954, when Toyota adopted a "supermarket" method in which each later process withdraws what it needs from the earlier one, with kanban cards as the control tool.[^s3] Ohno's achievement was to turn these ideas into a working system: TPS rests on two pillars, just-in-time (producing only what is needed, when it is needed) and jidoka (automation with a human touch, in which machines stop and signal when they detect a defect).[^s2]

His specific practices included small-lot production, a pull system controlled by kanban, and having workers inspect their own work, which shortened feedback loops so that quality and productivity improved together.[^s4] He framed the goal of TPS as getting maximum value output for minimum input, and he distinguished three forms of loss: muda (waste), muri (overburden) and mura (unevenness).[^s4] He is also credited with classifying seven categories of waste: overproduction, waiting, transporting, unnecessary processing, inventory, motion and defects or rework.[^s5]

## Writings and recognition

Ohno published *Toyota Production System* in Japanese in May 1978; the 1988 Productivity Press translation, subtitled *Beyond Large-Scale Production*, was the first English edition and became a founding text of lean thinking.[^s1] The book mixes his reflections with an account of how Toyota implemented the system, and includes a chapter on "the true intention of the Ford system", positioning TPS as a reinterpretation of mass production rather than a simple rejection of it.[^s1] He also wrote *Workplace Management* (1983) and *Just-in-Time for Today and Tomorrow* (1986), received Japan's Medal with Blue Ribbon (1973) and the Order of the Rising Sun (1982), and was inducted into the Automotive Hall of Fame in 2022.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Ohno never used the word "lean"; it was coined by John Krafcik in 1988, and later writers generalized TPS into lean as a way of creating value with fewer resources through continuous experimentation.[^s4] Researchers caution that this generalization can drift from Ohno's intent. Michael Cusumano argues that Ohno would have warned against pushing practices to extremes, for example running zero buffer inventory in a volatile environment, which can be dangerous.[^s4] Toyota's own history also complicates single-inventor accounts: it credits Kiichiro Toyoda with the just-in-time slogan and records machine-shop head Jiro Iwaoka limiting materials to each day's planned output at Koromo, a practice he likened to the later kanban system.[^s3]

## In practice

Ohno's influence is visible wherever managers run pull systems, visual signals and waste walks. The seven-wastes list is used by productivity bodies across Asia as a standard diagnostic, and later practitioners have added wastes such as untapped human potential.[^s5] In manufacturing his ideas spread from Japanese automakers to production systems worldwide.[^s2]

## Connections

Ohno **influenced** (and largely built) the **Toyota Production System** and its pull-signalling tool **kanban**, and he operationalized **just-in-time**, building on **Kiichiro Toyoda**, by whom he was **influenced**.[^s3][^s2] His **seven wastes** taxonomy is a standard diagnostic of **lean manufacturing**, his broader node.[^s5] His chapter on the Ford system links him to **Henry Ford**, the **Ford Model T** and **mass production**, which TPS reinterpreted at **Toyota Motor Corporation**.[^s1]
