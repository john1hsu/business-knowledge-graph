# Joseph M. Juran

Joseph Moses Juran (1904-2008) was an electrical engineer, lawyer and consultant whose central argument was that quality is not an inspection activity but a core management process, to be planned, controlled and improved with the same discipline firms apply to finance. Alongside W. Edwards Deming he is one of the founding figures of modern quality management, and his vocabulary (the "vital few", the cost of poor quality, the Quality Trilogy) still structures how operations managers talk about defects and waste.[^s1][^s3]

## Life and career

Juran was born in Romania on 24 December 1904 and grew up in Minneapolis after his family emigrated around 1912. He earned a B.S. in electrical engineering from the University of Minnesota in 1924 and, later, a law degree from Loyola University in 1935.[^s1] In 1926 he joined the inspection statistical department of Western Electric, the manufacturing arm of the Bell System, rising to head its Quality Inspection Results Division in 1929 and later serving as corporate industrial engineer at Western Electric/AT&T headquarters in New York, for a total of 17 years with the company.[^s1] During the Second World War he was lent to the Lend-Lease Administration for what was meant to be six weeks and stayed four years, an experience that shaped his 1944 book on bureaucracy.[^s1]

From 1945 he worked as an independent consultant and was a founding member of the American Society for Quality Control. He first published the *Quality Control Handbook* in 1951; it went through many editions and became a standard reference. He incorporated the Juran Institute in 1979 and stepped down as its chairman in 1987.[^s1] His honours included Japan's Order of the Sacred Treasure (1981), the U.S. National Medal of Technology (1992) and election to the National Academy of Engineering (1988). He died on 28 February 2008, aged 103.[^s1]

## Key ideas

**The vital few.** On a 1941 visit to General Motors, Juran encountered Vilfredo Pareto's work on the concentration of wealth and generalised it: in most quality problems a small number of causes account for most of the defects.[^s1] He named this the Pareto principle and coined the contrast between the "vital few" and the "trivial many", also called the "useful many", arguing that the pattern extends far beyond economics.[^s6] He later acknowledged that crediting Pareto was a misattribution, since the general principle was his own extension.[^s1]

**Managing for quality in Japan.** In summer 1954 Juran made his first visit to Japan at the invitation of Keidanren and the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE), whose QC Research Group, including Kaoru Ishikawa, translated his materials and organised his lectures.[^s5] Juran later contrasted his role with Deming's: Deming's 1950 lectures taught statistical methods, while his 1954 lectures addressed the managerial hierarchy and quality as a management task. He insisted that Japanese managers, not American advisers, were the real authors of Japan's quality revolution, with the visitors supplying only a jump-start.[^s3]

**The Quality Trilogy.** In a 1986 *Quality Progress* article Juran proposed a new direction for managing quality in response to the Western quality crisis.[^s2] The framework divides quality management into three linked processes: quality planning (designing products and processes able to meet customer needs and avoid the cost of poor quality), quality control (monitoring performance and taking corrective action to hold it stable) and quality improvement (raising performance to new, better levels over time).[^s4] Juran also distinguished "little q", quality of manufactured goods and factory processes, from "Big Q", which extends quality management to services and all business processes, including cycle time and productivity.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Juran's own historical account is candid about mixed results. He reports that he warned Western audiences in 1966 that Japan would reach world quality leadership within two decades, a forecast largely ignored until Japanese firms overtook Western leaders by the mid-1970s.[^s3] When U.S. companies did respond in the 1980s, many relied on exhortation, project-by-project improvement or statistical process control, and the results were often disappointing; Juran concluded that personal leadership by upper managers was the deciding variable.[^s3] The "Pareto" label itself is contested: Juran later learned the principle had been wrongly attributed to Pareto.[^s1] Encyclopedia treatments present the trilogy less as a tested theory than as a learning framework that balances stable operations with the search for improvement.[^s4]

## In practice

Pareto charts that rank defect causes by frequency or cost, annual project-by-project improvement programmes, and cost-of-poor-quality estimates in management reports all descend from Juran's work, and Juran Institute training built on the trilogy was widely used by the 1990s.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Juran sits under total quality management, which he influenced alongside Deming and Ishikawa; his contrast with W. Edwards Deming (management process versus statistical system thinking) is one he drew himself in his history of the field.[^s3] His naming of the vital-few pattern makes him the source of the 80/20 rule, while the three-process Juran Trilogy he formulated is treated as a framework node of its own; his project-by-project improvement videos became a basic model for continuous-improvement programmes, the lineage in which the Six Sigma node records his influence.[^s4][^s6][^s3] His work with JUSE ties him to Kaoru Ishikawa, and his emphasis on control links him to statistical process control and the cost of poor quality.[^s5][^s3]
