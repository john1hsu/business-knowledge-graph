# Lean Manufacturing

Lean manufacturing (or lean production) is a set of principles and methods for finding and removing activity that adds no value for the customer, whether in making a product or delivering a service.[^s5] The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes it as a blend of management system, organizational culture and continuous-improvement tools rather than a single technique.[^s5] Operations researchers Rachna Shah and Peter Ward define it more formally as an integrated socio-technical system whose main aim is to eliminate waste by reducing variability coming from suppliers, customers and internal processes.[^s3]

## Origin

The word "lean" was coined to name a system that already existed. John Krafcik, a researcher in MIT's International Motor Vehicle Program, introduced it in a 1988 *Sloan Management Review* article to describe Toyota's approach, which used far fewer resources than conventional plants.[^s2] James Womack recalls that in 1987 his MIT team needed a label for the Toyota system, and Krafcik proposed asking what it could do that mass production could not, which produced the term.[^s1] Womack, Daniel Jones and Daniel Roos popularized it in the 1990 book *The Machine That Changed the World*, which defined lean largely by outcomes: doing more with less of everything than a mass producer.[^s2] Womack and Jones followed with *Lean Thinking* in 1996, recasting the findings as a roadmap that any organization could follow, and the Lean Enterprise Institute was founded in 1997 to spread it.[^s1]

## How it works

*Lean Thinking* condensed the approach into five principles: specify value as the customer defines it; map the value stream for each product and challenge the wasteful steps; make value-creating steps flow without interruption; let the customer pull production where flow is not yet possible; and pursue perfection by repeatedly cutting steps, time and information.[^s1] In practice firms apply these through a toolbox that includes value stream mapping, multi-day kaizen improvement events, 5S workplace organization, cellular layouts, just-in-time production and built-in quality.[^s5] Shah and Ward's survey of 280 usable responses from U.S. manufacturers found that lean practice clusters into ten measurable dimensions, spanning supplier feedback, supplier just-in-time delivery, supplier development, customer involvement, pull, continuous flow, setup-time reduction, total productive maintenance, statistical process control and employee involvement.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The founding evidence came from automobile assembly. The MIT study's pilot comparison reported a lean plant nearly twice as productive as a mass-production plant with about three times the quality, and the main study of more than 90 assemblers concluded that lean could be reproduced anywhere.[^s2] Later work is more qualified. A study of 351 pharmaceutical plants in 37 countries, surveyed between 2004 and 2019, found that "hard" lean practices such as just-in-time, total quality management and total productive maintenance improved operational performance, while "soft" practices had no direct effect and mattered only as moderators: empowerment and visual management strengthened the gains, whereas heavy goal setting and rigid standardization appeared to weaken them.[^s4]

Critics have challenged the original study's methodological robustness and interpretive validity, its claims of universal applicability and financial payoff, and its effects on workers; one account argues that the "lean" label itself was chosen partly to avoid offending the study's sponsors.[^s2] Scholars also note that lean lacks a single accepted definition and is used to mean different things at different times.[^s2] Shah and Ward document the resulting confusion: lean, just-in-time, the Toyota Production System and pull production are often used interchangeably, and studies measure lean either as a philosophy or as a set of observable shop-floor practices.[^s3]

## In practice

Womack cites Pratt & Whitney, which in the late 1990s replaced an automated turbine-blade grinding system installed in 1988 with lean cells, cutting throughput time from ten days to 75 minutes.[^s1] Lean has also spread beyond factories: the EPA reports that state environmental agencies have used lean methods to speed permitting and other administrative processes, and argues that lean's continuous-improvement culture naturally supports pollution prevention.[^s5]

## Connections

Lean is a branch of operations management whose direct ancestor is the Toyota Production System, from which it was abstracted, and it was introduced to a wide audience in *The Machine That Changed the World*.[^s2][^s1] It defines itself against mass production, the large-batch model whose buffers and inventories it treats as waste, and just-in-time flow is one of its core components.[^s1][^s3] It overlaps with total quality management through statistical process control and built-in quality, and it is often paired with Six Sigma, as the EPA's combined guide to the two methods reflects.[^s3][^s5]
