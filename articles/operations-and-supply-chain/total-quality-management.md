# Total Quality Management

**Total Quality Management (TQM)** is a management approach that extends quality principles to every part of an organization rather than leaving them to an inspection department. Marketing, purchasing, accounting and shipping are treated as contributors to product and service quality just as much as the factory floor, and company-wide teams work to prevent problems and systematically improve key processes instead of reacting after defects appear.[^s1] Its core commitments are customer focus, continuous improvement measured with statistical techniques, and visible leadership from top management, which is expected to build a culture centered on quality.[^s1]

## Origin

TQM's roots lie in earlier American work. Walter Shewhart developed statistical methods for monitoring and controlling quality, while the Hawthorne studies of the late 1920s suggested that worker participation affects productivity, a counterpoint to scientific management's separation of planning from doing.[^s2] The decisive step came in Japan. In 1950 the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE) invited W. Edwards Deming to teach statistical quality improvement, and he gave the first of roughly a dozen lectures in Tokyo; JUSE created the Deming Prize the following year.[^s3] Joseph Juran taught managerial approaches to quality, Armand Feigenbaum's *Total Quality Control* anticipated the companywide idea, and Kaoru Ishikawa synthesized what the Japanese called companywide quality control.[^s2] The label "Total Quality Management" itself was first used by the U.S. Naval Air Systems Command to describe its Japanese-style improvement approach.[^s2]

American interest surged after NBC broadcast the documentary *If Japan Can, Why Can't We?* on 24 June 1980, which the Deming Institute treats as a turning point for U.S. quality control.[^s3] By the mid-1990s, TQM was, in Thomas Powell's phrase, "as pervasive a part of business thinking as quarterly financial results."[^s5]

## How it works

Hackman and Wageman, reviewing the founders' writings, describe TQM as a philosophy built on the work of Deming, Juran and Ishikawa and evaluate it against behavioral research on motivation, learning and organizational change.[^s4] In practice it combines several elements:

- **Customer-defined quality**: products are judged by how well they serve their purpose, not only by conformance to internal specifications.[^s1]
- **Prevention over inspection**: final inspection alone is insufficient; excellence must be built into every operation.[^s1]
- **Continuous improvement**: performance is tracked statistically and processes are refined incrementally.[^s1]
- **Participation and teams**: cross-functional teams at every level own process improvement.[^s1][^s2]

Later movements codified or extended these ideas. ISO 9000 standards, the Deming Prize and the Baldrige Award embedded TQM principles in certification and award criteria, and Six Sigma pushed the statistical side toward a target of 3.4 defects per million opportunities.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The performance evidence is mixed and depends on what is measured. Powell's 1995 study in the *Strategic Management Journal* found that the visible tools of TQM, such as quality training, process improvement and benchmarking, generally did not produce competitive advantage, whereas tacit, hard-to-imitate features did: an open culture, employee empowerment and executive commitment.[^s5] Powell concluded that firms acquiring those cultural resources could outperform rivals with or without the TQM label, a resource-based critique of "tool kit" adoption.[^s5]

Hendricks and Singhal (2001), using quality-award wins as a proxy for effective implementation, found no stock-price difference between winners and matched controls during a five-year implementation period, but significant outperformance over the following five years, with mean gains of roughly 38% to 46% depending on the control group.[^s6] The lag suggests TQM's payoff is real but slow, which may explain why impatient programs were abandoned. Hackman and Wageman questioned whether TQM was coherent and distinctive enough to endure and noted gaps in knowledge about its processes and outcomes.[^s4] As international standards matured, TQM as a term fell out of favor for describing an organization's quality system, even though its practices persisted under other names.[^s2]

## In practice

Ford Motor Company is the classic U.S. example. After Deming's consultations, Ford's Taurus became America's best-selling car in 1992, and Ford rose to second place in U.S. and world auto sales, according to the Deming Institute's account.[^s3] General Electric's later company-wide adoption of Six Sigma shows how TQM's statistical core was repackaged for a new generation of programs.[^s1]

## Connections

TQM sits under **operations management** and draws on **W. Edwards Deming**, **Joseph Juran** and **Kaoru Ishikawa**, whose writings Hackman and Wageman treat as its foundation.[^s4] It depends on **statistical process control** descended from Shewhart, and it contrasts with **scientific management**, which separated planning from execution and gave workers little voice.[^s2] **Benchmarking** was one of the common TQM features Powell tested,[^s5] and the **Ishikawa diagram** (for cause analysis) and **hoshin kanri** (for cascading improvement goals) are related quality-movement practices covered in their own nodes. The **Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award** codified its principles in the United States, and **Six Sigma** and **lean manufacturing** are successor and sibling movements.[^s1][^s3] **Ford Motor Company** exemplifies a large-scale adoption.[^s3]
