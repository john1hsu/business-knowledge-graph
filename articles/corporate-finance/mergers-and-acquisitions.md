# Mergers and Acquisitions: Takeovers, Synergies and the Market for Corporate Control

**Mergers and acquisitions (M&A)** are transactions in which independent companies are combined under common ownership. In a merger, two or more firms combine to form one new company; in an acquisition, a buyer (a corporation or an investor group) identifies a target and negotiates with its board to purchase it.[^s4] In practice the line blurs: control can pass through a purchase of assets for cash or securities, a purchase of shares, or an exchange of the acquirer's stock for the target's shares.[^s4] Financial economists treat the whole activity as a market for corporate control, in which management teams compete for the right to run a firm's assets.[^s3]

## Types and motives

Textbooks distinguish three classic forms. A **horizontal** deal joins firms at the same stage of the same industry; a **vertical** deal buys a supplier or distributor at an earlier or later stage; a **conglomerate** deal joins unrelated businesses, usually to spread risk across products and business cycles.[^s4] The stated motives are cost savings from removing overlapping operations, greater purchasing power and market share, faster growth or entry into new markets, and access to technology or management talent that would be slower or riskier to build in-house.[^s4] A financially driven variant is the leveraged buyout, in which borrowed money funds most of the price and the target is taken private.[^s4]

Research reviews add less flattering motives. Burkart and Panunzi's survey finds evidence that empire-building and surplus cash drive some acquisitions, and that such deals tend to earn low or negative returns for bidders; disciplining weak managers, correcting earlier value-destroying diversification, tax savings and transfers from employees each explain only part of observed premiums, and evidence for market-power gains is inconclusive.[^s3]

## How deals are reviewed

In the United States, the Hart-Scott-Rodino (HSR) Act requires parties to sufficiently large transactions to file a premerger notification and wait before closing. Filings go to both the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice, which decide between themselves which agency will review the deal.[^s5] The initial waiting period is 30 days (15 for cash tender offers and bankruptcies); a Second Request for more information extends it until the parties have substantially complied, after which the agency generally has 30 more days. The agency can then close its investigation, negotiate a consent order, or go to federal court to block the deal.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Event studies consistently find that **target** shareholders win: average abnormal announcement returns for US targets are typically 15 to 30 percent, while **bidder** returns cluster between about +5 and -5 percent, with studies disagreeing on the sign, and combined returns are positive but small, roughly 1 to 3 percent.[^s3] Andrade, Mitchell and Stafford, covering 1973 to 1998, found a positive combined market reaction and improved post-merger operating performance relative to industry peers, which they read as evidence that mergers on average create value. They also documented that deals cluster by industry in waves, that deregulation accounted for nearly half of activity from the late 1980s, and that 1990s deals were mostly stock swaps with hostile bids nearly disappearing.[^s1]

The sharpest critique concerns large deals at market peaks. Moeller, Schlingemann and Stulz estimate that acquiring-firm shareholders lost 12 cents per dollar spent, about $240 billion in total, on announcements from 1998 to 2001, against losses of $7 billion (1.6 cents per dollar) across the 1980s. Bidder losses in 1998–2001 exceeded target gains by $134 billion, and a small number of acquisitions by very highly valued firms drove most of the damage.[^s2] Taken together, the evidence suggests M&A creates value on average but transfers most of it to target shareholders, so the burden on acquirers is to avoid overpaying.[^s2][^s3]

## Example: AOL and Time Warner

The merger of America Online and Time Warner closed on January 11, 2001, at the top of the internet boom.[^s6] In the first quarter of 2002, on adopting new goodwill accounting rules, the combined company recorded a $54.235 billion non-cash charge for goodwill impairment, and it reported a 2002 net loss of $98.696 billion, compared with $4.934 billion in 2001.[^s6] The episode shows how a stock-financed deal struck at peak valuations can embed a purchase premium that later has to be written off.[^s6][^s2]

## Connections

M&A is a core decision area of corporate finance and corporate strategy, sitting alongside internal growth as a way to add products, markets and capabilities.[^s4] The premium over book value paid in an acquisition is recorded as goodwill, which links M&A to accounting.[^s6] HSR review ties it to antitrust law,[^s5] and leveraged buyouts and hostile takeovers are specialised forms of the practice.[^s4][^s3] Because managers can pursue deals for their own benefit, M&A is a recurring arena for agency costs and corporate-governance concerns, and overconfidence in bidders is one behavioral explanation offered for overpayment.[^s3][^s2]
