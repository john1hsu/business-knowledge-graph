# Efficient Market Hypothesis

The **efficient market hypothesis (EMH)** holds that financial markets are informationally efficient: security prices already incorporate the information available to investors, so no one can gain a lasting edge by trading on public information. Britannica traces the idea to Eugene F. Fama's 1965 doctoral research and its formalization in a 1970 paper, and notes its striking implication that, for everyone except insiders, stock picking resembles a game of chance.[^s4] Burton Malkiel states the operational version: markets are efficient if they do not let investors earn above-average returns without taking above-average risk.[^s2]

## Origin and formal statement

Fama's 1970 review in *The Journal of Finance* defined an efficient market as one in which prices "fully reflect" available information. Because that phrase alone cannot be tested, he recast it as an expected-return condition: given the information set, a security's expected return equals its equilibrium, risk-based return, so trading rules built on that information should not produce abnormal profits. The random walk model is a stricter special case of this "fair game" framing.[^s1] Fama listed sufficient conditions (no transaction costs, free information, and agreement on what information implies) but stressed that they are not necessary; costs and disagreement are only potential sources of inefficiency, to be judged empirically.[^s1]

## The three forms

Fama sorted tests by the information set involved.[^s1]

- **Weak form:** past prices and returns. Serial-correlation and filter-rule tests found little exploitable dependence.[^s1]
- **Semi-strong form:** all public information. Event studies of stock splits, earnings announcements and secondary offerings showed prices adjusting quickly.[^s1]
- **Strong form:** any information, including private. Fama found limited contrary evidence: stock-exchange specialists and corporate insiders appeared to profit from privileged access.[^s1]

He concluded that the efficient-markets model stood up well overall.[^s1] He also flagged the **joint-hypothesis problem**: every test of efficiency is simultaneously a test of the assumed model of expected returns, so an apparent anomaly may signal a bad risk model rather than an inefficient market.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The empirical record is mixed by horizon. The Nobel committee, awarding the 2013 economics prize to Fama, Robert Shiller and Lars Peter Hansen, summarized that asset prices are essentially unpredictable over days or weeks but show predictable patterns over several years. Shiller showed that stock prices swing far more than later dividends justify, so a high price-to-dividend ratio tends to precede weak returns. The committee noted that researchers still disagree on whether this reflects time-varying risk (the rational view) or psychology and institutional limits (the behavioral view).[^s3]

Malkiel's 2003 survey reviews the critics' catalogue: short-run momentum, long-run reversals, the January and size effects, value and valuation-ratio predictability, the 1987 crash and the internet bubble. He argues that most patterns are economically small after costs, unstable, or faded once published, and may proxy for risk; he concedes that internet-era prices were wrong in hindsight, but says limited arbitrage made them hard to exploit.[^s2]

A theoretical challenge came from Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz (1980). If prices perfectly revealed information, no one would pay to gather it, and the price system would break down. Markets must therefore stay inefficient enough to reward information gathering. Knowledge at Wharton calls this an efficiency paradox and notes its relevance to the active-versus-passive investing debate.[^s5]

## In practice

The most direct business test is whether professionals beat the market. Fama cited Jensen's finding that mutual funds, after expenses, did not on average earn more than risk-adjusted benchmarks.[^s1] Malkiel reported that over the 10 years to June 30, 2002, the S&P 500 outperformed about 79% of large-capitalization equity funds, and that top-performing funds of one decade rarely repeated in the next.[^s2] The Nobel committee credited this line of research with supporting the growth of index funds.[^s3] For corporate managers, the hypothesis implies that a firm's share price is an informative, if imperfect, signal of the market's assessment of its prospects, and that cosmetic financial engineering rarely fools investors for long.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The EMH is a central theory of **asset pricing** within **corporate finance**, created by **Eugene Fama** and introduced in his **1970 review of efficient capital markets**.[^s1][^s4] It relies on **arbitrage** to correct mispricing quickly, and it cannot be tested apart from a risk model such as the **capital asset pricing model**, because of the joint-hypothesis problem.[^s1][^s2] It contrasts with **behavioral finance**, which attributes long-horizon predictability to psychology and limits to arbitrage.[^s3] Its evidence on fund performance influenced the rise of the **index fund**.[^s3]
