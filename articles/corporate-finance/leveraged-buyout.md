# Leveraged Buyout

A leveraged buyout (LBO) is the purchase of a company by a specialized investment firm that contributes a relatively small amount of equity and borrows the rest, typically taking majority control of a mature business rather than funding a young one, as venture capital does.[^s1] The firms that sponsor these deals now usually describe themselves as private equity firms.[^s1] The U.S. Government Accountability Office describes the sponsored LBO as a fund's acquisition of majority or full ownership of a public company, private company or division, financed heavily with bank borrowing that sharply raises the target's debt-to-equity ratio.[^s2]

## How it works

The GAO breaks a typical deal into five stages: the sponsor raises a fund from investors such as pension plans and endowments, identifies a target, secures loan commitments that banks usually syndicate to other lenders, owns and works on the company for roughly three to five years, and then exits through an IPO or a sale to a strategic buyer, another private equity firm or a special purpose acquisition company.[^s2] In Kaplan and Strömberg's account, public targets are usually bought at a premium of 15 to 50 percent over the prevailing share price, debt covers 60 to 90 percent of the price (a senior secured loan plus, often, junior high-yield or mezzanine debt), and the fund supplies the remaining equity, with managers usually investing alongside it.[^s1] The funds themselves are closed-end limited partnerships with a typical ten-year life; general partners earn an annual management fee, a carried interest that is almost always 20 percent of profits, and deal and monitoring fees charged to portfolio companies.[^s1]

Sponsors are said to create value through three kinds of "engineering." Financial engineering gives managers large equity stakes and uses heavy debt to discipline spending; governance engineering installs smaller, more active boards and replaces underperforming executives quickly; and operational engineering, more prominent since the 2000s, brings industry and operating expertise to the portfolio company.[^s1] In a sample of U.S. deals from 1996 to 2004, the median CEO held 5.4 percent of the equity upside and the management team 16 percent.[^s1]

## Origin and history

LBOs became a major phenomenon in the 1980s. In a 1989 *Harvard Business Review* article, Michael Jensen argued that concentrated ownership, high-powered incentives and lean organization made such private forms superior to widely held public corporations in sectors where owner-manager conflicts were severe.[^s1][^s5] The first wave peaked with KKR's 1988 buyout of RJR Nabisco; the collapse of the junk bond market and a string of defaults then made public-to-private deals nearly disappear in the early 1990s.[^s1] Activity revived in the mid-2000s: the GAO counted about 2,994 LBOs of U.S. targets worth roughly $1.05 trillion from 2000 to 2007, rising from about 3 percent of U.S. merger value in 2000 to nearly 28 percent in 2007.[^s2] About 16 percent of those deals, holding about 44 percent of the value, were "club deals" in which several sponsors bought together.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Early evidence was favourable. Kaplan's study of 76 large management buyouts of public companies completed between 1980 and 1986 found that, over the following three years, operating income rose, capital spending fell and net cash flow increased, and that market-adjusted value rose by a median of 77 percent; he attributed the gains to sharper incentives rather than layoffs or insider exploitation of shareholders.[^s3] Later public-to-private deals show more modest operating improvements, and fund-level studies find that investors earned roughly the S&P 500's return after fees, with fees for a median fund estimated at about $19 in present value per $100 of capital.[^s1]

Critics raise four main concerns. Interest deductibility subsidizes leverage: Kaplan and Strömberg judge tax savings of 10 to 20 percent of firm value a reasonable estimate for 1980s deals, and less for later ones.[^s1] Buyouts may cost jobs: a census-based study of about 3,200 U.S. targets from 1980 to 2005 found employment at existing establishments fell 6 percent relative to matched controls over five years, though net job loss across all margins was about 1 percent over two years, alongside productivity gains driven by reallocation toward more productive units and lower earnings per worker.[^s4] Deal volume follows credit conditions more than fundamentals, so booms tend to precede weaker fund returns.[^s1] And the GAO reported a credit rating agency's view that private equity ownership raises default risk for some firms, though default rates on such loans had stayed historically low, while its own case studies of five companies showed job gains at two and losses at three.[^s2]

## In practice

RJR Nabisco remains the textbook example of both the model's power and its limits: the roughly $10 billion premium paid to public shareholders absorbed much of the value the buyout created, showing that sellers can capture the gains when sponsors bid aggressively.[^s1] Decades later, scholars revisiting Jensen's prediction argue that U.S. listed firms fell from a 1997 peak of 7,509 to 3,618 in 2016, but attribute this more to an "eclipse of the public markets" than to buyouts replacing public companies.[^s5]

## Connections

The LBO is a form of **mergers and acquisitions** and the core activity of **private equity**, and it sits within **corporate finance** as a deliberate choice of **capital structure**.[^s1] Its theoretical rationale is the control of **agency costs**: Jensen's argument that debt service forces managers to pay out **free cash flow** rather than waste it is the main reason heavy leverage is supposed to improve performance.[^s1][^s5] It tests the **Modigliani-Miller theorem**, since the tax shield on interest is one of the frictions that makes financing matter.[^s1] It is an alternative mechanism of **corporate governance** to boards and markets, and it contrasts with **venture capital**, which funds young firms with minority stakes.[^s1] The deals depended historically on **high-yield bonds**, and the **RJR Nabisco buyout** is its emblematic case.[^s1]
