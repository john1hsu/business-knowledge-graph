# Michael Jensen

Michael C. Jensen (1939–2024) was an American financial economist best known for agency theory: the analysis of what it costs when owners delegate control of a firm to managers whose interests differ from theirs.[^s4] He earned an MBA and a PhD in economics, finance and accounting at the University of Chicago, taught at the University of Rochester's Simon School from 1967, and moved to Harvard Business School in the 1980s, where he became Jesse Isidor Straus Professor of Business Administration and founded what is now the Negotiation, Organizations and Markets unit.[^s1] He served as president of the American Finance Association and died in April 2024.[^s1][^s4]

## Major contributions

**Agency costs and the theory of the firm.** With William Meckling, Jensen published "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure" in the *Journal of Financial Economics* in 1976.[^s2] The paper combined agency, property-rights and finance theory to explain ownership structure, defined agency costs, tied them to the separation of ownership and control, and analysed the agency costs of both outside equity and debt.[^s2] Rochester describes it as the most-cited article in business academia; its practical prescription was to give executives equity so their incentives track shareholders'.[^s4]

**Jensen's alpha.** His doctoral work, published in the *Journal of Finance*, built on the capital asset pricing model of Sharpe, Lintner and Treynor to create a risk-adjusted measure of how much a manager's forecasting skill adds to returns, now known as Jensen's alpha.[^s3] Applied to 115 mutual funds over 1945–1964, it showed that funds on average did not beat a buy-and-hold strategy, even before deducting management expenses.[^s3]

**Executive pay.** In 1990 Jensen and Kevin Murphy estimated that a typical CEO's wealth moved by only $3.25 for each $1,000 change in shareholder wealth, too little to motivate value creation, and they urged more equity-based pay.[^s5]

**Institution building.** Jensen founded the *Journal of Financial Economics* in the 1970s and edited it for two decades, founded Rochester's Managerial Economics Research Center in 1977, and in 1994 co-founded Social Science Electronic Publishing, the company behind SSRN, which he chaired.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Jensen's influence is hard to separate from its side effects. Murphy recalls that firms did not swap salary for equity as the pair expected; instead they layered option grants onto existing pay, and options roughly tripled inflation-adjusted S&P 500 CEO pay in under a decade.[^s5] Critics came to see Jensen as a defender of outsized executive pay and hostile takeovers, while admirers credited him with revitalizing American capitalism.[^s4]

Jensen revised his own views. In a 2005 paper with Murphy he argued that boards treated options as nearly free because of their accounting and cash treatment, that options should be expensed, and that **overvalued equity** creates its own agency costs: managers who cannot justify an inflated share price may make value-destroying acquisitions, manage earnings or commit fraud, and large equity stakes make them less willing to let the price correct.[^s6] After the Enron and WorldCom scandals and the 2008 crisis he turned to integrity, arguing in 2012 with Werner Erhard that it is as fundamental to economic performance as labor, capital and technology.[^s4]

## In practice

Jensen's ideas reached boardrooms mainly through equity-based executive pay, which followed directly from the 1976 prescription to give managers an ownership stake.[^s4][^s5] He also regarded hostile takeovers as an efficient way to unlock trapped value and defended golden parachutes.[^s4] His 1989 *Harvard Business Review* article "Eclipse of the Public Corporation" won the McKinsey Award for that year.[^s1] Murphy notes that some observers have even blamed the Jensen–Murphy *Harvard Business Review* article on CEO incentives for the later explosion in option grants, although he attributes most of it to unintended effects of congressional attempts to curb pay.[^s5]

## Connections

Jensen is a central figure in **corporate finance** and the creator of the **agency costs** framework, which formalizes the **principal-agent problem** for public firms and underpins modern **corporate governance**.[^s2][^s4] His co-author **William H. Meckling** shares credit for that framework.[^s2] His case for equity incentives shaped **executive compensation** and the **shareholder primacy** view that managers should maximize shareholder value, and his later work on overvalued equity critiques how that view was applied.[^s5][^s6] The graph's **free cash flow** and **leveraged buyout** articles trace his argument that high-powered ownership and payout discipline curb managerial waste, the theme of his award-winning 1989 essay.[^s1] **Jensen's alpha**, his risk-adjusted performance measure, links him to asset pricing and to the debate over the **efficient market hypothesis**.[^s3]
