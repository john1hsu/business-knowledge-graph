# Share Repurchases: Returning Cash by Buying Back Stock

A **share repurchase**, or **stock buyback**, is a transaction in which a company buys its own outstanding shares from investors, reducing the number of shares held by the public. Like a cash dividend, it moves corporate cash to shareholders, but only the holders who choose to sell receive it, and the company can scale or pause the program without the stigma attached to a dividend cut. In the United States the modern buyback era is usually dated to 1982, when the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted Rule 10b-18, a safe harbor that shields an issuer's open-market purchases of its own stock from manipulation liability if they meet set conditions.[^s1][^s4]

## How repurchases are executed

Research distinguishes three main methods: **open-market programs**, in which the company buys through the market over time; **fixed-price self-tender offers**, in which it offers to buy shares from holders at a set price; and **Dutch-auction self-tenders**, in which holders indicate the prices at which they would sell and the firm sets the price from those bids.[^s3] Open-market buying dominates: Lazonick found that almost all of the S&P 500 buybacks he studied for 2003-2012 were open-market purchases.[^s4]

Rule 10b-18 shapes how those open-market purchases are made. To stay inside the safe harbor, an issuer must use a single broker or dealer on any given day, avoid the opening trade and the final minutes of the session, pay no more than the highest independent bid or last independent trade price, and keep daily purchases within 25% of the stock's average daily trading volume over the prior four calendar weeks (with a once-a-week block exception). The rule was amended in 2003. Compliance is voluntary, and missing a condition does not by itself imply manipulation, but one miss removes all of that day's purchases from the safe harbor.[^s1]

## Why firms buy back stock

The leading explanations mirror the frictions that make payout policy matter. Under **signalling**, managers who believe their shares are cheap use a buyback to communicate that private view. Comment and Jarrell found that fixed-price tender offers are stronger signals of undervaluation than Dutch auctions or open-market programs, and that announcement price gains are larger when insiders' own wealth is at stake and after the stock has underperformed.[^s3] Under the **agency** view, returning surplus cash keeps it from being spent on low-return projects, and defenders argue that cash paid out is not lost to the economy because much of it flows back into other firms through share issuance.[^s5] A less flattering motive is mechanical: by shrinking the share count, buybacks lift earnings per share, which matters when executive pay is tied to EPS targets and stock price.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The undervaluation story has empirical support. Studying open-market programs announced from 1980 to 1990, Ikenberry, Lakonishok and Vermaelen found an average abnormal buy-and-hold return of 12.1% over the four years after the announcement, rising to 45.3% for high book-to-market "value" firms and absent for "glamour" firms. They read this as the market underreacting to the information in buyback announcements, a result that sits uneasily with strong forms of the efficient-market hypothesis.[^s2]

Critics argue that buybacks serve executives more than the firm. In a 2014 paper that became a widely read Harvard Business Review article, William Lazonick reported that 449 S&P 500 companies spent 54% of their 2003-2012 earnings, about $2.4 trillion, on buybacks and another 37% on dividends. He contended that open-market repurchases boost earnings per share and stock-based pay, that firms need not disclose the days on which they buy, and that Rule 10b-18 should be rescinded.[^s4]

Jesse Fried and Charles Wang replied in Harvard Business Review in 2018 that such figures ignore cash flowing the other way. For S&P 500 firms in 2007-2016, equity issuance of $3.3 trillion offset about 79% of the $4.2 trillion of repurchases, so net payouts were roughly half of net income; adding back after-tax R&D, net payouts fell to 41.5%, and R&D spending reached a record $274 billion in 2016. They conceded that executives can misuse buybacks and that better rules on that point are warranted.[^s5]

## In practice

Apple is the largest example. In fiscal 2025 (the year ended 27 September 2025) it repurchased $89.3 billion of its common stock while paying $15.4 billion in dividends and dividend equivalents, and in May 2025 its board authorized a new program of up to $100 billion, of which about $99.8 billion remained available at fiscal year-end.[^s6] That roughly six-to-one ratio of buybacks to dividends in a single year shows how heavily a cash-rich firm can lean on repurchases rather than dividends to return cash.[^s6]

## Connections

Share repurchases are a branch of **dividend policy** (payout policy) within **corporate finance**, and the main alternative to a regular **cash dividend**.[^s5] Their use is **regulated by** SEC **Rule 10b-18**.[^s1] The signalling rationale draws on **signalling theory** and depends on **information asymmetry** between managers and investors, while the long-run return evidence bears on the **efficient-market hypothesis**.[^s2][^s3] Paying out surplus **free cash flow** is meant to **mitigate agency costs**, yet the critique that buybacks inflate stock-based **executive compensation** and serve **shareholder primacy** at the expense of investment ties them to debates in corporate governance.[^s4][^s5] Netting buybacks against equity issuance, as Fried and Wang do, links them to how firms manage their **capital structure** over time.[^s5] **Apple** exemplifies the practice at scale.[^s6]
