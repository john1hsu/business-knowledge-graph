# Whistleblowing and Whistleblower Protection

**Whistleblowing** is the act of an organization member who believes they hold evidence of organizational wrongdoing bringing it to someone who can stop or sanction it. The target may be internal (a supervisor, an ethics hotline, the audit committee) or external (a regulator, a legislator, the media). In business, the term also covers the legal regimes that protect reporters from retaliation and, increasingly, pay them for useful tips.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin and theory

Academic study of the subject took shape in the 1980s. Janet Near and Marcia Miceli argued in 1985 that early research lacked a sound theoretical base. They modelled whistleblowing as a process: a member decides whether, how and to whom to report perceived wrongdoing, and the organization's authorities decide how to respond. Their model draws on motivation theory (why someone speaks up) and power theory (whether the organization listens or retaliates), and it framed whistleblowing as a form of organizational dissidence rather than simple disloyalty.[^s1]

## How it works in U.S. corporate law

Two federal statutes set the main framework for public companies. Section 806 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) protects employees of SEC-reporting companies, and of their subsidiaries, contractors and agents, who report possible securities fraud, mail, wire or bank fraud, or SEC rule violations. Reports to a federal agency, to Congress, or to a supervisor inside the company all count. Retaliation such as firing, demotion, blacklisting or reduced pay is unlawful if the protected report was a "contributing factor." Employees must file with OSHA within 180 days. Remedies include reinstatement, back pay with interest and litigation costs.[^s4]

The Dodd-Frank Act (2010) added Section 21F to the Securities Exchange Act, creating the SEC Whistleblower Program. Individuals who give original information leading to an enforcement action with monetary sanctions over $1 million can receive 10% to 30% of the sanctions collected. Claimants need not report internally first, although doing so may raise their award percentage.[^s3] In *Digital Realty Trust v. Somers* (2018), a unanimous Supreme Court held that Dodd-Frank's anti-retaliation protection covers only people who have reported to the SEC. Employees who report only internally must rely on Sarbanes-Oxley or state law. Commentators expected the ruling to push more tipsters directly to the regulator.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Employees are among the most important detectors of fraud. Dyck, Morse and Zingales studied every reported fraud at large U.S. companies (assets above $750 million) from 1996 to 2004. Employees uncovered about 19% of cases, more than industry regulators (16%), the media (14%), auditors (14%) or the SEC (6%). No single actor detected more than a fifth of cases. Monetary rewards for reporting fraud against the government raised detection without increasing frivolous suits, so the authors argued for extending such rewards to corporate fraud. Dodd-Frank later did just that.[^s2]

Internal channels also appear to pay off for firms. Stubben and Welch analysed nearly two million reports made through hotlines at more than 1,000 U.S. public companies. Firms whose systems were used more actively faced fewer material lawsuits and smaller government fines. This challenges the view that a busy hotline signals a troubled firm.[^s5] Critics of bounty programs raise the opposite worry. Paying for external tips may lead employees to bypass internal compliance, so firms learn of problems only after a regulator's investigation has advanced, a concern that sharpened after *Somers*.[^s6] Process models also stress that the organization's response, not only the reporter's courage, decides whether wrongdoing ends.[^s1]

## In practice

The SEC program shows the scale of modern whistleblowing. In fiscal year 2025 the SEC received about 27,000 tips. It awarded more than $60 million to 48 individuals across 31 covered actions.[^s3] For managers, the practical lessons are to run a credible, well-used internal reporting system, to route serious reports to the audit committee, and to treat retaliation as both a legal and a governance failure. Sarbanes-Oxley remedies make retaliation costly.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Whistleblowing is a practice within **business ethics** and a monitoring mechanism of **corporate governance**: it brings managers' hidden actions to light and so narrows the **information asymmetry** behind the **principal-agent problem**.[^s2] It is regulated chiefly by the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act**, which protects internal and external reporters, and the **Dodd-Frank Act**, which adds SEC bounties.[^s4][^s3] Employee tips are a leading way **accounting fraud** is uncovered.[^s2] Internal hotlines connect it to the **audit committee** and to **internal control over financial reporting**, whose effectiveness depends on reports reaching people who can act.[^s5]
