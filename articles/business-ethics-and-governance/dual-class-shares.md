# Dual-Class Shares: Founder Control Through Unequal Voting Rights

A **dual-class share structure** gives a company two or more classes of common stock that carry the same or similar economic claims but different voting power. Typically a "high-vote" class is held by founders and early insiders, while the class sold to the public carries one vote or none.[^s1] Because dual-class stock separates cash-flow rights from voting rights, it lets insiders control the board and every shareholder vote while owning only a minority of the equity.[^s3]

## Origin and history

For most of modern U.S. market history the New York Stock Exchange refused to list companies with unequal voting classes; its stance dates to before the Great Depression, and in 1940 it adopted a policy against non-voting common stock.[^s1] During the takeover battles of the 1980s, insiders lobbied exchanges to relax voting rules and the NYSE reversed course. The SEC tried to restore a uniform voting standard, but a 1990 D.C. Circuit ruling, *Business Roundtable v. SEC*, held that it lacked the authority.[^s1] Listing decisions have since been left mainly to exchanges and index providers.

The structure then spread with technology IPOs. According to SEC Commissioner Robert Jackson, more than 14% of the 133 companies that listed on U.S. exchanges in 2015 used dual-class voting, up from about 1% in 2005, and dual-class companies were collectively worth over $5 trillion by early 2018.[^s1] A 2026 review by Hannes and Libson puts the share of recent new listings at roughly a quarter to a third.[^s5] Snap's 2017 IPO went further by selling shares with no vote at all.[^s1]

## How it works

The corporate charter defines each class's votes per share, and the key design choice is duration. A **perpetual** structure lasts indefinitely; a **sunset** provision collapses the classes into one after a set period or event. Jackson reported that nearly half of the dual-class firms that went public in the previous 15 years gave insiders control in perpetuity.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Defenders argue that insulating visionary founders from short-term investor and activist pressure lets them pursue long-horizon strategies. Hannes and Libson note that as poison pills and staggered boards declined among S&P 500 firms, dual-class stock became founders' main durable defense against activists whose power has grown with concentrated institutional ownership.[^s5]

Critics focus on the wedge between control and ownership. Using a comprehensive sample of U.S. dual-class firms and instrumental-variable methods, Gompers, Ishii and Metrick found firm value rises with insiders' cash-flow rights and falls with their voting rights, consistent with agency costs from control without matching ownership.[^s3] Bebchuk and Kastiel argue that a founder's advantages tend to fade over time while the costs of entrenchment grow, and that controllers have incentives to keep the structure long after it stops serving public investors.[^s2] They propose sunset clauses of roughly ten or fifteen years, extendable only with approval from shareholders unaffiliated with the controller.[^s2]

Jackson's staff studied 157 dual-class IPOs (71 with sunsets, 86 perpetual). Valuations were similar for two years after the IPO, but seven or more years out, perpetual firms traded at a significant discount, and firms that dropped dual-class saw valuation gains; he cautioned that this preliminary analysis did not establish causation.[^s1] He urged exchanges to require that shareholders eventually vote on continuing the structure, while opposing the outright index exclusions some providers had adopted, such as S&P Dow Jones's plan to exclude dual-class firms going forward, because that would keep ordinary investors out of fast-growing companies.[^s1]

## Real-world example

Alphabet, Google's parent, has three classes: Class B stock carries 10 votes per share, Class A one vote, and Class C no votes except as required by law.[^s4] As of December 31, 2024, co-founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin beneficially owned about 87.4% of Class B, giving them approximately 52.1% of the total voting power. Alphabet's 10-K flags this concentration as a risk that limits other stockholders' influence over corporate matters.[^s4]

## Connections

Dual-class stock is a governance arrangement within **corporate governance** that deliberately reverses the Berle-Means **separation of ownership and control**: rather than dispersed owners facing weak managers, outside owners face an entrenched controller, so the main **agency costs** shift to conflicts between controlling and minority holders.[^s3] It reduces the reach of the **board of directors** and **proxy advisory firms** because shareholder votes cannot change outcomes, and it limits the threat of a **hostile takeover**, acting as an alternative to the **poison pill** as a defense.[^s5] The practice departs from the **one-share-one-vote** norm the NYSE long enforced, and its spread is tied to the **initial public offering** choices of founders at companies such as **Google**.[^s1][^s4]
