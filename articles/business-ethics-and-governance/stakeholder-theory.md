# Stakeholder Theory

Stakeholder theory is the view that a business is best understood, and best managed, as a web of relationships with every group that has a stake in what it does: customers, employees, suppliers, communities and financiers, of whom shareholders are only one. R. Edward Freeman's widely used definition treats a stakeholder as any group or individual that can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of an organization's objectives, a definition deliberately meant to widen strategic management beyond its purely economic roots.[^s1] Its central claim is that a company succeeds over time by creating value for all of these groups rather than for stock owners alone.[^s3]

## Origin

The idea crystallized in Freeman's 1984 book *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach*; Freeman went on to advance the theory as a professor at the University of Virginia's Darden School, where he co-led a summer institute devoted to it.[^s3] The term itself predates the book: Freeman and John McVea trace it to planning work at the Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s, which drew on ideas from Lockheed's planning department and from Igor Ansoff, and to process research by Ian Mitroff, Richard Mason and James Emshoff. Fragments of the concept circulated in corporate planning, systems theory, corporate social responsibility and organization theory before Freeman's synthesis gave managers facing a turbulent environment a single framework.[^s1]

## How it works

As a management process, the stakeholder approach asks executives to identify their concrete stakeholders by name rather than as abstract categories, understand what each group values, and craft strategy that serves those interests together instead of trading one off against another. It rejects the idea that a firm maximizes a single objective function; managers instead keep many relationships in balance, and shared values are treated as part of strategy because long-run cooperation depends on them.[^s1] The approach is meant to be both descriptive and prescriptive, folding economic, political and moral analysis into one framework.[^s1]

Thomas Donaldson and Lee Preston's 1995 *Academy of Management Review* article organized the sprawling literature into three related but distinct claims. The **descriptive** claim is that firms actually do have, and respond to, multiple stakeholders. The **instrumental** claim is that firms which manage stakeholder relationships well perform better. The **normative** claim is that stakeholders' interests have intrinsic value, so firms ought to consider them. The authors argued the three reinforce one another but must not be conflated, and that the normative base, which they tied to modern property-rights theory, is the most fundamental.[^s2] Freeman's own school continues to teach the theory through empirical, normative and instrumental lenses.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest critique comes from corporate-law scholars. Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita argue in the *Cornell Law Review* (2020) that leaders have little incentive to protect stakeholders beyond what also benefits shareholders, so formal "stakeholderism" should deliver few real gains. They warn it could instead weaken accountability to shareholders, raise managerial slack and lower performance, while creating false hopes that delay regulation that would actually protect workers or communities. Reviewing recent corporate pledges, they concluded these were largely for show.[^s6] A related legal objection is that Delaware doctrine, as articulated by former Chief Justice Leo Strine, requires directors to treat stockholder welfare as the end and other interests only as means; other scholars counter that wealth maximization is a norm rather than an enforceable duty because the business judgment rule shields most decisions.[^s5] Donaldson and Preston themselves cautioned that instrumental evidence and normative argument rest on different kinds of support, which is why disputes over whether stakeholder management "pays" do not settle whether it is right.[^s2]

## In practice

The theory's most visible moment came on August 19, 2019, when the Business Roundtable released a Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation signed by 181 chief executives. It committed signers to deliver value to customers, employees, suppliers, communities and shareholders, replacing a series of statements issued since 1997 that had endorsed shareholder primacy.[^s4] Reaction split along predictable lines: supporters such as JPMorgan Chase's Jamie Dimon framed it as a commitment to long-term value, while the Council of Institutional Investors warned it would give CEOs cover to evade shareholder oversight, and commentators asked what signers would actually change.[^s5]

## Connections

Stakeholder theory sits under **corporate governance** and **business ethics**, since it answers the governance question "in whose interest is the firm run?" with a moral as well as strategic argument.[^s2] It was created by **R. Edward Freeman** and introduced in **Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach**.[^s3] It **contrasts with shareholder primacy**, the Friedman-associated view that the firm's social responsibility is to increase profits, and the 2019 **Business Roundtable statement** exemplifies a public shift between the two.[^s4][^s5] It is closely related to **corporate social responsibility** and **ESG**, which operationalize stakeholder concerns, and to **agency costs**, because critics argue that serving many masters loosens managers' accountability.[^s6][^s1]
