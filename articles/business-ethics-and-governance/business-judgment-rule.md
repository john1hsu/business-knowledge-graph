# Business Judgment Rule: Judicial Deference to Directors' Decisions

The business judgment rule is the main standard courts use to decide whether to review a board of directors' decisions. In its classic Delaware form, it presumes that directors made a business decision on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that it served the company's best interests. A shareholder who challenges the decision carries the burden of pleading facts that rebut that presumption.[^s1] If the presumption holds, a court will not second-guess the merits of a decision by a non-conflicted board, even if the decision later proves unwise.[^s2] The rule matters most in Delaware, where many large U.S. companies are incorporated and where the courts have built directors' duties case by case.[^s2]

## Origin

The rule's most-cited modern statement comes from *Aronson v. Lewis* (Del. 1984). In that case a stockholder of Meyers Parking System challenged a generous employment contract and interest-free loans that the board had approved for Leo Fink, who owned 47% of the stock.[^s1] The Delaware Supreme Court held that director liability under the rule rests on gross negligence. It also held that the rule applies only to actual board decisions, so it does not protect directors who abdicated their role or never made a conscious choice. Directors who stand on both sides of a transaction cannot invoke it.[^s1] *Aronson* also set out a test for when a shareholder may sue derivatively without first asking the board to act. The plaintiff must plead particularized facts raising a reasonable doubt either that the directors were disinterested and independent or that the deal was a valid exercise of business judgment.[^s1]

## How it works

The rule has three practical elements.

- **Disinterest and independence.** It protects decisions by a board whose majority has no conflicting interest. When a majority is conflicted, courts generally require the directors to prove the transaction was entirely fair.[^s2]
- **Due care.** Directors must consider material information, though not every possible fact, and Delaware reviews their process under a gross-negligence standard.[^s2]
- **Good faith.** Bad faith requires an extreme set of facts, such as a knowing and complete failure to act. A plaintiff who simply dislikes a deal's merits cannot overcome the rule.[^s5]

Delaware's stated rationale is that boards must make many decisions under time pressure, and that rational stockholders would not want directors deterred from good-faith risk-taking by fear of personal liability.[^s2] *In re Dow Chemical* (Del. Ch. 2010) illustrates the point. Shareholders attacked Dow's roughly $18.8 billion purchase of Rohm and Haas, which was signed without a financing contingency. The Court of Chancery dismissed the claims, held that high stakes do not trigger stricter review, and said that weighing strategic trade-offs belongs to the board, not the court.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The landmark exception is *Smith v. Van Gorkom* (Del. 1985). Trans Union's board approved a cash-out merger at $55 a share after a meeting of about two hours. Most directors had no advance notice of the subject and no valuation study, and the price had been chosen privately by the CEO.[^s3] The Delaware Supreme Court held that the directors were grossly negligent, denied them the rule's protection, and held that a later stockholder vote did not cure the problem because the proxy omitted material facts.[^s3] The decision drew sharp criticism. It contributed to a directors-and-officers insurance crisis, and Delaware responded with Section 102(b)(7), effective July 1, 1986. That section lets charters eliminate directors' monetary liability for care breaches but not for disloyalty or bad faith, and most public Delaware companies have adopted such a provision.[^s6] The same commentators credit *Van Gorkom* with pushing boards toward heavier use of advisers and more careful deliberation.[^s6]

Scholars also disagree about what the rule is. Stephen Bainbridge contrasts two readings: a standard of liability under which courts assess the merits of board decisions, and an abstention doctrine under which courts decline to review them at all. He argues that the first reading, which some Delaware justices had embraced, lets more shareholder suits survive summary judgment and so raises their settlement value. He favors abstention.[^s4] The combined effect of deference and exculpation is that disinterested directors face personal liability only for bad faith, which requires scienter. Shareholders' main remedies therefore run through loyalty claims and entire-fairness review of conflicted transactions.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

The business judgment rule is the courts' main tool for enforcing **fiduciary duty** and a core doctrine of **corporate law**. It defines how much deference the **board of directors** receives.[^s2] It assumes that **independent directors** make the decision, and it contrasts with the **entire fairness standard** that applies when a board is conflicted.[^s1][^s2] **Smith v. Van Gorkom** is the classic case in which a board lost its protection, and the **Delaware General Corporation Law**, through Section 102(b)(7), shaped the rule's practical reach.[^s3][^s6] Oversight claims under the **Caremark oversight duty** require bad faith, and exculpation clauses reinforce that requirement.[^s5] By tolerating risk-taking while policing self-dealing, the rule is a judicial answer to **agency costs** within **corporate governance**. It also reflects the **shareholder primacy** view that directors act for stockholders as residual owners.[^s2][^s4]
