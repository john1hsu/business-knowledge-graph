# Corporate Law

Corporate law (called company law in Britain and much of the Commonwealth) is the branch of law that brings the business corporation into existence and sets the rules for its internal life: who owns it, who runs it, what managers owe investors, and how far creditors can reach owners' personal wealth. Britannica describes the corporation as an organization of people and resources chartered by the state for doing business, treated as a fictive person that can contract, own property, and sue or be sued in its own name.[^s4] Most of what MBA students meet as "corporate governance" sits on top of this legal chassis.

## The core features of the corporate form

Henry Hansmann and Reinier Kraakman identify five legal features that define the corporation in every major commercial jurisdiction: full legal personality, so the firm can bind itself to contracts and bond them with its own assets separate from owners' assets; limited liability for owners and managers; shared ownership by suppliers of capital; delegated management under a board; and freely transferable shares.[^s1] They note that before 1800 only a handful of specially chartered companies combined all five, but by about 1900 they had become default rules everywhere that mattered commercially.[^s1] Britannica lists a similar set, adding indefinite duration: the corporation can outlive its founders and investors.[^s4]

## Origins

The corporate form grew out of medieval civil and canon law, where towns, universities, religious orders and guilds were the first corporations, and English courts had accepted the principle of limited liability for such bodies by the 15th century.[^s4] Business incorporation began with royal charters such as the East India Company's in 1600; these were monopolies granted for mercantilist ends.[^s4] The decisive shift was general incorporation, which made incorporation a right for any group meeting statutory conditions rather than a privilege granted case by case: New York passed a general act in 1811, other US states followed by the 1840s and 1850s, and Britain liberalized its statutes after 1825 and extended limited liability to such companies.[^s4][^s5] Because the US Constitution left incorporation largely to the states, firms can choose their state of incorporation while operating nationally, and states competed in the late 19th century to offer more permissive laws.[^s5][^s4]

## Why limited liability: the economic case

Frank Easterbrook and Daniel Fischel's classic analysis explains why limited liability is efficient rather than a mere transfer of risk to creditors.[^s2] It lets people with capital but no managerial skill invest in firms run by people with skill but no capital; it spares shareholders from monitoring managers intensely and from monitoring one another's wealth; it makes shares fungible, so a single market price can form and takeovers remain feasible; and it lets investors diversify cheaply, which in turn lets managers accept risky but value-creating projects.[^s3] They argue that if the law did not supply limited liability, firms would largely recreate it by contract, so the legal rule mostly saves transaction costs.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Limited liability has never been absolute. Courts sometimes "pierce the corporate veil" to reach shareholders, a doctrine Easterbrook and Fischel call rare, unpredictable and widely regarded as confusing, though they argue economic reasoning explains where it is applied.[^s2] The sharpest critique concerns involuntary creditors, such as tort victims, who cannot negotiate a risk premium in advance and so may bear costs that shareholders shift onto them.[^s2][^s3] A second debate concerns the purpose corporate law should serve. Hansmann and Kraakman argued in 2001 that elites had converged on a shareholder-oriented model, in which shareholders hold ultimate control, managers run the firm in their interest, and other constituencies are protected through contract and separate regulation (labor, environmental, consumer and tort law).[^s1] They contended that manager-oriented, labor-oriented (for example German codetermination) and state-oriented alternatives had lost credibility, and that stakeholder models either loosen managers' duties or seat stakeholder representatives on boards, inheriting the same weaknesses.[^s1] Their own survey concedes the convergence was incomplete: German board codetermination and US constituency statutes, which let directors weigh non-shareholder interests, remain in force.[^s1]

## In practice: Delaware

In the United States, corporate law is mainly state law, and Delaware dominates. As of its 2025 annual report, Delaware counted more than 2.28 million legal entities, said it was the legal home of over two-thirds of the Fortune 500, and reported that nearly 70% of US-based IPOs in 2025 chose it.[^s6] The state attributes this to the Delaware General Corporation Law, which it calls the nation's most flexible corporate statute, and to the Court of Chancery, a specialized business court that has written most modern US corporate case law; in 2025 the state amended the statute to add safe-harbor protection for transactions approved through established governance procedures.[^s6] These are the state's own claims, but the figures explain why Delaware decisions on fiduciary duty and the business judgment rule set national practice.[^s6]

## Connections

Corporate law is a branch of `kt:business-law` and the legal foundation of `kt:corporate-governance`: it supplies the board structure and the `kt:fiduciary-duty` that governance mechanisms enforce, including the deferential `kt:business-judgment-rule`.[^s1] Its board-delegated management creates the `kt:separation-of-ownership-and-control`, and its rules on shareholder control and duties are among the main tools for limiting `kt:agency-costs`.[^s3][^s1] The convergence thesis is the legal expression of `kt:shareholder-primacy`.[^s1] Its components include `kt:limited-liability` and the exception of `kt:piercing-the-corporate-veil`, and the `kt:delaware-general-corporation-law` is its most influential statute.[^s2][^s6]
