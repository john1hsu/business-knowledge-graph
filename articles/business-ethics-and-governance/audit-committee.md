# Board Audit Committee

An audit committee is a standing committee of the board of directors that oversees a company's financial reporting, its internal controls and its relationship with the independent auditor. In the United States its role is set by law: Section 301 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act added Section 10A(m) to the Securities Exchange Act, and the SEC's Rule 10A-3, published in April 2003, directs stock exchanges to refuse listing to any company whose audit committee fails the required standards.[^s1] The committee sits between management, which prepares the numbers, and the outside auditor, which tests them, so that the auditor answers to directors rather than to the executives it audits.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Audit committees existed long before 2002, but their modern form traces to the Blue Ribbon Committee on Improving the Effectiveness of Corporate Audit Committees, formed in September 1998 by the New York Stock Exchange and the NASD and co-chaired by Ira Millstein and John Whitehead.[^s2] Its February 1999 ten-point plan called for committees composed solely of independent directors, at least three members who are all financially literate with at least one having accounting or financial management expertise, a written charter approved by the full board, and an outside auditor ultimately accountable to the board and audit committee.[^s2] The NYSE and NASDAQ adopted audit-committee requirements in 1999, and in 2002 the Sarbanes-Oxley Act turned the core of the plan, independence and direct control of the auditor, into federal law.[^s1][^s3]

## How it works

Rule 10A-3 sets five requirements.[^s1] Members must be independent: apart from board fees, they may accept no consulting, advisory or other compensatory fees from the company, directly or indirectly, and may not be affiliated persons of it. The committee must be directly responsible for appointing, paying, retaining and overseeing the external auditor, which reports directly to it. It must set up procedures to receive and handle complaints about accounting, internal control and auditing matters, including confidential, anonymous submissions from employees. It must have authority to hire independent counsel and other advisers, and the company must fund the auditor, those advisers and the committee's ordinary expenses.[^s1]

The auditor's side of the relationship is governed by the PCAOB's AS 1301. Before issuing its report, the auditor must walk the committee through the audit strategy and significant risks, critical accounting policies and estimates, significant unusual transactions, its view of the qualitative aspects of the company's accounting including possible management bias, uncorrected misstatements, disagreements with management whether or not resolved, and any significant difficulties encountered during the audit.[^s5] In practice the committee therefore spends much of its time on judgment calls: reserves, impairments, revenue timing and the tone management sets on reporting.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Research supports the case for independence, with caveats. April Klein's study of about 687 S&P 500 firms in 1991–1993 found larger abnormal accruals, a common proxy for earnings management, when fewer than half of audit committee members were independent; the effect appeared at the majority threshold, and she found no significant additional benefit from a fully independent committee, a result that cut against the 1999 exchange rules.[^s3] Mark DeFond, Rebecca Hann and Xuesong Hu examined market reactions to the appointment of 702 outside directors to audit committees before Sarbanes-Oxley took effect: appointing directors with accounting expertise drew positive abnormal returns, while appointing those with broader, non-accounting financial expertise drew none, and the gain was concentrated in firms that already had strong governance.[^s4] The implication is that expertise helps only when the rest of the governance system lets the committee use it.[^s4]

The main critique is that independence on paper does not guarantee diligent oversight. Committees depend on information that management and auditors choose to surface, and part-time directors may lack the time or industry knowledge to probe it.[^s6] Mandated structure can also become a box-ticking exercise, which is why the 1999 recommendations stressed the quality, not only the acceptability, of accounting choices.[^s2]

## In practice: WorldCom

WorldCom illustrates both the failure and the value of the committee. The board's Special Investigative Committee reported in March 2003 that the company had recorded more than $9 billion of false or unsupported accounting entries from 1999 to 2002, including over $7 billion of line-cost reductions.[^s6] It concluded that the audit committee's oversight had been inadequate: members met as little as three to five hours a year and did not appear to understand the company's internal financial workings, its culture, or Arthur Andersen's non-traditional audit approach. Yet the fraud surfaced because internal audit's capital-expenditure review found the improper capitalization in June 2002 and took it to the audit committee, which then pressed for prompt action.[^s6]

## Connections

The audit committee is part of the board of directors and one of the main mechanisms of corporate governance for limiting agency costs: it reduces the information asymmetry between managers who prepare financial statements and the shareholders who rely on them.[^s3][^s4] The Sarbanes-Oxley Act regulates its composition and powers through Rule 10A-3,[^s1] and it oversees internal control over financial reporting and the external audit performed under PCAOB standards such as AS 1301 against GAAP-based statements.[^s5] The WorldCom accounting scandal exemplifies what weak and then active audit-committee oversight looks like.[^s6]
