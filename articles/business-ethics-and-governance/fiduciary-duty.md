# Fiduciary Duty

A fiduciary is someone who holds power and trust over another person's property, and the law requires that person to put the beneficiary's interests ahead of their own.[^s1] That sets fiduciary relationships apart from ordinary commercial dealings, in which each side is free to pursue its own gain. Classic fiduciaries include agents, executors and administrators, trustees, guardians and corporate officers.[^s1] In business, the concept matters most in three places: corporate directors and officers owe duties to the corporation and its shareholders; trustees and asset managers owe duties to beneficiaries; and investment advisers owe a federal fiduciary duty to clients.[^s2][^s4]

## Origin

Fiduciary obligations are rooted in equity. U.S. securities law, for example, traces the adviser's duty to equitable common-law principles, citing the Supreme Court's 1963 decision in *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau*.[^s4] For corporations, the leading body of law is Delaware's, because so many large U.S. companies are incorporated there. Delaware's General Corporation Law vests management in the board (Section 141(a)), and its courts have built the content of directors' duties case by case.[^s2]

## How it works

Modern fiduciary law usually divides the obligation into two core duties.

- **Duty of care.** Directors must make informed decisions: they need to consider material information, though not every scrap of data, and must review management's input critically. Courts look at time spent, information reviewed and whether experts were consulted. Delaware applies a gross-negligence standard and intervenes only when directors drastically depart from careful conduct.[^s2]
- **Duty of loyalty.** Fiduciaries must advance the beneficiary's interests and may not use their position for personal gain through unfair self-dealing, entrenchment or misuse of confidential information. Delaware treats good faith as part of loyalty, including a duty to see that the company has compliance systems and monitors senior management.[^s2]

Courts enforce these duties through standards of review. Under the **business judgment rule**, a decision by a board whose majority is not conflicted, made with due care and in good faith, is presumed valid and is not second-guessed even if it turns out badly. When a majority is conflicted, the rule may fall away and directors must generally prove the transaction was **entirely fair** to the corporation; deals with controlling stockholders get fairness review even when procedural protections such as independent committees exist.[^s2] Section 102(b)(7) lets a corporation's charter eliminate directors' personal monetary liability for breaches of care, but not of loyalty, in order to encourage good-faith risk-taking.[^s2]

The **oversight** strand of loyalty comes from *In re Caremark* (Del. Ch. 1996), which held that boards must make sure a reasonable information and reporting system exists. *Stone v. Ritter* (Del. 2006) confirmed that liability requires either an utter failure to implement such a system or a conscious failure to monitor it, which means plaintiffs must show bad faith.[^s3]

The SEC's 2019 interpretation describes the investment-adviser version in parallel terms. The duty of care covers advice in the client's best interest, best execution and appropriate monitoring. The duty of loyalty forbids putting the adviser's interests first and requires full and fair disclosure of conflicts so the client can give informed consent. The duty can be shaped by agreement, but a blanket waiver is not allowed.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Two broad schools debate what fiduciary duty is. The contractarian view, associated with Frank Easterbrook and Daniel Fischel's 1993 article "Contract and Fiduciary Duty," treats fiduciary duties as default contract terms. On this view, courts should fill gaps by asking what fully informed, rational parties would have agreed to, and explicit agreements should generally override implied duties.[^s5] Critics answer that fiduciary relationships rest on dependence and entrusted power, which justifies mandatory duties parties cannot simply contract away. Gregory Klass concludes that the hypothetical-bargain method helps check welfare effects but does not by itself show that parties should be free to alter fiduciary obligations.[^s5] The SEC's refusal to permit blanket waivers by advisers reflects the mandatory side of that debate.[^s4]

A practical critique concerns enforcement. Because exculpation clauses remove money damages for care breaches and the business judgment rule is deferential, director liability in practice turns largely on loyalty and bad faith.[^s2] Caremark oversight claims are notoriously hard to win; the Caremark court itself called them possibly the hardest theory in corporate law on which to prevail.[^s3]

## In practice

*Marchand v. Barnhill* (Del. 2019) shows how oversight duties bite. After a listeria outbreak at Blue Bell Creameries, shareholders alleged the board had no board-level system to monitor food safety, a mission-critical risk for an ice cream maker. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed dismissal, holding that failing to make a good-faith effort to monitor central compliance risks breaches the duty of loyalty.[^s3] Commentators read the case as sharpening, not loosening, the Caremark standard. They note that the plaintiff used a books-and-records demand under DGCL Section 220 to plead specific facts, and that courts judge the design of the reporting system rather than whether harm could have been prevented in hindsight.[^s3]

## Connections

Fiduciary duty is a pillar of **corporate governance** and **corporate law**. It works as a legal remedy for the **principal-agent problem**: where contracts and monitoring cannot fully align managers with owners, mandatory duties limit the **agency costs** of delegation.[^s1][^s5] The **board of directors** is the main bearer of corporate fiduciary duties, and the **business judgment rule** defines how much deference courts give its decisions.[^s2] The **Caremark oversight duty** is a component of the duty of loyalty, and **Marchand v. Barnhill** is its leading modern example.[^s3] Advisers' duties are enforced under the **Investment Advisers Act of 1940**.[^s4] Debates over whom directors owe duties to link this node to **shareholder primacy** and **stakeholder theory**; Delaware frames the duties as owed to the corporation and its stockholders.[^s2]
