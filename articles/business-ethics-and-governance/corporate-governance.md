# Corporate Governance

Corporate governance is the set of relationships among a company's management, its board, its shareholders and its other stakeholders, together with the structure through which the company's objectives are set and its performance is monitored.[^s1] The shorthand most often used in Britain describes it as the system by which businesses are directed and controlled.[^s5] UK regulators frame it more operationally as the rules, practices and processes used to manage and control a company.[^s6] Whatever the wording, the core question is the same: how do the people who supply capital make sure that the people who run the firm use it well?

## Origin and development

The economic framing of the field was crystallised by Andrei Shleifer and Robert Vishny, whose survey (NBER working paper 1996; *Journal of Finance*, June 1997) reviewed research on governance by organising it around two forces: the legal protection given to investors and the concentration of ownership.[^s2] Their comparative lens explained why governance systems differ so sharply across countries.

On the policy side, modern codes trace to the United Kingdom's Cadbury Report of 1992, which the Institute of Directors calls the seminal starting point of UK governance; the collapses of BCCI and Maxwell in 1991 had strengthened the case for common standards of transparency and accountability.[^s5] Cadbury and its successors, including the Higgs and Smith reviews feeding the 2003 Combined Code and the 2009 Walker review of banks and financial institutions, evolved into the UK Corporate Governance Code, which promotes non-executive directors as a source of challenge to executives.[^s5] The Financial Reporting Council issued its most recent update to the Code in January 2024.[^s6]

Internationally, the OECD Council first adopted its Principles of Corporate Governance in 1999, revised them in 2004 and 2015, and adopted the current version on 8 June 2023; G20 leaders endorsed that revision in September 2023.[^s1]

## How it works

The G20/OECD Principles group the building blocks of a sound framework into six chapters: an effective legal and regulatory basis; shareholder rights and equitable treatment; institutional investors, stock markets and other intermediaries; disclosure and transparency; the responsibilities of the board; and sustainability and resilience. The 2023 revision was meant to update the text for developments such as sustainability, ownership concentration, the growth of institutional investors and digitalisation.[^s1] They are addressed mainly to policymakers but also guide stock exchanges, investors and companies, with the stated aim of supporting economic efficiency, sustainable growth and financial stability.[^s1]

In practice, governance mechanisms divide into internal ones (board composition and independence, committee structures, executive pay, audit) and external ones (shareholder voting, the market for corporate control, disclosure rules and legal enforcement). Shleifer and Vishny's survey highlights two of the most consequential levers across countries: how well the law protects outside investors, and how concentrated ownership is.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The best-known empirical test is Gompers, Ishii and Metrick's Governance Index, built from 24 takeover-defence and shareholder-rights provisions across roughly 1,500 US firms in the 1990s. Buying the strongest-rights decile and shorting the weakest earned an abnormal return of 8.5% a year, and weaker rights went with lower Tobin's Q, lower profits and sales growth, higher capital spending and more acquisitions.[^s3]

Later work refined rather than overturned that finding. Bebchuk, Cohen and Ferrell showed that six "entrenchment" provisions (staggered boards, limits on bylaw amendments, poison pills, golden parachutes and two supermajority rules) account for the negative link with value over 1990–2007; the other 18 provisions showed no such relationship.[^s4] The lesson for practitioners is that governance checklists can mislead: a few provisions that insulate managers from removal matter far more than many cosmetic ones. Critics also note that these indices capture mainly US takeover-era mechanics, that correlations may partly reflect reverse causality (which Bebchuk and colleagues test for), and that the data predate the 2008 crisis and the later rise of stewardship and sustainability expectations.[^s4] The OECD's addition of a sustainability chapter reflects a broader debate over whether governance should serve shareholders alone or a wider set of stakeholders.[^s1]

## In practice

The UK experience illustrates how governance reform typically follows failure: corporate collapses in 1991 prompted Cadbury, and the Walker review of 2009 sought a larger role for non-executives in bank risk and pay, followed in 2010 by the first Stewardship Code for institutional investors.[^s5] Investors and proxy advisers today routinely screen for the entrenchment provisions that the research flags, and boards benchmark themselves against national codes on independence, remuneration and accountability.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Corporate governance is the practical response to the **principal-agent problem** created by the **separation of ownership and control**; its mechanisms exist to reduce **agency costs**.[^s2] Its scope is contested between **shareholder primacy** and **stakeholder theory**, a tension now visible in the OECD's sustainability chapter and in **ESG** practice.[^s1] The **board of directors** is its central internal institution, the **Cadbury Report** and the **UK Corporate Governance Code** are landmark policy instruments, and the **entrenchment index** is a widely used measure of governance quality.[^s4][^s5]
