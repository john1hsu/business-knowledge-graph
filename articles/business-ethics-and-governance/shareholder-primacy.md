# Shareholder Primacy: The Corporate Objective of Maximizing Shareholder Value

**Shareholder primacy** is the doctrine that a for-profit corporation should be run for the benefit of its shareholders, so the yardstick for directors' and managers' decisions is the wealth those decisions create for the owners. Its best-known statement is Milton Friedman's 1970 essay in *The New York Times Magazine*, which argued that a corporate executive is an employee of the owners and should generally try to make as much money as possible while obeying the law and basic ethical custom.[^s1] In finance it appears as the textbook goal of maximizing firm or share value; in law and governance it is the position that directors' duties run primarily to shareholders.

## Origin and intellectual roots

An early judicial landmark is the dispute between Henry Ford and the Dodge brothers, minority shareholders who sued in 1916 after Ford cut prices and poured profits into expansion instead of dividends. The Michigan court ruled for the Dodges, stated in 1919 that a business exists for the profit of its stockholders, and ordered a dividend of about $19.3 million plus interest; Ford then bought out all minority holders for nearly $106 million.[^s5]

Friedman supplied the economic and political argument. When executives spend company money on social goals such as fighting inflation or pollution, he wrote, they are in effect levying taxes and choosing how to spend them, a governmental role that unelected managers should not play. Business therefore has one social responsibility: to increase profits while staying within "open and free competition without deception or fraud."[^s1]

## How it works

The doctrine gives managers a single objective. Michael Jensen's defence, published in 2001, holds that purposeful choice needs one criterion because no one can maximize in several dimensions at once, and that when there are no externalities or monopoly, social welfare is best served when each firm maximizes its total long-run market value.[^s2] Jensen's version, which he called **enlightened value maximization**, does not ignore employees, customers or communities; it treats them as means to long-run value and uses that value to settle trade-offs among them.[^s2] He also argued that multi-measure tools such as the balanced scorecard help managers understand value drivers but should not set pay, because they lack a single score.[^s2]

The doctrine leans on the agency view of the firm, in which shareholders are principals and directors and managers are their agents.[^s4] Governance tools such as shareholder-elected boards, equity-based executive pay and shareholder voting on corporate policy are then the means of keeping agents focused on owners.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest legal critique comes from Lynn Stout, who argued in 2012 that US corporate law imposes no enforceable duty on public-company directors to maximize profits or share price, and that the economic case rests on mistaken premises: that shareholders own the corporation, are its only residual claimants, and act as principals over directors.[^s4] She found little persuasive evidence that firms run for shareholder value outperform over time, and argued that chasing share price encourages asset sales, R&D cuts, large pay packages and leveraged buybacks that harm long-term and diversified shareholders.[^s4]

A second critique comes from within economics. Oliver Hart and Luigi Zingales argued in 2017 that when shareholders care about social outcomes and some externalities cannot be separated from production, maximizing shareholder **welfare** is not the same as maximizing market value; firms should follow investors' preferences, for example through shareholder votes on corporate policy.[^s3] Stakeholder theorists go further, rejecting the single objective entirely, which is precisely the indeterminacy Jensen objected to.[^s2]

## In practice: the Business Roundtable reversal

The Business Roundtable's 1997 statement declared that the paramount duty of management and boards is to the corporation's stockholders. In August 2019, 181 of its 193 member CEOs signed a new statement committing to customers, employees, suppliers, communities and shareholders without ranking them.[^s6] Lawyers who advised the group said the statement did not change directors' fiduciary duties to shareholders, and several states' constituency statutes already let boards weigh other groups' interests.[^s6]

## Connections

Shareholder primacy is a position within **corporate governance** and **corporate law** (`broader`), answering the question of whose interests the corporation serves.[^s4] It is the standard foil for **stakeholder theory** (`contrastsWith`), and **ESG** investing raised the questions Hart and Zingales address about prosocial shareholders.[^s3] It rests on agency reasoning: **agency costs** and the **board of directors** are the mechanisms for keeping managers focused on owners, while **fiduciary duty** and the **business judgment rule** shape how far courts actually enforce it, which Stout argues is not far at all.[^s2][^s4] It was shaped by **Milton Friedman** and **Michael C. Jensen** (`influencedBy`) and is exemplified by **Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.** and the **1997 Business Roundtable statement**.[^s1][^s5][^s6]
