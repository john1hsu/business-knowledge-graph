# Separation of Ownership and Control

The separation of ownership and control describes a large company whose legal owners, its shareholders, are too numerous and too small individually to direct it, so that salaried managers holding little equity make the real decisions. Adolf Berle, a corporate lawyer, and Gardiner Means, an economist, made the idea famous in *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* (1932), arguing that this split was a defining feature of big American corporations; the view quickly hardened into conventional wisdom about U.S. corporate governance.[^s1]

## Origin and evidence

Means's statistical work on the 200 largest U.S. corporations underpinned the book. By 1929, on the classification later summarized by Becht and DeLong, only about 11% of these firms were still controlled by large blockholders; roughly 44% were run by incumbents whose stakes had shrunk sharply, and in another 44% management had allegedly become self-perpetuating, choosing the directors who were supposed to oversee it.[^s2] The worry was that shareholders had become passive providers of capital while managers could pursue size, perquisites or security rather than value for the owners.[^s2]

## How it works

Eugene Fama and Michael Jensen (1983) reframed the separation as an efficient division of labor rather than a pathology. Freely traded common stock lets residual risk be spread across many diversified investors and lets firms hire specialized managers who need not be wealthy. The cost is an agency problem, which surviving open corporations contain by splitting *decision management* (initiating and implementing choices) from *decision control* (ratifying and monitoring them).[^s3] Control sits in decision hierarchies, mutual monitoring among executives, a board of directors that hires, pays and fires top managers, stock prices that signal performance, and, as a last resort, an outside takeover market in which bidders can bypass incumbents through tender offers or proxy fights.[^s3] Small firms, by contrast, combine management and risk-bearing in the same people and restrict ownership to them.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Harold Demsetz (1983) argued that ownership structure is an endogenous outcome of value maximization, so diffuse ownership need not mean worse performance: for very large firms, the risk-sharing benefits of dispersion can outweigh higher monitoring costs. His data showed management stakes of about 2% in the ten largest Fortune 500 firms but far higher stakes in smaller companies.[^s4]

International evidence narrowed the thesis further. Studying large firms in 27 wealthy economies, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer (1999) found that widely held firms are relatively rare outside countries with very good shareholder protection; most are controlled by families or the state, often through pyramids that give control rights well beyond cash-flow rights.[^s5] Explanations for why the U.S. ended up so dispersed include Mark Roe's political account of hostility to concentrated financial power, banking and fund regulation, deep equity markets and historical accident, none sufficient alone.[^s2] Historians have also questioned how universal the Berle-Means picture ever was; Cheffins and Bank conclude that U.S. ownership patterns are mixed but that the separation remains a sound reference point for analyzing American governance.[^s1]

## In practice

Founder-controlled technology companies show the separation can be engineered away even in widely traded firms. Meta Platforms' Class A shares carry one vote each and its Class B shares ten; as of April 1, 2026, Mark Zuckerberg held 99.8% of Class B and 60.8% of total voting power, making Meta a "controlled company" under Nasdaq rules.[^s6] Here public investors bear most of the economic risk while one insider holds control, the reverse of Berle and Means's manager-dominated firm, and outside shareholders lose the takeover discipline that Fama and Jensen treat as the final check.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

The separation is the structural root of the **principal-agent problem** in public companies and the condition that produces **agency costs**, which arise once decision-makers no longer bear most of the wealth effects of their choices.[^s3] It sits under **corporate governance**, whose mechanisms, above all the **board of directors** and the **market for corporate control**, exist to keep separated managers accountable.[^s3] Debates over **shareholder primacy** presuppose that owners and managers are different people, while **dual-class shares** show how founders can keep voting control through high-vote share classes.[^s6] Concentrated-ownership systems abroad reframe the central conflict as one between controlling and minority shareholders.[^s5]
