# Board of Directors

A board of directors is the group of people, elected by shareholders, in whom corporate law vests the power to run a corporation. Under Delaware's General Corporation Law, the statute that governs most large US public companies, a corporation's business and affairs are managed "by or under the direction of a board of directors" unless the charter says otherwise.[^s1] In practice the board delegates day-to-day management to officers it appoints and keeps for itself strategy, oversight and the hiring, pay and removal of the chief executive.[^s4] The OECD frames the job as guiding strategy, monitoring management and being accountable to the company and its shareholders, while exercising objective and independent judgement.[^s4]

## Origin and rationale

Boards long predate securities regulation, and they usually have more members than the law requires, which suggests firms adopt them because they solve a real problem rather than because a rule forces them to.[^s2] Benjamin Hermalin and Michael Weisbach trace that problem to Adam Smith (1776) and to Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means (1932): once ownership is spread among many shareholders, managers end up controlling other people's money.[^s2] On this view the board is an endogenous institution, a market-chosen and possibly second-best answer to the agency problem between diffuse owners and professional managers.[^s2]

## How it works

Statute sets the skeleton. In Delaware a board needs at least one director, every director must be a natural person, and a majority of the full board is normally a quorum, though bylaws may lower it to no less than one-third.[^s1] The board may form committees that exercise its powers within limits it sets, but for corporations formed after mid-1996 a committee cannot amend bylaws or approve matters that require a shareholder vote.[^s1] A charter or bylaw may stagger the board into up to three classes, so only part of it stands for election each year. Shareholders holding a majority of voting shares can normally remove directors with or without cause, but directors on a classified board can, by default, be removed only for cause.[^s1] Directors may rely in good faith on company records and on reports from officers and experts they reasonably believe competent, which shields them when they act on information others prepared.[^s1]

Two broad models exist worldwide. The unitary (one-tier) board common in the US and UK mixes executive and non-executive members, while two-tier systems separate a supervisory board of mainly non-executives, sometimes including employee representatives, from a management board of executives.[^s4] Across both, the OECD expects directors to act on a fully informed basis, in good faith, with due diligence and care, and with loyalty to the company and all shareholders rather than to a controlling holder.[^s4]

For US listed companies, exchange rules add independence requirements. The New York Stock Exchange rules, as proposed to the SEC in April 2003, require a majority of independent directors, meaning directors the board affirmatively finds have no material relationship with the company.[^s3] They also require regular executive sessions without management and fully independent nominating/governance and compensation committees, with an exemption for controlled companies in which one holder has more than half the voting power.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence that board structure drives performance is weaker than governance codes assume. Hermalin and Weisbach's survey finds no clear cross-sectional link between the share of outside directors and either accounting returns or Tobin's Q.[^s2] Board size looks more important: David Yermack found a significant negative relationship between board size and Tobin's Q among large US firms.[^s2] The strongest evidence concerns monitoring. Boards dominated by outsiders are more likely to replace a CEO after poor performance than insider-dominated boards, whose turnover decisions show no clear link to results.[^s2]

The central critique is endogeneity. Board composition responds to performance, since weak results tend to bring more independent directors, so simple correlations between governance and outcomes can mislead and may not be causal.[^s2] Critics also note that independence on paper does not guarantee informed oversight, because outside directors depend on information management supplies, which is why legal protection for good-faith reliance is paired with duties of care and loyalty.[^s1][^s4]

## In practice

Board leadership is one visible place where practice has shifted. As of the 2026 proxy season, 59% of S&P 500 boards separated the chair and CEO roles and 41% had an independent chair, according to the Spencer Stuart Board Index; those boards added 364 new directors in 2026.[^s5] Splitting the roles responds directly to the monitoring logic above: a CEO who also chairs the board sets the agenda for the body meant to evaluate him or her.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

The board is the core mechanism of **corporate governance** (`broader`) and the place where **fiduciary duty** attaches. It exists to reduce the **principal-agent problem** and the **agency costs** that follow from the **separation of ownership and control**.[^s2] Its powers come from state corporate law such as the **Delaware General Corporation Law**, and exchange standards shape its composition, including its **independent directors**.[^s1][^s3] The **audit committee** is one of its standing committees. Whom the board ultimately serves is the dispute between **shareholder primacy** and **stakeholder theory**; the 2023 OECD Principles ask boards to weigh stakeholder interests while owing loyalty to the company and its shareholders.[^s4]
