# Business Ethics

**Business ethics** examines the moral dimensions of commerce. In Jeffrey Moriarty's *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry, it studies the ethical side of exchanging goods and services and of the entities that offer them, covering production, distribution, marketing, sale and consumption.[^s1] The OpenStax *Introduction to Business* text frames ethics as a set of moral standards for judging right and wrong, and says an ethical issue arises whenever someone must choose between actions that may be ethical or unethical.[^s2] The term covers two related things: an academic field, and the codes of conduct and ethics-and-compliance functions that many firms run in practice.[^s1]

## Origin

People have argued about the ethics of trade for millennia; Moriarty points as far back as the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BC).[^s1] As an academic discipline, however, business ethics is young: it grew out of work by moral philosophers in the 1970s and 1980s.[^s1] Two contributions from that period shaped the field. Philip French's 1979 argument that corporations can be moral agents in their own right is described as seminal, and R. Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory followed in the 1980s.[^s1] Moriarty also notes a later shift: philosophers have gradually retreated from the field as business schools replaced retiring philosophers with social scientists, so empirical "behavioral ethics" now carries much of the research.[^s1]

## How it works: normative and descriptive questions

The field asks two kinds of question. **Normative** business ethics asks what is actually right or wrong in business; **descriptive** work, done mostly by social scientists, asks why people act unethically or whether ethical conduct pays.[^s1] The main normative topics include:

- **Corporate moral agency**: whether a firm, and not only its employees, can be held morally responsible; French said yes, while Donaldson and Velasquez raised objections.[^s1]
- **Corporate purpose**: whether managers should maximize shareholder wealth, as Milton Friedman argued in 1970, or balance the interests of all stakeholders.[^s1]
- **Markets and marketing**: the limits of what may be sold, product safety, price discrimination and gouging, and deceptive versus merely puffed-up advertising.[^s1]
- **Employment**: hiring and firing, discrimination, executive pay, sweatshops, meaningful work and whistleblowing.[^s1]

For individual decisions, OpenStax summarizes the standard moral lenses. **Utilitarianism** judges an act by its consequences, seeking the greatest good for the greatest number, but outcomes are hard to predict and someone usually loses.[^s2] **Deontology** stresses duties such as keeping promises and obeying the law, which gives consistency but can leave a person caught between conflicting duties.[^s2] **Justice** (fairness) and **individual rights**, both human and legal, complete the toolkit, and the surrounding workplace and culture also shape what people do.[^s2] OpenStax recommends first recognizing that a situation raises an ethical issue, using categories such as lying, creating false impressions, conflicts of interest, abuse of power and condoning others' misconduct, and then applying these lenses.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Behavioral research suggests that unethical conduct is not just a matter of character. Kish-Gephart, Harrison and Treviño's 2010 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* pooled more than 30 years of studies and organized the causes of unethical choice into "bad apples" (individuals), "bad cases" (features of the moral issue) and "bad barrels" (the organizational environment).[^s3] They found many interacting determinants, and several variables predicted actual unethical behavior better than stated intentions.[^s3] That led them to argue for giving more weight to a fast "ethical impulse" view, not only the traditional deliberate "ethical calculus."[^s3] The practical lesson is that codes and training aimed at individuals are insufficient if incentives and culture push the other way.

Survey data point the same way. The Ethics & Compliance Initiative's U.S. Global Business Ethics Survey, released in January 2025 from responses of more than 2,800 employees, found that in 2023 46% had observed misconduct and 17% felt pressure to compromise standards, down from 29% in 2020.[^s4] Only 64% of those who saw misconduct reported it (versus 71% in 2020), 49% perceived retaliation, and positive views of ethical culture fell to 56%, the lowest in the survey's decade of data.[^s4] Employees in weak cultures were four times as likely to feel pressure to cut corners.[^s4]

The field's critics come from two sides. Friedman's school holds that managers who pursue social goals with owners' money overstep their role and are not experts in solving social problems.[^s1] Others note that much of what firms call ethics is compliance work, which philosophers often set aside to focus on deeper normative questions.[^s1]

## In practice

Classic cases show the stakes. In the 1980s Beech-Nut sold a drink containing no juice as "100% apple juice"; the company was fined $2 million and two executives went to prison, illustrating the line between puffery and deception.[^s1] OpenStax cites Martin Shkreli's price increase of more than 5,000 percent on a drug as conduct most people regard as unethical, even though it was legal.[^s2] Firms respond with codes of conduct, ethics officers, hotlines and anti-retaliation policies, while professionals such as accountants and lawyers are also bound by professional codes.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Business ethics is a branch of **applied ethics** (`broader`) and draws on **utilitarianism** and **deontology** as decision lenses.[^s1][^s2] Its components include **corporate moral agency**, **whistleblowing** and the empirical study of **behavioral ethics**.[^s1][^s3] The corporate-purpose debate links it directly to **stakeholder theory** and **shareholder primacy**, and **corporate social responsibility** is its organizational expression beyond legal duty.[^s1] **Corporate governance** supplies the oversight structures that enforce ethical standards, and the **Enron scandal** is a case often studied for the combined failure of culture and controls; the meta-analytic "bad barrels" finding explains why such failures are organizational, not only individual.[^s3]
