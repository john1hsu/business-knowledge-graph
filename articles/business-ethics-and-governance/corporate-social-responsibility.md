# Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

**Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** is the view that a business owes society more than reliable products, fair prices and fair wages: it should also attend to its environmental footprint and to the social concerns its operations touch.[^s2] The European Commission's working definition is compact, calling CSR "the responsibility of enterprises for their impacts on society," and it expects firms to fold social, environmental, ethical, consumer and human-rights concerns into strategy and operations while complying with the law.[^s4] The Commission also adheres to the OECD's closely related notion of *responsible business conduct*.[^s4]

## Origin

Archie B. Carroll, a University of Georgia management professor and the field's leading historian of the concept, dates the modern CSR era to Howard Bowen's 1953 book *Social Responsibilities of the Businessman*.[^s1] The idea drew early opposition: Theodore Levitt argued in 1958 that a firm's main duty is profit, and Milton Friedman objected in 1962 that managers have no mandate to pursue social goals with shareholders' money.[^s1] Definitions multiplied through the 1960s as civil-rights, consumer and environmental movements pressed on business.[^s1] Public concern about corporate pollution, sharpened by Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* in 1962, is one example of the outside pressure that pushed firms to account for externalities.[^s2]

## How it works: Carroll's pyramid

Carroll proposed a four-part definition in 1979 and recast it as a pyramid in a 1991 *Business Horizons* article, which remains the most widely used model of CSR.[^s1] Its layers are:[^s1]

1. **Economic** responsibilities (base): being profitable, which sustains the firm and rewards its owners; required by society.[^s1]
2. **Legal** responsibilities: obeying the laws and regulations that codify society's basic rules; also required.[^s1]
3. **Ethical** responsibilities: doing what is fair and right beyond what law demands; expected by society.[^s1]
4. **Philanthropic** responsibilities (top): voluntary giving and good corporate citizenship; desired rather than demanded.[^s1]

Carroll stresses that the pyramid is an integrated whole rather than a sequence: firms are meant to fulfil all four layers at once, and ethics runs through every layer, including how profits are earned.[^s1] A firm that gives little to charity is not usually called unethical, but one that ignores ethical expectations is.[^s1] Other scholars have adapted the model, for instance reordering the layers for firms in developing economies or for small businesses.[^s1]

A related measurement idea is the **triple bottom line**, described by consultant John Elkington in 1994, which asks companies to report social ("people") and environmental ("planet") results alongside financial ones.[^s2]

## Strategic CSR and shared value

Michael Porter and Mark Kramer argued in *Harvard Business Review* (December 2006) that typical CSR programmes are generic, treat business and society as adversaries, and so often backfire as public relations or damage control.[^s5] They proposed analysing social issues with the same frameworks used for core strategy, choosing initiatives that strengthen the company's competitive context, and treating CSR as a source of innovation and advantage; they cite Whole Foods Market, Toyota and Volvo as examples.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The "business case" for CSR rests partly on research linking corporate social performance (CSP) to corporate financial performance (CFP). Orlitzky, Schmidt and Rynes's meta-analysis of 52 studies (33,878 observations), published in *Organization Studies* in 2003, found a positive association, stronger for accounting-based than market-based returns, with corporate reputation acting as an important mediator.[^s3] The authors also note that reputation indices may partly reflect halo effects, and the average correlation shrinks markedly when survey- and reputation-based measures are excluded, so the size of the payoff is debated.[^s3]

Critics raise two further concerns. Friedman's shareholder-primacy objection holds that social spending by managers is a tax on owners.[^s1] Ethicists warn that much CSR is superficial **greenwashing**: OpenStax uses Ben & Jerry's dairy sourcing after its growth and Coca-Cola's water use in Chiapas, Mexico, to show why company sustainability claims should not be taken at face value.[^s2] Carroll himself acknowledges that short-term CSR costs can appear to conflict with shareholder interests, a tension the business-case literature tries to dissolve.[^s1]

## In practice

Firms operationalise CSR through codes of conduct, supply-chain standards, environmental targets, community investment and sustainability reporting. Regulation increasingly turns voluntary practice into disclosure duties: California's Transparency in Supply Chains Act, for example, requires covered firms to disclose what they do about working conditions among suppliers, though it mandates disclosure rather than specific conduct.[^s2] In the EU, CSR policy has been organised around the Commission's renewed CSR strategy adopted in 2011.[^s4]

## Connections

CSR is a central topic within **business ethics** and bears on **corporate governance** (`broader`) because it asks whose interests the firm should serve.[^s1] It is the practical counterpart of **stakeholder theory**, which supplies the reason a firm owes duties to groups beyond owners, and it **contrasts with shareholder primacy**, the position Friedman defended against it.[^s1][^s2] **ESG** translated CSR ideas into factors investors can price, the **triple bottom line** is a reporting lens for it, and Porter and Kramer's **creating shared value** is a strategic reframing of it.[^s2][^s5] The concept is credited to **Howard Bowen**'s 1953 book, and Carroll's **pyramid of CSR** is its best-known component model.[^s1] The **Business Roundtable Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation** is a later public test of how far large firms embrace these commitments.
