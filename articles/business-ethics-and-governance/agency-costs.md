# Agency Costs

Agency costs are the losses and expenses that arise whenever one party (the principal) hires another (the agent) to act on its behalf and hands over some decision-making authority. Because both sides are assumed to maximize their own utility, the agent will not always act in the principal's best interest, and the principal pays, one way or another, for that gap.[^s1] The canonical setting is the public corporation, where dispersed shareholders delegate control to professional managers, though the logic applies to any delegated relationship, including lending and employment.[^s5]

## Origin

The concept was formalized by Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling in "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure," published in the *Journal of Financial Economics* in 1976. The paper fused agency theory, property-rights theory and finance theory to explain why firms have the ownership structures they do, and it explicitly tied agency costs to the separation of ownership and control.[^s2] It also redefined the firm as a legal fiction that serves as a nexus for a set of contracts among individuals.[^s1]

## How it works

Jensen and Meckling define agency costs as the sum of three components.[^s1]

- **Monitoring expenditures** by the principal to limit the agent's divergent behavior, including budget restrictions, compensation policies and operating rules.[^s1]
- **Bonding expenditures** by the agent to guarantee that it will not take harmful actions, or that it will compensate the principal if it does.[^s1][^s5]
- **Residual loss**, the dollar value of the welfare the principal still loses because, even after monitoring and bonding, the agent's decisions differ from those that would maximize the principal's welfare.[^s1]

Their central illustration is an owner-manager who sells part of the firm. Once he owns, say, 95 percent rather than all of the equity, he bears only 95 cents of each dollar spent on perquisites such as plush offices or a larger staff, so he consumes more of them, and he also puts less effort into creative, value-creating work.[^s1] Rational outside buyers anticipate this and pay only for the firm's value given the manager's changed behavior, so the selling owner-manager ultimately bears the decline. That is why managers have an incentive to offer bonding devices, such as audited financial reports, that reduce the discount.[^s1][^s5]

Debt creates its own agency costs. A heavily levered owner can promise lenders a safe project and then switch to a riskier one with the same expected payoff, transferring wealth from bondholders to shareholders, because equity behaves like a call option on the firm and gains value from higher variance. Lenders who foresee this price the risk in, and covenants and monitoring become the remedies.[^s1] Jensen and Meckling argued that these costs give capital structure real consequences even though, under perfect-market assumptions, the debt-equity mix would not affect firm value.[^s1]

In 1986 Jensen extended the idea to **free cash flow**: cash beyond what a firm can invest in positive-value projects. Conflicts over firm size and payouts are most severe where free cash flow is large, and debt can discipline managers by committing that cash to creditors, acting as a substitute for dividends. He used the framework to explain why diversification programs tend to destroy value and why takeover waves hit cash-rich industries such as oil, broadcasting and tobacco.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical work generally supports the link between ownership and agency costs, with qualifications. Singh and Davidson (2003), studying large U.S. public firms, found that higher managerial ownership was associated with better asset utilization but did not significantly curb excessive discretionary expenses; smaller boards helped reduce agency costs, while independent outside directors and outside blockholders showed limited effect.[^s4] The free-cash-flow version has proved durable: a 2025 Chicago Booth working paper by Douglas Skinner argues Jensen's 1986 analysis transformed how financial economists understand corporate payout policy.[^s6]

Critics target the model's foundations. Its assumption of narrowly self-interested actors is contested among economists, a limitation the original authors acknowledged, and principals rarely observe agents' actions fully, which limits how far monitoring can go.[^s5] Jensen and Meckling themselves warned against treating agency costs as simple waste: they are an unavoidable cost of delegation, and comparing real firms with a frictionless ideal is misleading.[^s1]

## In practice

Agency-cost reasoning underpins incentive pay, independent audits, board oversight, bond covenants and performance contracts.[^s5] Its most dramatic application was the 1980s wave of leveraged buyouts and takeovers, which Jensen interpreted as markets forcing cash-rich firms, notably in the oil industry, to pay out free cash flow rather than spend it on low-return expansion.[^s3] Share repurchases, a direct way of returning free cash flow, have since become politically contested.[^s6]

## Connections

Agency costs are a core concept of **corporate governance** and the measurable consequence of the **principal-agent problem**; they depend on the **separation of ownership and control** in widely held firms.[^s2] The idea was created by **Michael C. Jensen** and **William H. Meckling** and introduced in their **1976 theory-of-the-firm paper**, which challenged the irrelevance result of the **Modigliani-Miller theorem** by showing why financing choices matter.[^s1] Jensen's later work links agency costs to **free cash flow**, **capital structure** and the **leveraged buyout**.[^s3] Later scholars stretched agency analysis to organizations' ethical and social costs, making it a reference point in debates over **stakeholder theory**.[^s5] Governance mechanisms such as board size and managerial ownership are judged partly by how well they reduce these costs.[^s4]
