# Business Roundtable Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation

The **Business Roundtable Statement on the Purpose of a Corporation** is a one-page declaration released on August 19, 2019 by the Business Roundtable (BRT), an association of chief executives of major U.S. companies. Signed at launch by 181 CEOs, it committed their companies to serve five groups: delivering value to customers, investing in employees, dealing fairly and ethically with suppliers, supporting the communities where they operate, and generating long-term value for shareholders.[^s1] It is widely treated as the highest-profile corporate endorsement of **stakeholder theory** and a public break with **shareholder primacy**.

## Origin

The BRT has issued periodic Principles of Corporate Governance since 1978. According to the BRT, every version from 1997 onward had endorsed shareholder primacy, the view that companies exist principally to serve their shareholders; the 2019 statement expressly superseded that language.[^s1] The BRT's announcement featured Johnson & Johnson CEO Alex Gorsky, chair of its Corporate Governance Committee, and JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon, chair of the Roundtable itself.[^s1] Bebchuk and Tallarita put the combined market capitalization of the signing companies at more than $13 trillion at the time of release.[^s6]

## What the statement says and does not say

The text sets out the five stakeholder commitments side by side, with shareholders listed last and framed in terms of long-term value.[^s1] It changes no legal duty: Bebchuk and Tallarita note that it is silent on whether stakeholders should get anything beyond what also serves shareholder value, and on the corporate-law constraints that keep many boards from treating stakeholder interests as ends in themselves.[^s6] The BRT's own fifth-anniversary essay in August 2024 framed the pledge narrowly, as covering the stakeholders who contribute directly to a firm's success rather than every social problem, and argued that purpose and profit are complementary rather than competing goals.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Beyond the initial public debate, the most cited scholarly responses tested whether the statement changed how signatories were governed or behaved.[^s2][^s4]

- **No board involvement.** Lucian Bebchuk and Roberto Tallarita of Harvard Law School contacted 173 of the 184 signatory companies; of the 48 that answered, 47 said the CEO alone decided to sign and only one reported board approval.[^s2]
- **Unchanged governance documents.** Reviewing the governance guidelines of the 20 companies whose CEOs sat on the BRT board, they found as of September 2020 that half had amended their guidelines after the statement, but only one (S&P Global) had changed its statement of corporate purpose, and several still told directors to act for shareholders.[^s2]
- **The broader argument.** Bebchuk and Tallarita conclude that managers' incentives make the pledge mostly a matter of public relations, and that "stakeholderism" could weaken accountability to shareholders while delaying regulation that would protect stakeholders more effectively.[^s3]
- **Conduct of signatories.** Aneesh Raghunandan and Shiva Rajgopal, in the *Journal of Law and Economics* (2024), compared signatories with industry peers and found more environmental and labor-law violations, higher carbon emissions and greater reliance on government subsidies, with no improvement after signing; they read the statement as not a credible commitment.[^s4]

Defenders, including the BRT, reply that the statement describes how well-run companies already operate and that long-term shareholder value depends on treating workers, customers and suppliers well.[^s5]

## In practice

For managers and directors the episode is a case study in the gap between **corporate purpose statements** and the mechanisms that actually govern a firm: charters, board guidelines, executive pay and shareholder voting. A board that wants a stakeholder commitment to bind would need to write it into those instruments, and the evidence above suggests few signatories did so.[^s2] For investors, the case illustrates why stated commitments on **ESG** or social responsibility are usually tested against measurable conduct rather than accepted at face value.[^s4]

## Connections

- **Broader:** It is an episode within **corporate governance** (who the firm is run for) and **business ethics** (what firms owe non-owners).[^s1]
- **Exemplifies / contrasts with:** It exemplifies **stakeholder theory** and contrasts with **shareholder primacy**, which the BRT's 1997 statement had endorsed.[^s1]
- **Related:** Its claims connect to **fiduciary duty** and the **board of directors**, since Bebchuk and Tallarita's critique turns on legal duties and the absence of board approval;[^s2] and to **corporate social responsibility** and **ESG**, which supply the yardsticks scholars used to judge signatories' conduct.[^s4]
