# ESG

ESG stands for environmental, social and governance, the three families of non-financial issues that investors and companies use to judge how a business manages risks and opportunities outside the traditional financial statements. Environmental factors cover matters such as climate and pollution; social factors cover relations with workers, customers and communities; governance factors cover boards, shareholder rights and controls. The core claim of the framework is that these issues are value drivers, so they belong inside mainstream financial research and investment decisions rather than in a niche reserved for ethically motivated investors.[^s1]

## Origin

The label grew out of the UN Global Compact's "Who Cares Wins" initiative. Its report, *Who Cares Wins: Connecting Financial Markets to a Changing World*, was published and endorsed in June 2004 by a group of 18 financial institutions from nine countries that together managed more than US$6 trillion in assets, with signatories including ABN Amro, Goldman Sachs, HSBC, Morgan Stanley and UBS alongside IFC and the World Bank Group.[^s1] The report set out guidelines for weaving ESG considerations into asset management, brokerage and sell-side research, and addressed recommendations to companies, analysts, pension funds, stock exchanges, regulators and consultants. A 2005 follow-up conference in Zurich, co-sponsored by the Swiss government and IFC, reviewed early progress and found that many market participants still needed persuading that integration added value rather than costing returns.[^s1]

## How it works

ESG operates on two sides of the capital market. On the investor side, asset managers integrate ESG data into security analysis, screening, engagement and proxy voting, usually relying on scores from commercial rating agencies. On the corporate side, companies disclose sustainability information so that investors can assess it. The disclosure side was consolidated globally on 26 June 2023, when the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), an independent standard-setter within the IFRS Foundation, issued IFRS S1, which sets general requirements for reporting sustainability-related risks and opportunities over the short, medium and long term, and IFRS S2, which adds climate-specific disclosures and fully incorporates the recommendations of the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures.[^s4] The ISSB framed both standards around investor decision-usefulness and global comparability, which makes them a sibling of financial-reporting standards rather than a general social-responsibility code.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The empirical case is mixed but not hostile. Gunnar Friede, Timo Busch and Alexander Bassen aggregated roughly 2,200 studies of the link between ESG and corporate financial performance and found that about 90% report a non-negative relationship, with most reporting a positive one (2015).[^s3] Measurement is the main weakness. Florian Berg, Julian Kölbel and Roberto Rigobon compared ratings from six major providers — KLD, Sustainalytics, Moody's ESG (Vigeo-Eiris), S&P Global (RobecoSAM), Refinitiv (Asset4) and MSCI — and decomposed their disagreement: measurement differences explained 56% of the divergence, differences in scope 38% and differences in weighting just 6%. They also detected a "rater effect," in which an agency's overall view of a firm colours how it scores individual categories.[^s2] Because the same company can look strong to one rater and weak to another, critics argue that ESG labels give investors noisy signals and managers ambiguous targets.[^s2] A political critique holds that mandated ESG disclosure is costly and intrusive, an objection that shaped U.S. regulation after 2024.[^s5]

## In practice

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's climate rule shows how contested ESG disclosure has become. The Commission adopted rules on climate-related risk and greenhouse-gas emissions disclosure on 6 March 2024, then stayed them while states and private parties challenged them in consolidated litigation, *Iowa v. SEC*, in the Eighth Circuit. On 27 March 2025 the Commission voted to stop defending the rules, and Acting Chairman Mark Uyeda described them as costly and unnecessarily intrusive.[^s5] The episode illustrates the gap that opened between the U.S. federal approach, where the rules remained stayed and undefended as of March 2025, and the investor-focused global baseline the ISSB had published in 2023.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

ESG sits under **corporate social responsibility** and **sustainable finance**: it translates broad responsibility ideas into factors that capital markets can price.[^s1] It was **introduced in the Who Cares Wins report**[^s1] and is **influenced by stakeholder theory**, since its social and environmental pillars track the interests of non-shareholder groups; it **contrasts with shareholder primacy** where ESG goals are pursued at shareholders' expense.[^s3] Its performance is **measured by ESG ratings**, whose divergence is its central methodological problem.[^s2] On the reporting side it is **regulated by the IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards**, issued under the same foundation as **IFRS**.[^s4] Its "G" pillar overlaps with **corporate governance** and with efforts to contain **agency costs**, and the SEC episode shows how securities regulators shape what ESG means in practice.[^s5]
