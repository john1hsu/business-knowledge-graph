# Executive Compensation: Pay-for-Performance, Equity Incentives and Say on Pay

**Executive compensation** covers how a company pays its most senior managers, above all the chief executive: fixed salary, annual bonuses, equity awards such as stock options and restricted or performance shares, and deferred items like pensions, perquisites and severance. In governance terms it is one of the main tools a board has for aligning managers who run the firm with the shareholders who own it, and also one of the most contested.[^s2][^s3] Researchers disagree about whether pay levels reflect efficient contracting in a competitive market for managerial talent or managers' influence over the boards that set their pay.[^s3]

## Origin and theory

The modern debate grew out of agency theory. If owners cannot observe everything a manager does, tying the manager's wealth to firm value gives the manager a reason to act as an owner would. Michael Jensen and Kevin Murphy put a number on this link in a 1990 *Journal of Political Economy* study. Counting salary, bonus, options, stockholdings and the threat of dismissal, they estimated that a typical CEO's wealth moved by only about $3.25 for every $1,000 change in shareholder wealth.[^s1] Most of that sensitivity came from stock ownership, yet most CEOs held a trivial fraction of their firm's shares, and the authors argued that public and private political pressures kept boards from paying for performance more aggressively.[^s1]

## How pay packages work

A package is set by the board, often with input from outside compensation consultants, and combines elements that do different jobs.[^s2][^s3] Salary is fixed. Bonuses reward annual targets. Equity awards tie wealth to the share price over several years, while pensions and severance shape behaviour at the end of a tenure.[^s3] Edmans, Gabaix and Jenter's survey of the field tracks how both the level and the mix of top-executive pay have changed over time and across firms, and how strong the resulting incentives are.[^s3] They also compare U.S. public companies with private firms and with companies abroad.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The leading critique is the **managerial power** view of Lucian Bebchuk and Jesse Fried. They argue that in widely held firms the board cannot bargain at arm's length with the CEO, so executives shape their own pay. Executives then prefer pay that is less visible and less tied to performance.[^s2] The view, they argue, explains features that efficient contracting finds puzzling. Examples include option plan design, "stealth" compensation, loans to executives, large exit payments, retirement benefits, the use of compensation consultants and the link between CEO power and pay levels.[^s2] Surveying both literatures, Edmans, Gabaix and Jenter weigh three explanations that are not mutually exclusive: boards maximizing shareholder value, rent extraction by executives, and institutional forces such as regulation, taxation and accounting rules. They conclude that no single explanation fits all the facts and trends.[^s3]

## Regulation and disclosure

In the United States, the Dodd-Frank Act made pay a matter for shareholder votes. Under rules the SEC adopted in January 2011, companies subject to the proxy rules must hold an advisory **say-on-pay** vote at least once every three years. Shareholders also vote at least once every six years on how often that vote should occur, and they get a separate advisory vote on merger-related golden parachutes. All three votes are non-binding.[^s4] In August 2022 the SEC added **pay-versus-performance** disclosure. Companies must tabulate executive "compensation actually paid" for the five latest fiscal years against total shareholder return, peer-group return, net income and one measure the company selects. They must also list three to seven measures they consider most important for linking pay to performance.[^s5]

## Real-world example

Citigroup became the first Wall Street bank to lose a say-on-pay vote at its April 17, 2012 annual meeting, when 55 percent of votes cast opposed its executive pay.[^s6] CEO Vikram Pandit had received $14.8 million for 2011, plus a $10 million retention award tied to non-financial goals. The proxy adviser ISS had recommended a vote against, partly because the retention award lacked financial metrics. Citigroup's shares had fallen about 90 percent between Pandit's appointment in December 2007 and the end of 2011.[^s6] The vote did not oblige the bank to change anything, but its board said it would consult shareholders about their concerns, an illustration of how advisory votes work through pressure rather than legal force.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Executive compensation sits under **corporate governance** because the board sets it and shareholders now vote on it.[^s4] It is a practical response to the **principal-agent problem** and **moral hazard**: incentive pay is meant to reduce **agency costs** by giving managers a stake in outcomes they control but owners cannot observe.[^s1] It builds on **contract theory**, and **Michael C. Jensen**'s measurement of pay-performance sensitivity framed the modern debate over how strongly pay should track shareholder wealth.[^s1][^s3] **Say on pay**, created under the **Dodd-Frank Act**, is one component of the modern regime, and **proxy advisory firms** shape how institutions cast those votes.[^s4][^s6] The **board of directors** is both the designer of pay and, in the managerial power critique, the weak link.[^s2] Debates about **shareholder primacy** frame what pay should reward in the first place.[^s3]
