# Independent Director

An independent director is a board member who is not part of management and has no relationship with the company that could compromise his or her judgement about it. For companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange, independence is not self-declared: the board must affirmatively determine that the director has no material relationship with the company, whether directly or as a partner, shareholder or officer of an organization that deals with it.[^s2] The idea is to place on the board people whose careers and incomes do not depend on the chief executive, so they can evaluate, pay and if necessary replace the executives they oversee.[^s4]

## Origin and how independence is tested

Governance codes and listing rules turn the idea into tests. The UK Corporate Governance Code asks companies to identify in the annual report each director the board considers independent, and it lists circumstances likely to impair independence: employment by the company within five years, a material business relationship within three years, pay beyond the director's fee, close family ties to advisers or senior staff, cross-directorships, representing a significant shareholder, and more than nine years of service.[^s8] A UK board may still call such a director independent if it explains why, and at least half the board, excluding the chair, should be independent.[^s8]

US rules mix a judgement-based standard with bright-line disqualifications. Under the NYSE's tests, a director is not independent if he or she or an immediate family member received more than $120,000 in direct compensation from the company in any twelve-month period within the last three years, director fees excepted; the threshold was raised from $100,000 in 2008 to match the SEC's related-party disclosure level.[^s1] Ties to the company's auditor within the same three-year look-back are also disqualifying.[^s1] For compensation committee members, boards must additionally weigh the source of a director's pay, including any consulting or advisory fee, and whether the director is affiliated with the company or a subsidiary.[^s2]

Audit committees carry a federal overlay. Section 301 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 led the SEC to adopt Rule 10A-3, effective April 25, 2003, under which audit committee members may accept no consulting, advisory or other compensatory fee from the issuer beyond board pay and may not be affiliated persons of the issuer.[^s3] The rule offers a safe harbor for a director who is not an executive officer and owns no more than 10% of any class of voting equity, while stressing that 10% is not a ceiling for ownership.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The case for independence rests on monitoring, and the cleanest test of its value comes from an unusual natural experiment. Bang Dang Nguyen and Kasper Meisner Nielsen studied sudden deaths of US directors between 1994 and 2007 and found share prices fell by 0.85% on average when an independent director died, which they read as evidence that independence itself, not just individual talent, is valuable to shareholders.[^s4] The study covered 229 deaths, 108 of them independent directors. Losses were larger when the board had fewer outsiders, when the director held a swing vote, or when he or she chaired the board or sat on the audit committee, and smaller for directors the sitting CEO had appointed.[^s5]

Broader correlational evidence is less encouraging. Sanjai Bhagat and Bernard Black's large-sample, long-horizon study found that poorly performing firms tend to add independent directors but that firms with more independent boards do not subsequently outperform, and argued that companies should be free to experiment with board designs other than the pure monitoring model.[^s6] A second critique is that formal independence can mask dependence. Jeffrey Coles, Naveen Daniel and Lalitha Naveen show that boards packed with directors appointed after the current CEO took office are weaker monitors, with CEO turnover less sensitive to performance and pay higher without stronger pay-for-performance links; independence measured only among directors who predate the CEO explains monitoring better than the conventional measure.[^s7] Practitioner bodies make a similar point, describing box-ticking independence as necessary but not sufficient for good directorship.[^s8]

## In practice

In practice independence is a recurring board determination rather than a permanent label: a US listed board must make an affirmative finding for each director, and a UK board names its independent directors in the annual report and explains any judgement that departs from the Code's list.[^s2][^s8] Because a single fee or family tie above the bright lines disqualifies a director, nominating committees screen candidates' consulting arrangements, auditor links and relatives' employment before proposing them.[^s1][^s3] The sudden-death evidence implies a concrete trade-off for a nominating committee: a newcomer who passes every formal test but owes the seat to the CEO may supply less monitoring value than a longer-serving independent director appointed before that CEO.[^s5][^s7]

## Connections

Independent directors are a component of the **board of directors** and a central instrument of **corporate governance** (`broader`). They exist to mitigate the **principal-agent problem** and the **agency costs** that arise when managers control owners' capital.[^s4] Their definition is set by the **NYSE corporate governance listing standards**, the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** for the **audit committee**, and the **UK Corporate Governance Code**, which descends from the **Cadbury Report**.[^s1][^s3][^s8] They contrast with **inside directors**, who are executives or otherwise tied to the firm. Independence matters for the **business judgment rule**, which presumes disinterested decision-makers, and for **executive compensation**, which independent compensation committees set.[^s2][^s7]
