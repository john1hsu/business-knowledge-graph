# Proxy Advisory Firm

A **proxy advisory firm** (proxy advisor) analyses the items that come up for shareholder votes at public companies, such as director elections, executive pay, equity plans, mergers and shareholder proposals, and sells research, voting guidelines and vote recommendations to institutional investors. Many clients also use the firm's platforms to cast their votes. In the United States the industry is close to a duopoly: Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) and Glass Lewis together hold almost the entire market.[^s3] A December 2025 executive order put their combined share at more than 90 percent.[^s4]

## Origin

Proxy advice grew alongside institutional ownership. Institutions held about 8% of the stock of the largest US companies at mid-century, but by 1988 they owned 45% of the shares of the 100 largest firms, which pushed them toward governance as a way to protect their holdings.[^s6] ISS, founded in the mid-1980s in the same year as the Council of Institutional Investors, was the first of a group of firms that rated governance and advised investor groups.[^s6] In 1988 the US Department of Labor told Avon's management that the fiduciary duties of pension plans covered by ERISA included proxy voting, widening the federal role in corporate governance.[^s6] Because institutional investors must vote on every ballot item and disclose their votes, many found it cheaper to buy standardized analysis and recommendations than to research thousands of meetings themselves.[^s3]

## How it works

A proxy advisor publishes benchmark voting policies, applies them to each company's proxy statement and issues a "for" or "against" recommendation per item; clients can follow the benchmark, buy a custom policy, or use the research only.[^s3] The economic logic is scale: a single analysis serves many investors, solving part of the collective-action problem that leaves diversified shareholders with little incentive to study each company. The trade-off is that a small number of policy designers can shape outcomes across thousands of companies, which is why the firms sit at the centre of debates about who actually controls the shareholder vote.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Research consistently finds large effects on vote outcomes. Using a regression-discontinuity design built on a cutoff in ISS's own guidelines, Malenko and Shen estimated that a negative ISS recommendation on say-on-pay in 2010–2011 cut shareholder support by about 25 percentage points.[^s2] Larcker, McCall and Ormazabal found that, ahead of the first mandatory say-on-pay votes in 2011, many firms reshaped pay plans toward advisor-preferred designs, and the market reacted negatively to those changes, suggesting that deference to advisors can reduce shareholder value.[^s1] Surveys of the literature add that ISS opposition is linked to a 51-point gap in institutional support versus about 2 points among retail holders, and that one study counted 114 institutions managing roughly $5 trillion that voted almost in lockstep with ISS ("robo-voting").[^s3]

Critics raise three main concerns: recommendation quality and opaque methodologies; conflicts of interest when an advisor also sells consulting to the companies it rates; and whether influence comes from insight or from regulatory demand for votes.[^s3] Larcker and Tayan frame the open question as whether advisors serve mainly as useful information intermediaries and issue-spotters or as agenda setters whose guidelines investors follow without scrutiny.[^s3]

## Regulation and recent changes

US policy has swung back and forth. In July 2020 the SEC codified proxy voting advice as a "solicitation" under Regulation 14A and conditioned the advisors' exemptions on conflict disclosure and on giving companies advance access to advice; in July 2022 it rescinded the advance-access conditions but kept conflict disclosure and anti-fraud liability.[^s5] Executive Order 14366, signed on December 11, 2025, directed the SEC to review proxy-advisor rules and consider registration as investment advisers, the FTC to examine possible unfair competition, and the Department of Labor to revisit when advisors to ERISA plans are fiduciaries, citing concerns about ESG and DEI-related recommendations.[^s4]

## In practice

Boards routinely engage with ISS and Glass Lewis policies when designing pay plans, because an "against" recommendation can sink a say-on-pay vote; the 2011 say-on-pay round is the textbook example of companies redesigning compensation to win advisor support.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Proxy advisors are a mechanism within corporate governance for monitoring boards and limiting the agency costs created when dispersed shareholders delegate control to managers.[^s1] Their reach rests on the fiduciary duty of institutional investors to vote, the same duty the 2025 executive order targets.[^s4][^s6] Their recommendations on director elections bear directly on the board of directors, and their ESG positions tie them to debates over ESG and shareholder primacy.[^s3][^s4] ISS and Glass Lewis exemplify the category, say-on-pay is where their influence is best measured, and the Securities Exchange Act's proxy rules (Regulation 14A) govern their advice.[^s2][^s5]
