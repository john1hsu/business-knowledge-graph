# Cadbury Report

The Cadbury Report is the final report of the UK Committee on the Financial Aspects of Corporate Governance, published in December 1992 under the chairmanship of Sir Adrian Cadbury.[^s1][^s2] It gave the field its most widely quoted definition, describing corporate governance as "the system by which companies are directed and controlled", with boards responsible for governance and shareholders appointing directors and auditors.[^s1] Its lasting contribution was less any single rule than a method: a voluntary Code of Best Practice backed by a listing requirement that companies disclose whether they follow it and explain where they do not.[^s2]

## Origin

The committee was set up in May 1991 by the Financial Reporting Council, the London Stock Exchange and the accountancy profession, at a time when investor confidence in UK listed companies had weakened because the sudden failures of Coloroll and Polly Peck had not been signalled in their published accounts.[^s2] The collapse of BCCI and the discovery that Robert Maxwell had taken about £440m from his companies' pension funds added urgency while the committee was working; a draft was issued for comment in May 1992.[^s2] Its twelve members included Sir Ron Dearing, Jonathan Charkham and Andrew Likierman, with a secretary seconded from the Department of Trade and Industry.[^s1]

## What the Code recommended

The Code focused on the board's control and reporting functions rather than on strategy.[^s1]

- **Division of power:** the chairman and chief executive roles should in principle be separate; where combined, the board needs a strong independent element and a recognised senior member.[^s1]
- **Non-executive directors:** every board should have at least three, a majority of whom are independent of management and free of relationships that could materially interfere with their judgement.[^s1]
- **Audit committee:** at least three non-executive members with written terms of reference, normally meeting at least twice a year and privately with the external auditors.[^s1]
- **Pay:** a remuneration committee made up wholly or mainly of non-executives, with executive directors taking no part in setting their own pay; new service contracts over three years need shareholder approval.[^s1]
- **Reporting:** directors should report on the effectiveness of internal control and state that the business is a going concern, and auditors should review parts of the compliance statement.[^s1]

The London Stock Exchange amended its listing rules so that companies had to state how far they complied with the Code and give reasons for any departure, the principle now known as comply or explain.[^s2][^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The best-known evaluation, by Dahya, McConnell and Travlos in the Journal of Finance (2002), found that CEO turnover rose after the Code was issued and became more sensitive to poor performance, with the effect concentrated among firms that adopted it.[^s3] A companion study of 523 CEO appointments from 1989 to 1999 reported that the Code increased the number and share of outside directors, reduced combined chair-CEO roles, and that boards with more outsiders were more likely to hire an outside CEO; the authors cautioned that more turnover does not by itself prove better performance.[^s4]

The flexibility of comply or explain is also its weakness. Arcot, Bruno and Faure-Grimaud, studying 245 non-financial UK firms from 1998 to 2004, found that compliance with the successor Combined Code rose but that firms often offered boilerplate explanations for departures, pointing to weak monitoring and enforcement by shareholders.[^s5]

## Legacy

Cadbury was followed by the Greenbury Report on directors' pay (1995) and the Hampel review (1998), which merged their recommendations into the Combined Code; that code was renamed the UK Corporate Governance Code in 2010 and revised in 2018 and 2024, while keeping comply or explain at its core.[^s6][^s2]

## Connections

The report is a foundational text of corporate governance in the UK and the direct ancestor of the UK Corporate Governance Code, which absorbed its provisions through the Combined Code.[^s6] Its recommendations shaped the modern board of directors, the board audit committee, the role of the independent director, and the remuneration committees that oversee executive compensation.[^s1] Its board-monitoring logic targets the agency costs that arise when managers are not fully accountable to owners, which is why researchers tested it by asking whether poorly performing CEOs became more likely to be replaced.[^s3][^s4] Its voluntary, disclosure-based design, in which shareholders rather than regulators judge explanations, is the feature that sets it apart from mandatory statutory regimes such as the US Sarbanes-Oxley Act, and the feature its critics say depends on investor monitoring.[^s2][^s5]
