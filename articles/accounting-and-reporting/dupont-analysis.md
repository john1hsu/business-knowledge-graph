# DuPont Analysis: Decomposing Return on Equity

DuPont analysis is a way of reading return on equity (ROE) as the product of three ratios rather than as a single number. In its common three-step form, ROE equals profit margin (net income over sales) times total asset turnover (sales over total assets) times the equity multiplier (total assets over shareholders' equity).[^s1] Because the sales and total-asset terms cancel, the product is algebraically identical to net income over equity, but the decomposition tells an analyst whether a high ROE comes from operating efficiency, from intensive use of assets, or from leverage.[^s1] OpenStax's finance textbook labels the three drivers operating efficiency, asset usage and leverage, and traces the method to DuPont's internal performance measurement.[^s1]

## Origin

The approach grew out of work by F. Donaldson Brown, who joined DuPont's Explosives Department as a salesman in 1908 and in 1914, as assistant treasurer, combined earnings, working capital and plant investment into a single measure he called return on investment.[^s3] DuPont made ROI the main yardstick for its operating departments and required an ROI forecast for capital appropriations; senior executives tracked it for decades in a dedicated Chart Room.[^s3] Accounting historians Dale Flesher and Gary Previts describe Brown's 1914 expanded ROI measure, which is also called the DuPont formula, as one of the most consequential innovations in twentieth-century financial management.[^s2] The familiar three-factor ROE version adds the equity multiplier to Brown's margin-and-turnover logic.[^s1]

## How it works

Each factor points to a different managerial lever. Profit margin reflects pricing power and cost control; asset turnover reflects how much revenue each dollar of assets produces; the equity multiplier reflects how much of the asset base is financed by liabilities rather than owners' capital.[^s1] Two firms with the same ROE can therefore have very different profiles: a discount retailer may pair thin margins with fast turnover, while a luxury brand does the reverse. In OpenStax's worked example, a sporting goods firm with a 29% margin, 0.53 turnover and a 2.37 equity multiplier earns an ROE of roughly 36%.[^s1] The textbook stresses that the result should be judged against the firm's own history and close competitors, since an ROE that looks strong against one industry benchmark can look ordinary against another.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The main critique is that the three-step identity mixes operating and financing activities. Nissim and Penman, writing in *Review of Accounting Studies* in 2001, propose a hierarchical ratio analysis that separates the two and treats operations as the source of value.[^s5] Their version expresses return on common equity as return on net operating assets plus financial leverage times the spread between that operating return and the net borrowing cost, with the operating return itself split into operating margin and turnover.[^s6] They argue that measures such as return on total assets blend financial items into operating profitability and understate operating returns; in their sample the median return on net operating assets was 10.3% against a median return on total assets of 7.0%.[^s6] A related caution is that leverage raises ROE mechanically, so a rising equity multiplier can flatter returns while adding risk.[^s1]

Empirical work supports the decomposition's usefulness. Mark Soliman's 2008 study in *The Accounting Review* finds that DuPont components of operating return help predict future earnings beyond previously studied accounting signals, and that stock returns and analyst forecast revisions respond to them.[^s4] Those responses were incomplete, though: later forecast errors and abnormal returns were predictable, suggesting markets do not fully use the information.[^s4]

## In practice

The best-known applied case is General Motors. Brown became GM's vice president of finance in 1921 and adapted ROI into a pricing method in which each division set a multi-year target return and a standard production volume, then derived the average price needed to hit the target.[^s3] Flesher and Previts note that by the 1950s ROI had become a dominant approach to managing industrial corporations, promoted by the American Management Association and Harvard's Robert Anthony, and later adopted at Ford by a management team that included a former GM vice president.[^s2] Analysts use the decomposition to diagnose why ROE moved between periods and to compare peers.[^s1]

## Connections

DuPont analysis is a core technique of financial statement analysis and is built on ROE, the metric it decomposes.[^s1] It was created by Donaldson Brown and is exemplified by its use at General Motors.[^s2][^s3] Its leverage term links it to capital structure, because the equity multiplier rises as more assets are financed with debt.[^s1] Its ratios rest on accrual-based income and balance-sheet figures, which is why cash-based measures such as free cash flow are a useful cross-check. Nissim and Penman's operating-versus-financing refinement connects it to residual-income valuation, where forecast ratios feed estimates of shareholder payoffs.[^s5][^s6] Its use as an internal yardstick for divisions also ties it to performance management.[^s3]
