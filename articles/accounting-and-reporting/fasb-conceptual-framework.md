# FASB Conceptual Framework

The **FASB Conceptual Framework** is the set of Statements of Financial Accounting Concepts through which the Financial Accounting Standards Board explains *why* US accounting standards look the way they do. The SEC's Chief Accountant describes it as a body of interrelated objectives and fundamental concepts that guide the nature, function, elements, measurement approaches and disclosure objectives of financial accounting and reporting.[^s2] The FASB itself frames its purpose as giving structure and direction to financial reporting so that it supplies unbiased information that helps capital markets allocate resources efficiently.[^s1]

## Status: guidance for the standard setter, not GAAP

The framework is not itself accounting law for preparers. Concepts Statements sit outside the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, do not establish generally accepted accounting principles, and may be amended, superseded or withdrawn.[^s1] Each Concepts Statement is explicitly nonauthoritative.[^s4] The FASB reinforced this separation with ASU 2024-02, which removed remaining references to the Concepts Statements from the Codification, while still treating the framework as a foundation of its standard-setting process.[^s6] In short, companies apply the standards; the Board applies the framework when writing them.

## Origin and history

The FASB issued its first concepts statement in 1978 and had issued seven by 2000. In 2014, responding to stakeholder feedback, the Board decided to revisit the framework comprehensively.[^s2] The rebuilt framework is organized as chapters of Concepts Statement No. 8. Chapter 1 on the objective of financial reporting was developed jointly with the International Accounting Standards Board, through a 2006 discussion paper and a 2008 exposure draft, and it absorbed and superseded the older Concepts Statement No. 4.[^s1] Academic review notes that the Concepts Statements issued between 1978 and 2018 were meant to guide the Board in creating GAAP, and that Chapter 8, on notes to financial statements, was added in 2018.[^s5]

The revision accelerated in the 2020s. In December 2021 the FASB issued Chapter 4, *Elements of Financial Statements*, which superseded Concepts Statement No. 6, and Chapter 7, *Presentation*, which superseded parts of Concepts Statement No. 5 on recognition and measurement.[^s4] On July 12, 2024, it issued Chapter 6, *Measurement*, which completed the framework project.[^s2] [^s3]

## How it works

The framework starts from an objective and works outward. Chapter 1 states that general purpose financial reporting exists to provide information useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in deciding whether to provide resources to the entity; these users are primary because they usually cannot demand information directly from the company.[^s1] Later chapters translate that objective into concepts for which items count as elements (assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expense), when they are recognized and derecognized, how they are measured, how they are presented, and what notes should disclose.[^s2] [^s4] The measurement chapter, for example, does not prescribe a basis for any item; it describes two relevant and faithfully representative systems, an entry-price system and an exit-price system, plus the considerations for choosing between them.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Because the framework is nonauthoritative, a recurring criticism is that it can be ignored when convenient. The SEC's Chief Accountant, Paul Munter, addressed this in an August 2024 statement urging the Board to use the completed framework in agenda setting and project scoping, to deliberate publicly on how it applies, and to explain in each standard's basis for conclusions whether the amendments are consistent with it, and why if they are not. He also stressed that disclosing an amount is not a substitute for recognizing it.[^s2] [^s6] Individual chapters have drawn debate as well: Johnson (2020) evaluated the controversial parts of the disclosure chapter using stakeholder comment letters and questioned how meaningful the addition was.[^s5]

## In practice

Practitioners meet the framework indirectly, in the basis-for-conclusions sections of new accounting standards updates and in comment letters responding to FASB proposals. The SEC has urged that stakeholder feedback on active projects be anchored in the framework's principles, so that debates about a standard turn on whether it meets the reporting objective and produces information with the qualitative characteristics of usefulness.[^s2]

## Connections

The framework sits within financial accounting and was created by the Financial Accounting Standards Board.[^s1] It supplies the reasoning behind US GAAP without being part of it, which is why it influences GAAP rather than belonging to it.[^s1] [^s6] It is the conceptual home of accrual accounting: Chapter 1 explains why accrual-based measures of performance are more informative than cash receipts and payments alone.[^s1] Its Chapter 1 was written jointly with the IASB, so it parallels and contrasts with the IASB's own conceptual framework that underpins IFRS.[^s1]
