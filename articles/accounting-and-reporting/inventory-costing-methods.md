# Inventory Costing Methods: FIFO, LIFO, Weighted Average and Specific Identification

When a merchandiser or manufacturer buys the same item at different prices during a period, it must decide which of those costs leave the balance sheet as cost of goods sold (COGS) and which stay behind in ending inventory. An inventory costing method, or cost flow assumption, is management's rule for making that split. It is an accounting estimate that need not mirror how goods physically move through the warehouse, and it spares the firm from tracking the purchase cost of every individual unit.[^s1]

## The four methods

- **Specific identification** assigns each unit its actual purchase cost; it fits expensive, distinguishable goods but is impractical for high volumes of similar items.[^s1]
- **First-in, first-out (FIFO)** assumes the oldest units are sold first, so ending inventory carries the most recent purchase costs.[^s1]
- **Last-in, first-out (LIFO)** assumes the newest units are sold first, so ending inventory carries the oldest costs.[^s1]
- **Weighted average** divides the total cost of goods available for sale by the units available; under a perpetual system the average must be recomputed after each purchase.[^s1]

The selling price of a unit is the same under every method; only the cost matched against it changes. In OpenStax's worked example, one unit sold for $36 yields a gross margin of $15 under FIFO, $3 under LIFO and about $8.38 under weighted average, because each method assigns a different historical cost to the same sale.[^s1] Whatever method a firm picks, it is expected to apply it consistently, and a change must be justified as giving a better picture of operations and disclosed in the notes.[^s1]

## Effects when prices rise

During inflation FIFO charges the older, cheaper costs to COGS, producing higher gross margin and net income and an ending inventory closer to current replacement cost. LIFO does the reverse: higher COGS, lower reported profit, lower taxable income and an inventory balance that can be far below current prices.[^s1] A worked example in *The CPA Journal* (2025) shows LIFO cutting tax on a unit's profit by about 45% relative to FIFO when purchase costs rise from $100 to $110, and argues that LIFO income better reflects what it costs to replace the goods sold.[^s5] The gap between LIFO inventory and its FIFO or replacement cost is known as the LIFO reserve.[^s5]

## Rules: US GAAP, IFRS and the tax code

US GAAP permits all four methods, while IFRS prohibits LIFO.[^s1] IAS 2, the IFRS inventory standard, requires inventories to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value, allows specific identification for items that are not ordinarily interchangeable, and otherwise permits only FIFO or weighted average cost; the IASB issued its revised IAS 2 in December 2003.[^s2]

In the United States, LIFO is tied to the income tax. Section 472 of the Internal Revenue Code lets a taxpayer elect LIFO for listed goods, and the election is made on IRS Form 970 filed with the tax return.[^s3][^s4] Section 472(c) adds the "LIFO conformity" condition: a taxpayer may use LIFO for tax only if it also uses LIFO in reports to shareholders and lenders.[^s3] This is an exception to the usual freedom to use different methods for tax and book purposes, and it is why LIFO's tax saving comes bundled with lower reported earnings.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Researchers have long debated why so many firms forgo LIFO's tax savings. The *CPA Journal* review points to Biddle's (1980) "LIFO puzzle" (firms paying extra tax by staying on FIFO) and to later work suggesting that managers paid on reported income prefer FIFO's higher earnings.[^s5] Usage estimates differ widely by sample: OpenStax cites an AICPA estimate that 35–40% of US companies use LIFO, whereas the 2025 review puts the share of US public companies with inventory at about 7%, and reports roughly 30 US firms switching from LIFO to FIFO in 2021–2022 as inflation squeezed reported margins.[^s1][^s5] Critics of LIFO add that its balance-sheet figures can be badly outdated and that it serves as a tax deferral; repealing it was estimated to raise about $102 billion in federal revenue over 2017–2026.[^s1] The US corporate rate cut from 35% to 21% in 2017 also shrank the method's tax advantage.[^s5]

## In practice

Grocer Kroger shows the stakes. For its fiscal year ended January 28, 2023, it valued about 89% of inventories on LIFO (fuel was mostly on FIFO), recorded a LIFO charge of $626 million against $197 million the year before as food inflation surged, and reported that replacement cost exceeded the carrying amount of its inventories by $2,196 million.[^s6] An analyst comparing Kroger with a FIFO or IFRS reporter would add back that reserve to put inventories and margins on a common basis.[^s6][^s1]

## Connections

Inventory costing is part of financial accounting and is governed by US GAAP in the United States and by IAS 2 under IFRS, the clearest single difference between the two frameworks on inventories.[^s1][^s2] It is the accounting counterpart of inventory management, which decides how much stock to hold, while costing decides what that stock and its sales are worth on the statements. Its purpose is to apply the matching principle by pairing revenue with an assumed cost of the goods sold.[^s1] Kroger illustrates a large LIFO reporter and its reserve disclosures.[^s6]
