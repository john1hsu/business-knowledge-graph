# Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002

The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002** (SOX) is the US federal statute, Public Law 107-204, that President George W. Bush signed on July 30, 2002, after the collapses of Enron and WorldCom shook confidence in corporate financial reporting.[^s1] Its formal name, the Public Company Accounting Reform and Investor Protection Act, signals its core aim: repairing the auditing of US public companies, which by the early 2000s was widely seen as performing badly and deteriorating.[^s2] It reshaped the duties of executives, boards and auditors of every US-listed company.[^s1][^s7]

## Origin

Before 2002 the audit profession largely regulated itself. Coates argues the scandals exposed an auditing system that had been getting worse, and the president singled out consulting work by audit firms as a source of conflicts of interest.[^s2][^s1] Congress reconciled competing House and Senate bills in conference in July 2002; the final text followed the Senate bill on the new auditor regulator and on criminal penalties for false certifications.[^s7]

## Main provisions

The act works through several reinforcing mechanisms:

- **Auditor oversight.** It created the five-member Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), which sets auditing standards, inspects audit firms and disciplines violators under SEC supervision.[^s7]
- **Auditor independence.** Auditors may no longer provide certain consulting services, such as designing financial information systems or performing internal audit work, to their audit clients; lead audit partners must rotate after five consecutive years.[^s7]
- **Audit committees.** The board's audit committee hires, pays and oversees the auditor, must set up channels for anonymous complaints, and is expected to include at least one financial expert.[^s7]
- **Executive accountability.** CEOs and CFOs must personally certify that their company's reports are accurate, executives who profit from misconduct must return gains, and company loans to top executives are banned.[^s1][^s7]
- **Penalties.** The maximum prison term for common fraud rose from 5 to 20 years, and penalties for shredding documents and obstructing investigations increased.[^s1]

## Section 404 and internal control

The most debated provision is Section 404. Section 404(a) requires each annual report to contain management's statement of responsibility for internal control over financial reporting and its assessment of that control's effectiveness at fiscal year-end; Section 404(b) requires the company's external auditor to attest to that assessment under PCAOB standards.[^s5] Coates argues that 404 deliberately pushed firms to spend more on controls than they would have chosen to after the scandals alone.[^s2] Smaller companies won repeated delays, a relaxed SEC approach in 2007, and in 2010 the Dodd-Frank Act permanently exempted issuers with market capitalization of $75 million or less from the auditor attestation in 404(b).[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Critics called 404 compliance costs extreme, and small-business advocates claimed costs could reach 10–15% of revenue, an estimate the Congressional Research Service reported but did not verify.[^s5] Stronger evidence comes from Iliev, who compared firms just above and below the $75 million public-float cutoff in 2004: filing the management report roughly doubled audit fees (an increase of about 98%, or around $698,000 per firm) while making reported earnings more conservative.[^s4] He concluded that, on net, compliance lowered the market value of small firms.[^s3]

Defenders reply that early costs fell quickly and that the benefits, including lower fraud risk, more reliable reporting and possibly a lower cost of capital, accrue over the long run; Coates called the law a work in progress whose 404 rules were applied too aggressively at first, and urged better implementation rather than repeal.[^s2] An SEC staff study mandated by Dodd-Frank found in April 2011 that issuers with auditor attestation had lower restatement rates, that compliance costs had fallen after the 2007 reforms, and that there was no conclusive evidence the requirement deterred US listings; it recommended against widening the exemption.[^s6]

## In practice

The 2011 SEC study illustrates who bears the auditor-attestation burden: it identified 967 issuers with a public float between $75 million and $250 million as of December 31, 2009, about 10% of all filers that year.[^s6] For such firms, SOX compliance is an annual cycle of documenting key controls, testing them, remediating material weaknesses, and having executives sign certifications, with the external auditor reviewing the result.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

SOX sits within corporate governance and securities regulation: it tries to mitigate the principal-agent problem and the agency costs that arise when managers control financial reporting for absent shareholders, by strengthening independent monitors such as the board's audit committee and the outside auditor.[^s2][^s7] Its centerpiece institution is the PCAOB, which it created.[^s7] It was triggered by the Enron scandal and was later amended by the Dodd-Frank Act's small-company exemption.[^s1][^s5] It also links to US GAAP, the rulebook whose application auditors check, and to the internal control over financial reporting that Section 404 requires firms to assess.[^s5]
