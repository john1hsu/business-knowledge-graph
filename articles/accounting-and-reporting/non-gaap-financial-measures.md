# Non-GAAP Financial Measures: Pro Forma and Adjusted Earnings Under Regulation G

A **non-GAAP financial measure** is a number describing a company's historical or projected performance, financial position or cash flows that includes or excludes amounts relative to the most directly comparable measure calculated under generally accepted accounting principles. The SEC's 2003 rulemaking gave EBITDA and operating income excluding "non-recurring" items as typical examples.[^s1] The definition leaves out operating and statistical metrics (such as unit sales or store counts), ratios built only from GAAP figures, and measures that GAAP, SEC rules or another regulator require.[^s1] Common names include pro forma earnings, adjusted earnings, core earnings and, in the investment community, "street" earnings.[^s3]

## Origin

Adjusted earnings are not new, but their use exploded in the late 1990s. Mark Bradshaw and Richard Sloan compared GAAP net income with the "street" earnings that analysts forecast and track, and found that over roughly two decades the gaps between the two grew sharply in both frequency and size.[^s3] Street earnings were strongly biased to exceed GAAP earnings, the stock market's reaction to street earnings had displaced its reaction to GAAP earnings, and managers actively defined and emphasized the street figure in their earnings releases.[^s3] After the dot-com collapse and the accounting scandals of 2001–2002, Section 401(b) of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act directed the SEC to write rules for these measures, and the resulting rules took effect on March 28, 2003.[^s1]

## How the rules work

The US regime has two layers. **Regulation G** applies whenever an SEC-reporting company publicly releases material information containing a non-GAAP measure, in a press release, a webcast or a filing. The company must present the most directly comparable GAAP measure, provide a quantitative reconciliation between the two, and must not present the measure in a way that is misleading.[^s1] **Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K** adds stricter conditions for measures in SEC filings: the GAAP measure must be given equal or greater prominence, management must explain why the measure is useful, and certain practices are barred. These include excluding cash-settled charges from liquidity measures (with an exception for EBIT and EBITDA), labelling a recurring item as non-recurring when similar items occurred in the prior two years or are reasonably likely within the next two, putting non-GAAP figures on the face of the financial statements, and using titles confusingly similar to GAAP line items.[^s1] Earnings releases are furnished to the SEC on Form 8-K, so the Item 10(e) usefulness disclosure applies to them as well.[^s1]

SEC staff interpretations, last updated on December 13, 2022, explain how the "not misleading" test is applied. Excluding normal, recurring cash operating expenses needed to run the business can make a measure misleading, and an expense counts as recurring even if it appears only occasionally or irregularly.[^s2] So-called individually tailored accounting, such as recognizing deferred revenue when billed or switching to cash-basis accounting, can also be misleading, as can mislabelling a measure; the staff adds that extensive disclosure about an adjustment does not by itself cure a misleading measure.[^s2] On prominence, leading an earnings headline with the adjusted number, using bolder type for it, or charting only the non-GAAP series all fail the test.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Defenders argue that removing one-time and non-cash items helps investors see persistent earning power. The research supports that only in part. Doyle, Lundholm and Soliman found that larger exclusions from pro forma earnings predicted lower future cash flows, that investors did not fully price this at the announcement, and that a trading strategy based on the size of exclusions earned large abnormal returns in later years.[^s4] In other words, many "excluded" expenses were real, recurring costs.[^s4]

Regulation appears to have helped. Black and co-authors found that after Sarbanes-Oxley and Regulation G, firms reporting non-GAAP earnings were less likely to exclude recurring items beyond those analysts excluded, and less likely to use exclusions to meet earnings targets they would otherwise have missed, though some firms continued to exclude specific recurring items aggressively.[^s5]

## In practice

The SEC enforces the rules through comment letters and, occasionally, enforcement actions. In September 2023 Newell Brands agreed to pay a $12.5 million civil penalty, and its former CEO $110,000, to settle charges that its non-GAAP "core sales" growth figures for 2016 and 2017 misrepresented its actual sales performance, including a violation of Rule 100(b) of Regulation G; both settled without admitting or denying the findings.[^s6] Careful analysts start from the reconciliation, ask whether each excluded item really is unusual, and compare a company's adjustments over time and against its peers.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Non-GAAP measures are defined against, and contrast with, GAAP figures, and they sit within financial statement analysis and financial accounting.[^s1] They are regulated by Regulation G, which was mandated by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.[^s1] EBITDA, which the SEC cited as a standard example, is a prominent member of the family,[^s1] and free cash flow is a related cash-based figure that companies also define for themselves. Because managers choose the adjustments, the topic connects to earnings management and financial reporting quality, where the research on exclusions is most relevant.[^s3][^s4][^s5]
