# International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)

International Financial Reporting Standards, now branded IFRS Accounting Standards, are the set of accounting rules developed by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), an independent standard-setting body inside the IFRS Foundation. The Foundation, a not-for-profit public-interest organisation created in 2001, states that its mission is standards that bring transparency, accountability and efficiency to capital markets. As of November 2026 its website reports that IFRS Accounting Standards are required in more than 140 jurisdictions.[^s1] The body of rules is made up of numbered IFRS standards plus older International Accounting Standards (IAS) that the IASB inherited and still maintains, together with interpretations from the IFRS Interpretations Committee.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin and spread

The decisive step toward global use came from the European Union. Regulation (EC) No 1606/2002, adopted on 19 July 2002, set out to improve the comparability of financial statements and the functioning of the internal market by having publicly traded companies apply a single set of high-quality international standards in their consolidated accounts. Its recitals tie the core requirement to 2005 and allow some deferrals so that all publicly traded EU companies use the global standards by 2007 at the latest.[^s2] Because standards apply in the EU only after endorsement, the regulation also created a filter between the IASB and European law.[^s2]

The United States moved partway. On 15 November 2007 the SEC voted unanimously to accept financial statements from foreign private issuers prepared under IFRS without reconciliation to US GAAP, for fiscal years ending after that date, but only if they used IFRS as issued by the IASB, a condition meant to discourage national variants.[^s3] Domestic US companies still report under US GAAP.

## How it works

IFRS is usually described as more principles-based than US GAAP, with fewer bright-line rules and less industry-specific guidance. The SEC staff's 2012 final report listed examples where IFRS lacks the industry guidance found in US GAAP, such as rate-regulated utilities, oil and gas, investment companies and broker-dealers, and fundamental differences such as the prohibition of LIFO inventory, the reversal of certain impairment losses and component depreciation of assets.[^s4] The FASB and IASB ran joint convergence projects, among them revenue recognition and leases, but the staff found the remaining gap larger than it had expected in 2010.[^s4]

The IASB keeps the body of standards current through due process. A recent example is IFRS 18, *Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements*, issued in April 2024 and effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027. It replaces IAS 1, adds two defined subtotals to the income statement (operating profit, and profit before financing and income taxes) and requires disclosure of management-defined performance measures used in public communications.[^s6] Since 2021 the Foundation has also housed a second board, the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), which issues IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards alongside the accounting standards.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The most cited early study of mandatory adoption, by Daske, Hail, Leuz and Verdi (Journal of Accounting Research, 2008), examined firms in 26 countries. It found that market liquidity generally rose around the introduction of IFRS, and that cost of capital fell and equity valuations rose only when effects were allowed to begin before the official adoption date.[^s5] Benefits appeared only where firms had incentives to be transparent and legal enforcement was strong, and the authors cautioned that concurrent enforcement and governance reforms mean the gains cannot be credited to the accounting switch alone.[^s5]

The SEC staff's 2012 report captures the main objections to full US adoption. It made no recommendation to adopt, and it cited the IASB's dependence on large accounting firms for funding, uneven application and enforcement across countries, the high cost of a direct switch for US issuers, and the many US laws and private contracts that refer to US GAAP. It discussed an endorsement approach, nicknamed "condorsement", under which the FASB would incorporate IFRS standard by standard.[^s4]

## In practice

For a multinational, IFRS shapes reported numbers directly. A European parent consolidating subsidiaries prepares group accounts under EU-endorsed IFRS, while a foreign company listing American depositary shares in New York can file IFRS statements as issued by the IASB without a GAAP bridge.[^s2][^s3] Analysts comparing such a company with a US peer must adjust for differences such as inventory costing and impairment reversals before ratios line up.[^s4] From 2027, the new IFRS 18 subtotals will give a more uniform starting point for comparing operating profit across IFRS reporters.[^s6]

## Connections

IFRS is a branch of financial accounting created by the International Accounting Standards Board and contrasts with US GAAP, its main rival framework.[^s1][^s4] Revenue recognition was one of its joint convergence projects with the FASB, which is why IFRS 15 and ASC 606 are treated as one node here, and its impairment rules for intangible assets, which differ from US GAAP's, link it to goodwill accounting; like GAAP, it rests on accrual accounting.[^s4] Its spread was driven by the EU IAS Regulation, IFRS 18 is one of its newest component standards, and through the ISSB the same Foundation now influences sustainability reporting and ESG disclosure.[^s1][^s2][^s6]
