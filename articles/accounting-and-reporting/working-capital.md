# Working Capital

**Working capital** is the pool of short-term resources a business uses to pay its bills and payroll and keep operations running. In its gross sense it simply equals current assets: cash and cash equivalents, marketable securities, accounts receivable and inventory, all of which are cash or expected to become cash within a year. The more informative measure is **net working capital (NWC)**, defined as current assets minus current liabilities such as accounts payable, short-term notes, accrued expenses, taxes payable and the current portion of long-term debt.[^s1] NWC shows whether short-term assets cover short-term obligations and by how much, which makes it a basic gauge of liquidity on the balance sheet.[^s1]

## How it works

OpenStax frames NWC as a spectrum. Negative NWC can signal liquidity trouble, zero NWC leaves no cushion, and positive NWC indicates the firm can meet its obligations; but a very large surplus suggests cash and other assets are sitting idle rather than earning a return.[^s1] In its worked example, a firm with $1,000,000 of current assets and $750,000 of current liabilities has $250,000 of net working capital.[^s1]

**Working capital management** is the set of decisions behind those balances. Each component involves a trade-off: cash provides liquidity but earns nothing; lean inventory frees cash and cuts obsolescence risk but can strain operations; looser credit terms win sales but swell receivables; and stretching supplier payments conserves cash but can damage supplier relationships and the firm's credit standing.[^s1] Growth adds pressure, because a profitable company that is expanding still has to fund more receivables and inventory, often with lines of credit or factoring.[^s1]

Because a balance-sheet snapshot says nothing about speed, analysts pair NWC with flow measures. The **cash conversion cycle** adds the inventory conversion period to the receivables collection period and subtracts the payables deferral period, giving the number of days cash is tied up in operations; shorter is generally better.[^s1] In OpenStax's example, trimming inventory and receivables and using more trade credit cut a firm's cycle from about 57 days to about 44 with no change in sales or cost of goods sold.[^s1]

## Origin

The accounting idea of current assets net of current liabilities is long established, but the time-based view of working capital is usually traced to Verlyn Richards and Eugene Laughlin, who introduced the cash conversion cycle in a 1980 article in *Financial Management*. Writing in the *Journal of Accountancy* in 2013, Cagle, Campbell and Jones noted that textbooks still largely omitted the measure.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The best-known empirical link between working capital and performance is Marc Deloof's study of 1,009 large non-financial Belgian firms over 1992–1996. Using days of receivables, inventories and payables, with the cash conversion cycle as an overall measure, he found that firms could raise profitability by cutting receivable and inventory days, and that less profitable firms took longer to pay their suppliers.[^s2] The second finding is a caution: long payables periods can be a symptom of weakness rather than a deliberate strategy.[^s2]

Static working capital ratios draw sustained criticism. Cagle, Campbell and Jones argue that the current and quick ratios ignore how fast assets turn into cash and when liabilities fall due. A firm can lift its current ratio by paying down liabilities, which drains cash, and rising receivables can flatter the ratio even as collections slow.[^s3] Their comparison of Circuit City and Best Buy in the decade before Circuit City's 2008 bankruptcy makes the point: Circuit City's average current ratio (2.08) looked stronger than Best Buy's (1.24), yet its average cash conversion cycle was 35 days against Best Buy's 5.[^s3] The cycle has its own blind spot, since it ignores obligations such as payroll, interest and taxes, so the authors recommend using it alongside the static measures.[^s3]

## In practice

Dell is the classic case of a firm that runs on suppliers' money. Cagle and colleagues reported fourth-quarter cash conversion cycles of roughly minus 33 to minus 36 days for Dell in fiscal 2010–2012, and minus 38 days for Amazon in 2012.[^s3] Dell's balance sheet still shows negative NWC: as of January 30, 2026 it reported total current assets of $57,602 million against current liabilities of $63,269 million, compared with $36,229 million and $46,527 million a year earlier.[^s4] Dell's 10-K says it generally keeps minimal component and product inventories, and attributes the working capital swings of fiscal 2025–2026 mainly to demand for AI-optimized servers.[^s4] For such firms, negative NWC reflects bargaining power over suppliers rather than distress, showing why the number must be read with the business model in mind.

## Connections

Working capital is one of the core areas of corporate finance and a standard input to financial statement analysis.[^s1] It is built from accrual-accounting balances such as receivables, payables and accrued expenses, and deferred revenue is a current liability that lets subscription businesses collect cash before delivering service. Changes in NWC are a deduction in free cash flow, so working capital discipline feeds directly into valuation. It is measured by the cash conversion cycle and by liquidity ratios such as the current ratio,[^s1][^s3] depends heavily on trade credit from suppliers,[^s1][^s2] and links to just-in-time production, whose small inventories shorten the inventory leg of the cycle. Dell exemplifies a negative working capital model.[^s3][^s4]
