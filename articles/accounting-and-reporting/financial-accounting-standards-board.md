# FASB: The Private-Sector Body That Sets US GAAP

The **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** is the independent, private-sector organization that establishes and improves financial accounting and reporting standards in the United States, with the stated aim of giving investors and other users decision-useful information. It was established in 1973 and is overseen, administered, financed and staffed with board members by the Financial Accounting Foundation (FAF), created a year earlier, which also oversees the Governmental Accounting Standards Board.[^s1] The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) recognizes the FASB as the designated accounting standard setter for public companies, so its pronouncements form the core of US GAAP.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Before 1973, US accounting principles were set by committees of the accounting profession, most recently the Accounting Principles Board (APB), whose members were part-time volunteers who kept their other jobs. Concerns about the APB's independence led the Wheat Study Group to recommend a new structure, and the FASB replaced the APB in 1973 with seven full-time members chosen by the FAF.[^s3] In December 1973 the SEC's Accounting Series Release No. 150 declared that the Commission would rely on the private sector, through the FASB, for accounting principles, treating FASB pronouncements as authoritative unless the SEC decided otherwise.[^s2][^s3]

That arrangement rested on SEC policy until the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002. Section 108 of the Act added Section 19(b) to the Securities Act of 1933, allowing the SEC to recognize principles from a standard setter that meets set criteria: a private entity with a public-interest board of trustees, most of whom are not tied to a registered audit firm, independent funding, majority-vote procedures for prompt changes, and attention to international convergence. In a 2003 policy statement the SEC found that the FASB and FAF met those criteria, while keeping its own authority to set standards for enforcement purposes.[^s2] Section 109 also replaced voluntary fundraising with fees assessed on issuers.[^s2][^s3]

## How it works

The board has seven full-time members appointed by the FAF trustees, generally for five-year terms and up to ten years in total.[^s1][^s2] The FASB describes its due process as transparent, inclusive and comprehensive, inviting broad stakeholder participation before standards are finalized. It is advised by standing groups including the Financial Accounting Standards Advisory Council, an Investor Advisory Committee, a Not-for-Profit Advisory Committee and a Public Markets Advisory Committee; its Emerging Issues Task Force addresses narrower issues within the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, and the Private Company Council proposes private-company alternatives that take effect only if the FASB endorses them.[^s1]

In practice, the board adds projects to a technical agenda, votes proposals out for public comment as proposed Accounting Standards Updates (ASUs), and issues final ASUs that amend the Codification. In the second quarter of 2026, for example, it issued ASU 2026-01 on paid-in-kind preferred dividends and ASU 2026-02 on environmental credits, and its technical agenda grew to 17 projects as of June 30, 2026, under chair Richard R. Jones.[^s6] The chair's report stressed that timeliness is part of high-quality standard setting.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Because standards shift reported earnings, the FASB has always been politically exposed. The SEC Historical Society recounts that in 1996, after a contested FASB statement on stock options, a preparer group pushed governance changes the SEC viewed as an attempt to give preparers control of the FAF; the SEC chairman threatened to withdraw ASR 150, and the FAF was reconstituted with a public and user majority.[^s3] Issuer-paid fees reduced dependence on preparers and auditors but raised questions about government influence over funding and agendas.[^s3]

Research also shows that board members' own backgrounds matter, not just lobbying. Allen and Ramanna found that FASB members with financial-services experience tended to propose standards that lower reliability and raise relevance, largely because they favored fair-value measurement, and that longer tenure was associated with the same tilt.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

The FASB is the creator of US GAAP and issues the nonauthoritative FASB conceptual framework that guides its decisions; standards such as ASC 606 on revenue recognition are its output.[^s1] It sits within the Financial Accounting Foundation, succeeded the Accounting Principles Board, and owes its statutory recognition to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.[^s2][^s3] Its global counterpart is the International Accounting Standards Board, which sets IFRS; Sarbanes-Oxley explicitly asks a recognized standard setter to consider international convergence.[^s2] By standardizing what companies report, it helps reduce information asymmetry between managers and the investors who rely on financial statements.[^s1]
