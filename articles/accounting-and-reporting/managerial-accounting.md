# Managerial Accounting: Information for Planning, Control and Decisions Inside the Firm

**Managerial accounting** (often called management accounting) is the branch of accounting that serves decision makers inside an organization. OpenStax defines it as the process that lets managers set and evaluate business goals, which includes working out what information a given decision needs and how to analyze and communicate it.[^s1] Where financial accounting reports the company as a whole to investors and creditors, managerial accounting cuts the data by product, customer or division, must arrive in time to act on, and freely mixes nonfinancial measures such as employee satisfaction, customer survey results or contracts per client with financial ones.[^s1]

## What it does

OpenStax frames the work around three recurring management responsibilities arranged in a loop around the mission statement.[^s1] **Planning** turns the mission into goals and measurable objectives, including strategic plans and budgets. **Controlling** monitors those objectives through financial, performance and physical controls, and managerial accountants produce performance reports that show variances between planned and actual results. **Evaluating** compares actual with expected results at the product, department or company level so managers can continue, modify or abandon a plan.[^s1] The textbook adds that senior managers spend relatively more time planning and lower-level managers more time evaluating.[^s1] Typical questions it answers include whether to drop a product line, whether to make or buy a component, and whether an equipment purchase such as additional MRI machines will pay for itself.[^s1]

As a profession, the Institute of Management Accountants (IMA), as quoted by OpenStax, describes management accountants as partners in management decision making who design planning and performance management systems and supply reporting and control expertise.[^s2] The role spans cost accountants, budget and financial analysts, internal auditors, controllers and CFOs, and the IMA's Certified Management Accountant (CMA) credential, a two-part exam, is the field's main certification.[^s2] OpenStax's 2019 text cites IMA figures that nearly three quarters of financial professionals work as management accountants.[^s2]

## Origins

Management accounting is older than modern external reporting. H. Thomas Johnson's NBER history traces it to 19th-century American enterprises: New England textile mills such as Lyman Mills tracked conversion cost per pound by department, railroads under managers like Albert Fink of the Louisville & Nashville built cost-per-ton-mile accounts in the late 1860s, and distributors such as Marshall Field's and Sears watched gross margin and inventory turnover.[^s3] Kaplan's survey likewise dates cost accounting's growth to 1850–1915, driven by railroads, steel and the scientific management movement.[^s4] DuPont, from 1903, and the reorganization of General Motors around 1920 then produced the tools for running decentralized divisions: return on investment as a performance yardstick, formal budgeting and incentive plans.[^s4][^s3] Later decades added discounted cash flow analysis and decision models from management science.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The field's best-known critique came from its own historians. Kaplan argued in 1984 that control procedures designed more than 60 years earlier for mass production of standard, labor-intensive products no longer fit modern firms, and that short-term profit was a poor sole basis for evaluating managers.[^s4] Johnson argued that after about 1950 American firms began steering people and subunits with numbers built for financial reporting, a practice he calls "managing by remote control," and linked it to the decline of US manufacturing competitiveness after 1960.[^s3] The two made the case jointly in *Relevance Lost: The Rise and Fall of Management Accounting* (Harvard Business School Press, 1987), a book prompted partly by Hayes and Abernathy's 1980 charge that managing by the numbers was eroding US industry.[^s6]

The remedies split the authors. Kaplan, with Robin Cooper and David Norton, championed activity-based costing (prototyped in Cooper's Schrader Bellows case) and the balanced scorecard (popularized in 1996), while Johnson came to argue that measurement-driven management was itself the problem; Cooper conceded that many firms adopting ABC never realized its benefits.[^s6] Survey evidence suggests practice changed slowly. In a questionnaire of 113 usable responses from UK food and drink firms, Abdel-Kader and Luther found that budgeting for cost control and planning, financial performance measures and product profitability analysis dominated, while ABC ranked among the least used practices; they concluded that traditional management accounting remained alive and well, with about 40% of firms classed in the two most advanced stages of IFAC's evolution model.[^s5]

## In practice

General Motors is the classic example: its early-1920s divisional reorganization relied on DuPont-style ROI reporting so that headquarters could control division managers through capital allocation.[^s3] A modern equivalent is the performance report that sets planned against actual results, flags variances for investigation and feeds what managers learn back into the next round of planning.[^s1]

## Connections

Managerial accounting is a branch of **accounting** that **contrasts with financial accounting**, which serves outside users under GAAP or IFRS.[^s1] **Cost accounting** and **budgeting** are core parts of it, and its tools extend into **capital budgeting** and analysis of a firm's **cost structure** for **decision-making**.[^s1][^s4] **Activity-based costing** and the **balanced scorecard** grew directly out of the *Relevance Lost* critique.[^s6] **General Motors** exemplifies the ROI-and-budget control system that defined the field for much of the 20th century.[^s3][^s4]
