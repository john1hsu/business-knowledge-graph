# US GAAP

US generally accepted accounting principles (US GAAP) are the standards that govern how American businesses measure, recognise and disclose transactions in their financial statements. The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), an independent nonprofit, writes GAAP for both public and private companies, while the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) enforces it for public registrants and has delegated the standard-setting task to the FASB.[^s1] Most other countries instead use International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), issued by the IASB.[^s1]

## Origin and authority

GAAP is a private-sector product sitting on a public-law foundation. Congress gave the SEC power to prescribe how financial reports are prepared, but in 1938 the Commission let the accounting profession set the standards while retaining oversight. The AICPA's Committee on Accounting Procedure took the role in 1939, the Accounting Principles Board replaced it in 1959, and after the Wheat Commission recommended a full-time body independent of the AICPA, the Financial Accounting Foundation appointed the FASB.[^s6] The SEC recognised FASB pronouncements as authoritative in Accounting Series Release No. 150 in December 1973.[^s2]

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 put that arrangement on a statutory footing. Section 108 added Section 19(b) to the Securities Act, allowing the SEC to treat a private body's principles as "generally accepted" if it meets conditions such as a public-interest board of trustees, independent funding and procedures for promptly addressing emerging issues. In April 2003 the Commission found that the FASB and its parent foundation satisfied those criteria.[^s2]

## How it is organised

Until 2009, GAAP was scattered across FASB Statements, APB Opinions, EITF abstracts and other pronouncements. FASB Statement No. 168 made the Accounting Standards Codification the single source of authoritative nongovernmental GAAP for periods ending after September 15, 2009. The Codification reorganised existing rules into roughly 90 topics without substantively changing them, and the FASB now amends it through Accounting Standards Updates rather than stand-alone statements. For SEC registrants, SEC rules, interpretive releases and staff bulletins remain additional sources of GAAP.[^s5]

Underneath the topical rules sit shared principles and assumptions. Textbook treatments list revenue recognition, expense recognition (matching), the historical-cost principle and full disclosure, alongside the going-concern, monetary-unit, time-period and separate-entity assumptions.[^s1] Because the FASB writes for a single legal and tax system, its guidance tends to be more detailed and industry-specific than IFRS, which must work across more than 120 jurisdictions.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The most influential critique is that GAAP became too rules-based. In a 2003 study mandated by Sarbanes-Oxley, SEC staff argued that bright-line thresholds let preparers engineer transactions to land on the preferred side of a line, that exceptions produce different accounting for economically similar deals, and that detailed guidance invites a cycle of financial engineering followed by still more guidance. The staff warned that reporting can become compliance rather than communication.[^s3] Its examples included lease classification, which relied on 75% and 90% tests spread across dozens of pronouncements, and the old pooling-of-interests rules, under which a one-percentage-point difference in ownership could change the accounting method for a merger.[^s3]

The study did not endorse pure principles-only standards either, noting they can lack operational guidance, reduce comparability and invite after-the-fact disputes with regulators and plaintiffs. It recommended "objectives-oriented" standards grounded in a coherent conceptual framework, with few exceptions and enough implementation guidance to be usable.[^s3]

## In practice

A practical boundary of GAAP is the earnings release. Companies frequently publish adjusted metrics such as EBITDA or adjusted earnings that depart from GAAP. Regulation G, effective March 28, 2003, requires any public disclosure of such a non-GAAP measure to be accompanied by the most directly comparable GAAP figure and a quantitative reconciliation. In SEC filings, Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K additionally bars companies from labelling as non-recurring a charge that is reasonably likely to recur within two years, and from placing non-GAAP measures on the face of the GAAP statements.[^s4] For analysts, the reconciliation is where judgement about "true" performance usually starts.

## Connections

GAAP is the operative rulebook of financial accounting, and it builds on the FASB conceptual framework, which the FASB uses to keep individual standards consistent, the remedy the SEC study recommended.[^s3] It is created by the Financial Accounting Standards Board under SEC oversight strengthened by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act.[^s2] Its core recognition rules embody accrual accounting and the matching principle, and topics such as ASC 606 revenue recognition are components of the Codification.[^s1][^s5] It contrasts with IFRS, the principal alternative used internationally, and it defines the baseline against which non-GAAP financial measures must be reconciled.[^s1][^s4]
