# Financial Accounting: Reporting a Firm's Results to Outside Stakeholders

**Financial accounting** is the branch of accounting that measures how an organization has performed and where it stands financially, using standard conventions, and then prepares and distributes reports on those results. Its audience sits outside the firm: owners and shareholders, lenders, and government bodies such as the SEC and the IRS.[^s1] OpenStax contrasts it with managerial accounting, which mixes financial and nonfinancial data to support budgeting, pricing and costing decisions inside the organization and is not subject to the same external oversight.[^s1]

## Objective and users

In the United States, the Financial Accounting Standards Board's conceptual framework states the purpose formally. General purpose financial reporting exists to give existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors information that helps them decide whether to provide resources to the entity, whether by buying, selling or holding its securities or by extending credit.[^s2] These users are singled out because they cannot demand tailored information from the company and must rely on published reports; management, by contrast, can obtain whatever it needs internally.[^s2] The framework is explicit that the reports are not meant to show what the company is worth. They supply inputs that help users estimate value themselves.[^s2]

## What it produces

The output is a set of financial statements. The SEC's investor guide describes the balance sheet as a snapshot of what a company owns and owes at one date, organized around assets equal to liabilities plus shareholders' equity; the income statement as revenue less costs and expenses over a period; the cash flow statement as cash in and out from operating, investing and financing activities; and the statement of shareholders' equity as the change in owners' interests.[^s3] The guide stresses that the footnotes carry information the statements omit, such as accounting policies, pension funding and stock compensation, and that management's discussion and analysis must disclose known trends and uncertainties likely to have a material effect.[^s3]

Underneath the statements is accrual measurement. The FASB framework holds that recording transactions in the periods in which they occur, even when cash moves at another time, gives a better basis for judging past and future performance than cash receipts and payments alone.[^s2] The framework also asks reporting to distinguish changes in resources that come from performance from those that come from financing, such as issuing debt or equity.[^s2]

## Oversight

Because outsiders cannot check the books themselves, financial accounting operates within an institutional structure. OpenStax names the SEC, the FASB, the American Institute of CPAs and the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board as the main overseers in the United States, noting that the PCAOB was created by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 after a wave of corporate frauds.[^s1] For listed companies the annual Form 10-K is the central vehicle: its financial statements section contains the audited income statement, balance sheets and statement of cash flows, while MD&A lets management explain the year's results in its own words.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The classic test of whether financial accounting tells investors anything is Ball and Brown's 1968 study of earnings and stock returns. Replicating it fifty years later, Ball and Brown report that annual earnings still capture a large share of the information priced over a year, in the US and 16 other countries, although much of that information reaches prices before the announcement.[^s5] They also find that, in US data, the share of price-relevant news contained in earnings at announcement has fallen, while urging more caution about that trend than critics such as Lev and Gu.[^s5]

That critique has a longer history. Lev and Zarowin (1999) found that earnings and earnings changes explained 6–12% of return variation in 1977–1986 but only 4–8% in 1987–1996, with similar declines for book values and cash flows.[^s6] They tied the decline to faster business change and to rising investment in intangibles such as R&D, which conservative rules expense immediately, and proposed capitalizing such spending once projects pass a feasibility threshold and restating reports as outcomes become known.[^s6]

## In practice

A practical example is the annual report a public company files with the SEC. An analyst reading a 10-K starts from the audited statements, then reads the footnotes and MD&A to understand the policies, estimates and risks behind the numbers.[^s3][^s4] The same statements feed credit decisions by lenders and valuation models built by equity investors, which is exactly the decision-usefulness the conceptual framework sets as the goal.[^s2]

## Connections

Financial accounting is a branch of accounting and **contrasts with managerial accounting**, its internally focused counterpart.[^s1] Its rules in the US are **regulated by the Financial Accounting Standards Board** and codified as **US GAAP**, with **IFRS** serving the same role in most other jurisdictions; both draw on conceptual frameworks such as the **FASB Conceptual Framework**, which states its objective.[^s1][^s2] It rests on **double-entry bookkeeping** and **accrual accounting**, and the **statement of cash flows** is one of its core outputs.[^s2][^s3] By giving outsiders information they cannot otherwise obtain, it helps **mitigate information asymmetry** between managers and capital providers.[^s2] The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** strengthened its oversight, and **financial statement analysis** is the toolkit users apply to its output.[^s1][^s4]
