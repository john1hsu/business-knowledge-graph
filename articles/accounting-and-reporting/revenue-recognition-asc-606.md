# Revenue from Contracts with Customers

*Revenue from Contracts with Customers* is the single, principles-based revenue standard that the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) issued jointly in May 2014: as Accounting Standards Update 2014-09, which created Topic 606 of the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, and as IFRS 15.[^s1][^s2] Its core principle is that a company recognises revenue to depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers, measured at the consideration it expects to be entitled to in exchange.[^s1]

## Origin

The boards started from problems on both sides of the Atlantic. Under IFRS, the old standards (IAS 18 *Revenue* and IAS 11 *Construction Contracts*) offered little guidance on matters such as multiple-element arrangements, so some companies borrowed selectively from US rules and practice diverged. Under US GAAP, broad concepts were layered with many industry- and transaction-specific rules, so economically similar deals could be booked differently. Revenue disclosures in both regimes were frequently boilerplate.[^s2] The project ran through a 2008 discussion paper, a 2010 exposure draft and a revised 2011 exposure draft, drawing more than 1,500 comment letters and over 650 stakeholder meetings.[^s2] IFRS 15 replaced IAS 11, IAS 18 and four interpretations, and after a 2015 deferral it took effect for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018; a 2016 amendment clarified application without changing the principles.[^s1] OpenStax's accounting text describes the US update as replacing industry-specific practices with a principle-based approach.[^s3]

## How it works: the five steps

The standard applies one model to every industry:[^s1][^s3]

1. **Identify the contract** with a customer.
2. **Identify the performance obligations**, the distinct goods or services promised in it.
3. **Determine the transaction price**, including estimates of variable consideration such as rebates or bonuses.
4. **Allocate the price** to each performance obligation in proportion to stand-alone selling prices.
5. **Recognise revenue** when (or as) each obligation is satisfied, at a point in time or over time.[^s1][^s3]

Revenue arrives only at the fifth step, so it can be recorded before or after the cash is collected.[^s3] Companies could adopt either with full retrospective restatement of prior periods or with a cumulative-effect adjustment at adoption plus extra disclosures comparing the old and new results.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The IASB's post-implementation review, completed on 30 September 2024, found that IFRS 15 is working as intended: no fundamental flaws in its core principles, benefits to investors close to expectations and costs not significantly above them.[^s5] The review still flagged persistent judgement areas, notably consideration payable to customers and principal-versus-agent assessments for services and intangibles, and pushed sale-and-leaseback questions into the review of IFRS 16 *Leases*.[^s5]

Academic evidence suggests the effects were concentrated rather than universal. A study of US software firms, which typically bundle several deliverables across reporting periods, compared them with electronic-computer makers over 2016–2019. The average cumulative adoption adjustment equalled 4.4% of total assets for software firms against 1.6% for computer firms, and only the software firms showed better comparability, a tighter link between revenue accruals and cash collections, and some gain in earnings informativeness.[^s6] Disclosure volume rose in both industries.[^s6] The recurring critique is that a single principles-based model leans heavily on judgement, about which promises are distinct, how to estimate variable consideration and who controls a service, and the review's list of open issues shows where that judgement still produces difficulty in practice.[^s1][^s5]

## In practice: Microsoft

Microsoft adopted the standard early, from 1 July 2017, using the full retrospective method. In an August 2017 Form 8-K it reported a material effect on its income statements and balance sheets but none on cash flows. The biggest change was Windows 10: revenue previously deferred and spread over a device's life would now be recognised mainly at billing and delivery. Licence revenue in certain multi-year commercial agreements bundled with Software Assurance also moved to contract execution, while Office 365, LinkedIn and hardware revenue were largely unaffected.[^s4] The case illustrates the standard's central lesson for analysts: the timing of reported revenue can change sharply while cash, and underlying economics, do not.

## Connections

ASC 606 is a component of US GAAP, issued by the Financial Accounting Standards Board, and its twin IFRS 15 is part of IFRS, issued by the International Accounting Standards Board; the joint project was a high point of convergence between the two regimes.[^s1][^s2] It is the operative rule set for accrual accounting's revenue side, deciding when earned revenue is recognised irrespective of cash.[^s3] It also governs when deferred revenue, cash collected ahead of performance, is released into revenue, since revenue is recognised only as each obligation is satisfied.[^s1][^s3] Microsoft's Windows 10 restatement exemplifies how the standard reshapes reported revenue for software companies.[^s4][^s6]
