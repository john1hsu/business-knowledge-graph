# Accrual Accounting

**Accrual accounting** is the basis of accounting that records the economic effects of transactions and other events in the periods in which those effects occur, even when the related cash arrives or leaves in a different period. The FASB's conceptual framework treats it as the foundation of general purpose financial reporting, arguing that it gives users a better basis for judging an entity's past and future performance than information about cash receipts and payments alone.[^s1] Its counterpart, cash-basis accounting, records a transaction only when money changes hands.[^s2]

## Origin and standing

Accrual ideas are old: one recent working paper notes they have circulated in accounting for hundreds of years, though their modern formal definitions are much younger.[^s5] In the United States, the accrual basis is embedded in the FASB's Concepts Statement No. 8, which links accrual-based measures of financial performance to an entity's ability to generate net cash inflows from operations rather than from new financing by investors and creditors.[^s1] Public companies reporting under US GAAP or IFRS must use the accrual basis, and lenders commonly ask borrowers for accrual-basis income statements.[^s2]

## How it works

Two recognition principles drive the method. Under the revenue recognition principle, revenue is reported once it is earned, meaning goods have been delivered or services performed, regardless of when the customer pays. Under the expense recognition (matching) principle, expenses are reported in the period they are incurred to produce that revenue.[^s3] Because recognition and cash can fall in different periods, significant long-lived purchases such as equipment are capitalized and depreciated over their useful lives (straight-line, double-declining-balance or units-of-production) instead of being expensed when paid for.[^s3] Each entry follows double-entry bookkeeping, so debits equal credits and the accounting equation stays balanced.[^s3]

Researchers distinguish between *deferrals*, which mirror a past cash flow in amount and timing with little estimation (capitalizing the purchase of plant and equipment), and *accruals proper*, which spread cash flows across periods and require judgment (depreciation schedules, collectability of receivables).[^s5]

## Trade-offs versus the cash basis

OpenStax summarizes the accrual basis as giving a truer picture of profitability across periods and better comparability for investors and lenders, at the cost of more recordkeeping and complexity. The cash basis is simpler and remains common among small startups, farms and professional practices, but its timing can distort results when cash lags the underlying activity.[^s2] Tax rules shape the choice too: the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act expanded access to the cash method to most taxpayers meeting a $25 million average annual gross receipts test under Sec. 448(c), up from $5 million under prior law, and relaxed the inventory restriction for those taxpayers, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2017 (figures as reported in January 2019).[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The biggest empirical finding about accruals is that they are less reliable predictors of future earnings than cash flows. Richard Sloan's 1996 study in *The Accounting Review* found that the accrual component of earnings is less persistent than the cash flow component, yet stock prices behaved as if investors could not tell the two apart. A portfolio long low-accrual firms and short high-accrual firms earned size-adjusted returns of about 10.4% in the first year, with much of the return arriving around later earnings announcements.[^s4] This "accrual anomaly" turned accrual quality into a standard screen for earnings management and aggressive estimates.

A second critique is conceptual. Dichev (2025) argues that the dominant balance-sheet definition of accruals, as net changes in non-cash assets and liabilities, misses their real role of moving cash flows into the right income period, and that net figures can hide large offsetting gross flows.[^s5] The framework view accepts the estimation risk as the price of a more informative performance measure.[^s1]

## In practice

OpenStax illustrates the stakes with a landscaping business that took in $1,400 of cash revenue in its first month and kept $250 after expenses; whether that business is actually profitable depends on matching costs to the work they paid for, not on when the cash moved.[^s2] For larger companies the same logic explains why reported profit and cash flow can diverge in any given period: OpenStax notes that the timing of cash can differ from when revenues and expenses are recorded.[^s3]

## Connections

Accrual accounting is a core concept within financial accounting and is set out in the FASB conceptual framework, which supplies the rationale that GAAP standards build on.[^s1] It depends on the matching principle and finds its most detailed modern expression in revenue recognition rules such as ASC 606.[^s3] It contrasts with cash-basis accounting, which remains an option mainly for small, cash-heavy businesses.[^s2] Because accrual earnings differ from cash, it is closely tied to working capital and free cash flow analysis, and Sloan's evidence on accrual persistence is why analysts compare the two.[^s4]
