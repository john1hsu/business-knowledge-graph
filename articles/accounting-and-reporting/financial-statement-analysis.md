# Financial Statement Analysis: Ratios, Trends and Comparisons for Evaluating Firm Performance

Financial statement analysis is the review of the information in a company's financial statements to support decisions such as budgeting, cost reduction, revenue growth and capital investment.[^s1] Investors, lenders, suppliers and managers all use it, because expressing raw figures as ratios and percentages strips out differences in company size and makes firms comparable with one another and with their own past.[^s2] The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission stresses that no single statement tells the whole story: changes on the balance sheet flow through revenues and expenses, and cash flow is related to, but not the same as, net income, so the statements are best read together.[^s3]

## How it works

Analysts typically combine three techniques. **Horizontal (trend) analysis** tracks one line item across periods, computing the dollar change from a base year and that change as a percentage of the base-year amount.[^s1] **Vertical (common-size) analysis** expresses each line as a percentage of a base figure in the same period: net sales on the income statement, and total assets or total liabilities plus equity on the balance sheet.[^s1] **Ratio analysis** relates figures to each other, and OpenStax groups the ratios into four families:[^s1][^s2]

- *Liquidity*, the ability to meet short-term obligations: working capital, the current ratio and the quick ratio.[^s1]
- *Solvency*, the ability to meet long-term obligations: debt-to-equity and times interest earned (EBIT over interest expense).[^s1]
- *Efficiency*, how well assets are managed: receivables turnover, inventory turnover, days' sales in inventory and total asset turnover.[^s1]
- *Profitability*, returns from operations: profit margin, return on total assets and return on equity.[^s1]

Investors usually add market measures. The SEC's guide, for example, explains the price-to-earnings ratio (share price over earnings per share) alongside operating margin, inventory turnover and debt-to-equity.[^s3] Because a balance sheet is a snapshot at one date, turnover ratios use an average of beginning and ending balances, and the ratios themselves are computed by the reader rather than printed in the statements.[^s3] The SEC also urges readers to study the footnotes, which disclose accounting policies that often involve management's most subjective judgments, and Management's Discussion and Analysis, where companies must describe known trends and uncertainties that could materially affect results.[^s3]

## Interpreting the numbers

A ratio means little in isolation. OpenStax's *Introduction to Business* recommends comparing ratios over three to five years, against industry averages and against similar firms, and notes that because the inputs are historical, ratio analysis flags potential problems rather than proving them.[^s4] Norms differ sharply by sector: a current ratio near 2 has traditionally been considered healthy, but grocers turn inventory far faster than heavy-equipment makers, and acceptable margins vary widely.[^s4] A high return on equity can simply reflect high risk, and lenders often write minimum ratio levels into loan covenants.[^s4] The SEC likewise advises comparing ratios only within the same industry.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The textbook limitations are consistent: past performance may not predict future results; inflation or recession can distort the figures; changes in when or where transactions are recorded may not be visible to users; and although GAAP compliance is required, estimates and flexibility in applying standards let firms present similar economics differently.[^s2][^s1] Ratios computed on accrual figures are therefore only as reliable as the underlying accounting.

The strongest evidence for ratio analysis comes from failure prediction. William Beaver's 1966 *Journal of Accounting Research* study, "Financial Ratios As Predictors of Failure", treated accounting ratios as predictors of business failure and has been cited by more than 2,400 articles.[^s7] Edward Altman's 1968 Z-score moved from single ratios to a weighted multivariate score built for manufacturers from five ratios scaled mostly by total assets: working capital, retained earnings, EBIT, sales, and market value of equity to book liabilities, with zones labelled safe, grey and distress.[^s5] Altman places this in a lineage that ran from qualitative judgment in the 1800s through univariate measures such as early rating-agency assessments and corporate systems like DuPont's.[^s5] His later reviews also show the limits: rising leverage and a changing credit market increased missed defaults, separate models were needed for private firms, emerging markets and SMEs, and for SMEs the score should not be used alone.[^s5] In 2016 he told NYU Stern that the most common misuse is applying the model across all sectors, since service and retail firms tend to score higher than manufacturers.[^s6]

## In practice

A credit analyst reviewing a borrower might run a five-year trend of the current and quick ratios, a common-size income statement to see whether gross margin is eroding, and a debt-to-equity and interest-coverage check against covenant thresholds.[^s1][^s4] An equity analyst would add profitability and market ratios, then break return on equity into margin, turnover and leverage with DuPont analysis.[^s1][^s3] In OpenStax's worked example, a small firm's 1.4 current ratio, 1.1 quick ratio and 89.1% debt-to-equity are judged against its history and its industry rather than against fixed rules.[^s4]

## Connections

Financial statement analysis sits within financial accounting, whose rules (GAAP) shape every input, and supports corporate finance decisions on credit, investment and valuation.[^s1][^s2] It is the parent technique for several existing nodes: DuPont analysis decomposes return on equity, working capital and the current ratio measure liquidity, and the statement of cash flows, free cash flow and EBITDA extend the analysis from accrual earnings toward cash.[^s1][^s3] Debt-to-equity and return on equity are among its core ratios.[^s1][^s4] Multivariate distress models such as the Altman Z-score grew directly out of ratio analysis.[^s5]
