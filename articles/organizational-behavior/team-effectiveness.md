# Team Effectiveness

Team effectiveness is the extent to which a work team produces results that matter to the people who depend on it, and it is also the name of the research field that tries to explain why some teams deliver those results and others do not. Mathieu, Maynard, Rapp and Gilson's 2008 review in the *Journal of Management* treats effectiveness as a set of outcomes valued by one or more constituencies, chiefly team performance and members' affective reactions, and adopts a definition of work teams as interdependent collectives that share goals, interact socially, manage boundaries and operate inside an organizational context.[^s1] A 2015 National Research Council report summarizes it more simply as a team's capacity to reach its goals, including member satisfaction alongside tangible outputs.[^s2]

## Criteria: what counts as effective

The most widely taught criteria come from J. Richard Hackman. The OpenStax *Organizational Behavior* text lists three: productive output that meets or exceeds the organization's quantity and quality standards, satisfaction of members' personal needs, and the group's capacity to cooperate on future tasks.[^s3] Research reviews group the outcomes similarly into performance, attitudes and behaviors. Mathieu and colleagues note that performance is the most studied criterion yet often the least precisely specified, and that measures of member affect and team viability have received less attention and tend to blur into performance ratings.[^s1]

## How it works: from IPO to IMOI

Most research uses the input-process-output (IPO) framework proposed by Joseph McGrath in 1964. Inputs are antecedents such as member characteristics, team design, task features and organizational context; processes are the interactions through which members combine their efforts; outputs are the effectiveness criteria.[^s1][^s2] Hackman's own version adds intermediate criteria (group effort, pooled knowledge and skill, and suitable performance strategies), whose relative weight depends on the work: routine tasks hinge on effort, while complex work such as R&D also needs skill and knowledge.[^s3]

Critics argued that many of the variables linking inputs to outputs are not processes at all. Marks, Mathieu and Zaccaro (2001) separated processes, which are things members do, from emergent states such as cohesion, collective efficacy, trust and shared mental models, which are cognitive, motivational or affective properties of the team.[^s1] Ilgen and colleagues (2005) then proposed the input-mediator-output-input (IMOI) model, which widens "process" to any mediator and adds a feedback loop in which a team's outputs, such as past performance, become inputs to its next cycle of work.[^s1][^s4] Mathieu's review classifies processes into transition processes (planning, goal setting), action processes (coordination, monitoring, backup behavior) and interpersonal processes (conflict and affect management).[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Kozlowski and Ilgen's 2006 monograph concluded that there is a solid basis for an emerging science of team effectiveness, and that organizations can pull several levers (composition, training and leadership) to shape the processes that drive results.[^s5][^s2] The National Research Council chapter reports meta-analytic correlations of roughly .41 between team efficacy and performance and about .34 between shared leadership and effectiveness, finds that cohesion matters more when members are highly interdependent, and notes that relationship and process conflict tend to hurt teams while task conflict has more mixed effects.[^s2] Shared mental models and transactive memory (knowing who knows what) are positively related to team processes and performance.[^s2]

The field has well-known limitations. Mediators such as cohesion, trust and efficacy are highly intercorrelated, partly because they are usually measured at the same time from the same respondents, so Mathieu and colleagues call for time-lagged designs and multiple data sources.[^s1] Composition findings are mixed: demographic diversity often appears harmful at first but its effects may fade as members work together longer.[^s1] Much of the evidence comes from military, business and health care teams, and the committee observed that little research tests interventions for building cohesion.[^s2] Classic stage models such as Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing sequence were derived from therapy and clinical groups, which limits their fit with organizational work teams.[^s4] OpenStax also cautions that cohesiveness alone does not guarantee effectiveness, because a cohesive group may hold norms at odds with the organization's goals.[^s3]

## In practice

Google's people-analytics study, published on its re:Work site, shows the measurement problem in action. To define effectiveness for 180 teams (115 engineering teams and 65 sales pods), researchers combined executive, team-leader and team-member evaluations with sales performance against quarterly quota, because the subjective ratings captured culture while the sales figures ignored context.[^s6] They concluded that how members worked together mattered more than who was on the team, and named five dynamics: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact.[^s6] In IMOI terms these are mostly emergent states, which is why the result fits the research literature rather than overturning it.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

Team effectiveness sits under **organizational behavior** as its group-level outcome. It is the broader concept beneath **high-performance teams**, which describes teams at the top of the effectiveness distribution, and it was shaped by **J. Richard Hackman**'s criteria and by Joseph McGrath's IPO framework.[^s3][^s1] It depends on emergent states including **psychological safety** and shared mental models, and Google's **Project Aristotle** is a corporate example of measuring it.[^s2][^s6] Its process side overlaps with **group decision-making** and its failure modes include **groupthink**, while **leadership** is one of the main levers for improving it.[^s2][^s5]
