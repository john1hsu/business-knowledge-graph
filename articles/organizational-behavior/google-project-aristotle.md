# Google Project Aristotle

**Project Aristotle** was Google's internal research effort to work out what makes a team effective. Run by Google's People Operations (HR) function, it examined 180 teams, 115 engineering project teams and 65 sales pods spanning high and low performers, ranging from 3 to 50 people with a median of nine.[^s1] Its headline conclusion was that the way a team works together matters more than who is on it, and that **psychological safety** is the most important of five dynamics that separate effective teams from the rest.[^s1] Google first shared the findings publicly in a November 2015 re:Work post by People Operations analyst Julia Rozovsky.[^s2]

## Origin

Aristotle followed **Project Oxygen**, the people-analytics study that Google's People Analytics group, led by Prasad Setty, began in early 2009 to test whether managers mattered at all.[^s3] Oxygen set out to prove that they did not, found instead that small gains in manager quality were linked to lower turnover and higher satisfaction, and distilled eight behaviors of good managers that Google then fed into upward-feedback surveys and training.[^s3] Aristotle applied the same evidence-driven approach to teams rather than individual managers.[^s1]

## How the study worked

The researchers first separated *teams*, which are highly interdependent and plan, decide and review work together on a project, from looser *work groups* organized by reporting line, and let members define their own team boundaries rather than relying on the org chart.[^s1] Because no single measure of effectiveness existed, they combined four: executive ratings, team-lead ratings, team-member ratings, and sales performance against quarterly quota.[^s1] They held hundreds of double-blind interviews, drew on more than 250 items from Google's employee engagement survey and its longitudinal gDNA study, and ran more than 35 statistical models on hundreds of variables, keeping only factors that were robust across outcomes and team types.[^s1] The work spanned two years.[^s2]

## The five dynamics

In ranked order, the effective teams showed: (1) psychological safety, the sense that members can admit mistakes or float ideas without embarrassment or punishment; (2) dependability, completing quality work on time; (3) structure and clarity of roles, plans and goals, which Google often sets with OKRs; (4) meaning, the personal importance of the work; and (5) impact, the belief that the work makes a difference.[^s1] Google reported that teams high in psychological safety were less likely to lose members, made more use of teammates' diverse ideas, brought in more revenue, and were twice as often rated effective by executives.[^s2] Factors that did *not* significantly predict effectiveness at Google included colocation, consensus decision-making, extroversion, individual performance, workload, seniority, team size and tenure, though Google cautioned they may matter elsewhere.[^s1]

## In practice

Google turned the findings into a ten-minute "gTeams" pulse survey on the five dynamics, followed by a facilitated team discussion; by late 2015 more than 3,000 employees on 300 teams had used it.[^s2] Teams that committed to a new norm improved their psychological-safety ratings by 6% and structure-and-clarity ratings by 10%.[^s2] The guide recommends Amy Edmondson's seven-item scale to measure safety and her advice to frame work as a learning problem, admit one's own fallibility and model curiosity.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Aristotle is an internal company study, published as a practitioner guide rather than a peer-reviewed paper, so its models and data cannot be independently checked.[^s1] Its effectiveness measures were largely ratings by executives, leads and members, and a 2017 meta-analysis of 136 samples found psychological safety correlated far more strongly with subjective performance ratings than with objective performance measures, warning also of same-source bias and a shortage of longitudinal designs.[^s4] The same meta-analysis nonetheless found that psychological safety predicted task performance at both individual and group levels and added explanatory power beyond work engagement and leader relations, which broadly supports Google's emphasis.[^s4] Garvin's account of Oxygen flags parallel limits of Google's people analytics, such as unclear links to bottom-line results and the ease with which rivals can copy published behaviors.[^s3] Independent laboratory work points the same way as Aristotle's composition-versus-process finding: Woolley, Malone, Chabris, Pentland and Hashmi found in 2010 that a group's collective intelligence tracked members' social sensitivity and evenly shared conversational turns, not average or maximum individual intelligence.[^s5][^s6]

## Connections

Project Aristotle sits under **people analytics**, the data-driven HR practice Google built with Oxygen, which it **follows**, and under **high-performance teams**, the outcome it set out to explain.[^s1][^s3] It **exemplifies psychological safety** and was **influenced by Amy Edmondson**, whose definition and measurement scale Google adopted.[^s1] It **applies to team effectiveness** as a field of study, links to **OKRs** through the structure-and-clarity dynamic, and is part of the management history of **Google**.[^s1][^s2] The collective-intelligence research is a useful academic counterpart to its findings.[^s5]
