# Centralization and Decentralization: How Far Authority Is Pushed Down the Hierarchy

**Centralization** is the extent to which formal authority is concentrated in one place or level of an organization, usually its top. **Decentralization** is the opposite movement: shifting the power to make and carry out decisions down the hierarchy so that lower-level managers and staff carry more responsibility.[^s1] Managerial accounting texts frame the two as ends of a single spectrum. At one end a single leader sets strategic direction and allocates resources; at the other, decisions are made at many levels, typically after the business is split into segments whose results can be measured separately.[^s2]

## Origin and measurement

The idea of degree of centralization as a measurable property of structure owes much to the Aston studies of the 1960s. Derek Pugh and colleagues profiled 46 British organizations on a handful of structural dimensions, among them specialization, standardization, formalization, configuration and centralization.[^s6] Centralization was scored from key-informant interviews about where in the hierarchy particular decisions and control systems sat, where the information behind them came from, and how often decisions were reviewed above.[^s6] Unlike the other dimensions, which moved together, centralization correlated only weakly and negatively with them in the Aston data.[^s6]

Economists later built firm-level survey measures. In a 2006 telephone survey of plant managers at almost 4,000 medium-sized manufacturers in twelve countries, Nicholas Bloom, Raffaella Sadun and John Van Reenen scored decentralization from four questions: how much capital a plant manager can spend without headquarters approval, and how much autonomy the manager has over hiring, new product introduction, and sales and marketing.[^s3]

## Trade-offs

Centralization gives senior managers a broad view of operations and tight financial control, and can cut costs by removing duplicated work. Its costs are slower responses to customers and fewer chances for junior staff to build judgment and leadership skills.[^s1] Decentralization tends to speed decisions, encourage creativity and flexibility, develop managers faster and lift job satisfaction, but it risks expensive errors by under-skilled staff, poor communication, competing objectives and duplicated effort.[^s1] The managerial accounting view adds coordination problems, higher administrative costs, inconsistent practices across divisions and the danger that units become self-serving fiefdoms; it also notes that new small businesses usually start out centralized.[^s2]

Introductory texts suggest decentralization fits very large firms and those that are geographically dispersed or operate in fast-moving environments, citing Unilever, Costco and Bank of America for size and Hilton, Coca-Cola and Samsung for spread.[^s1] Underneath these lists is an economic trade-off: delegating lets people act on local information headquarters lacks, while keeping authority central limits the risk that local managers pursue their own goals.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Bloom, Sadun and Van Reenen found that firms are more decentralized where generalized trust and the rule of law are stronger, that affiliates of multinationals carry traces of their home country's trust levels, and that cultural, legal and economic factors together explain about four-fifths of cross-country differences in decentralization.[^s3] They argue decentralization lets firms grow larger, which links organizational design to national productivity gaps.[^s3]

The payoff appears to depend on the environment. Using World Management Survey data on roughly 1,300 firms in ten OECD countries and US Census data on about 8,700 plants, Philippe Aghion and co-authors showed that firms that had delegated more authority to plant managers before the Great Recession outperformed centralized rivals in the sectors hit hardest by the crisis.[^s4] Before the downturn there was no such advantage, and the gains were larger where product churn and stock-market volatility rose most, supporting a "localist" view that turbulence raises the value of local information.[^s5] The authors estimate that these organizational differences account for about 15% of international differences in post-crisis GDP growth.[^s4]

The concept also has critics. A 2025 meta-analysis by John Wagner of 155 publications spanning 1955 to 2020 found that correlations among the classic structural dimensions, including decentralization, weakened markedly in later studies, and concluded that the bureaucratic dimensional model no longer gives an unchallenged foundation for structure research.[^s6] Survey measures also capture formal autonomy reported by plant managers, which may differ from who actually controls decisions.[^s3]

## Example

OpenStax contrasts Apple, described as centralized around its CEO's strategic direction, with Johnson & Johnson, which ran as a decentralized group of operating companies under CEO William Weldon from 2002 to 2012.[^s2] Decentralized firms like J&J rely on responsibility accounting, holding each unit's manager accountable for the results of the decisions delegated to them.[^s2]

## Connections

Centralization is a core dimension of **organizational structure** and a long-standing subject of **organization theory**, measured first in the **Aston studies** and later in the **World Management Survey**.[^s6][^s3][^s4] It is the aggregate, organization-wide counterpart of **decision rights** and **delegation**, which concern who holds authority over particular decisions, and it interacts with **span of control**, which OpenStax discusses alongside it.[^s1] The trade-off at its heart rests on **information asymmetry** between headquarters and local managers and the **agency costs** of delegating to them, and its fit with size, dispersion and turbulence is a classic **contingency theory** argument.[^s5][^s1] **General Motors** is linked as the classic historical case of a decentralized divisional firm, covered in its own node, while **responsibility accounting**, which ties each unit's results to the manager given authority over it, is the control system that makes decentralization workable.[^s2]
