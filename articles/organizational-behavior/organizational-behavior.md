# Organizational Behavior

**Organizational behavior (OB)** is the study of how people behave in organizations. It applies the social and behavioral sciences to understand and predict human behavior at work, with the practical aim of making organizations both more effective and more humane.[^s1] It sits between psychology-heavy topics such as motivation and the structural questions handled by organization theory.

## Levels of analysis and the OpenStax model

OB examines behavior at three levels: the **individual** (perception, attitudes, motivation, decision making), the **group** (teams, norms, communication, conflict) and the **organization as a whole**.[^s1] Scholars often split the field into *micro* OB, which covers individuals and groups, and *macro* OB, which covers organization-wide design and relations with the environment; the OpenStax textbook argues the two must be integrated because structural choices shape individual outcomes such as motivation.[^s1]

The same textbook offers a simple systems model. Organizations draw inputs from their environment (capital, materials, labor, community and government support) and convert them into outcomes at three levels: goal attainment for the organization, effectiveness for groups and performance for individuals. In between sit five building blocks: individuals and groups, tasks and technology, organization design, organizational processes such as leadership, communication, decision making and power, and management, which holds the others together.[^s1] OB borrows from psychology, sociology, cultural anthropology, economics and political science, but differs from each by making the organization the setting and object of analysis.[^s1]

## Origins: from scientific management to human relations

The field's intellectual birth is usually traced to the **Hawthorne experiments**, a nine-year research program run from 1924 to 1933 at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago, the manufacturing arm of AT&T.[^s2] Harvard Business School's Elton Mayo and his protégé Fritz Roethlisberger led the work, which produced hourly output charts and interviews with thousands of employees, and moved management study from a purely engineering approach toward a multidisciplinary one centered on workers' needs and motivations.[^s2] Baker Library's history credits these studies with laying the groundwork for the human relations movement and for OB itself, and notes that later analysis linked them to now-standard ideas about job satisfaction, group norms, resistance to change, participation and leadership.[^s3] The exhibit describes this as a shift from a scientific to a multidisciplinary approach, a break with the engineering focus of scientific management.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

OB's founding story has itself been scrutinized. The "Hawthorne effect", which Roethlisberger described as subjects changing their performance because they are being observed, became one of social science's best-known ideas.[^s3] Yet when economists Steven Levitt and John List located the long-lost data from the first illumination experiments in 2009, they found the famous accounts of output rising with any change in lighting were unsupported, though the data showed hints of subtler observation effects.[^s4]

A broader critique concerns whether OB research actually reaches practice. In her 2005 Academy of Management presidential address, published in 2006, Denise Rousseau argued that managers rarely base practices on the best available evidence. She noted that well-supported practices such as goal setting and performance feedback remain underused, that discredited ideas such as Herzberg's two-factor theory persist in textbooks, and that one survey found fewer than 1 percent of HR managers regularly read academic research.[^s5] Her call for **evidence-based management** framed OB's main weakness as a research-practice gap rather than a lack of findings.[^s5]

## In practice

Managers use OB whenever they diagnose why a team underperforms, redesign jobs, set goals, give feedback or plan a change program. Rousseau gives the example of a health-system director who restructured clinic performance reports around the research finding that people can process only a limited amount of information at once, after which performance improved.[^s5] The Hawthorne program itself began as an applied plant study, and its interview program and test-room findings were later spread through Harvard's teaching and writing on human relations.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

OB is a branch of **management** and of the wider **social sciences**, drawing on the disciplines listed above.[^s1] Its closest sibling is **organization theory**, which takes the macro view of whole organizations, while OB concentrates on individuals and groups.[^s1] It was **influenced by** the **Hawthorne studies** and **Elton Mayo**, and it **contrasts with** **scientific management**'s engineering view of work.[^s2][^s3] Its parts include **employee motivation**, **group decision making**, **leadership**, **organizational culture**, **psychological safety** and **high-performance teams**, each of which studies one of the building blocks in the OpenStax model.[^s1] Its research-practice gap is the problem that **evidence-based management** was proposed to fix, and Rousseau's argument criticizes how business schools teach popular solutions rather than research-based principles.[^s5]
