# Devil's Advocate Method

The devil's advocate method (devil's advocacy) is a group decision technique in which a member, or a subgroup, is formally assigned to attack a proposal: to expose faulty logic, question how alternatives were judged and find weaknesses in the recommended course before the group commits to it.[^s2] Unlike spontaneous disagreement, the critique is a role, which gives the critic permission to dissent and signals to everyone else that the plan is expected to survive scrutiny rather than be waved through.[^s2] Management textbooks list it among the standard remedies for groupthink, alongside building diverse groups and inviting everyone to speak before a decision.[^s2]

## Origin

The name comes from the Latin *advocatus diaboli*, the popular label for the Catholic Church's Promoter of the Faith (*promotor fidei*), whose job in beatification and canonization cases was to argue against the candidate by presenting unfavourable facts and exposing exaggerations in the evidence.[^s1] Pope Sixtus V formally established the office in 1587, and its influence faded after John Paul II reformed canonization procedure in 1983.[^s1] In everyday usage the phrase came to mean anyone who argues the weaker side for the sake of testing an argument, and organizational researchers borrowed it for an assigned-critic procedure in strategic planning.[^s1][^s3]

## How it works

A proposal and its underlying assumptions are put on the table, and the assigned critic probes them: pointing out flawed reasoning, challenging how the options were evaluated and naming weaknesses in the favoured solution, so that the group weighs pros and cons more carefully before deciding and implementing.[^s2] Strategy researchers framed it as one of two structured-conflict approaches, the other being **dialectical inquiry**, in which assumptions are debated through competing views, and compared both against **consensus**, in which members simply discuss until they agree.[^s3][^s4] In everyday management the role can be assigned to an individual in a meeting or seated in an audience to voice the other side's arguments.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

A laboratory study published in the *Academy of Management Journal* in 1986 found that groups using either devil's advocacy or dialectical inquiry produced higher-quality strategic recommendations and examined assumptions more carefully than consensus groups; dialectical inquiry was better at surfacing hidden assumptions.[^s3] The cost was social: consensus groups reported more satisfaction, more willingness to keep working together and more acceptance of the final decision.[^s3] A follow-up in the *Strategic Management Journal* (1989) attributed the advantage to better use of members' abilities: devil's-advocacy groups beat the average member of the group, and their recommendations even beat the best individual, while consensus groups did no better than their members.[^s4]

Later work tilted the comparison toward devil's advocacy. In a 1994 experiment, Charles Schwenk and Joseph Valacich found that, pooled across individuals and groups, devil's advocacy yielded better solutions than both dialectical inquiry and an expert-based approach with no built-in conflict, and the edge over dialectical inquiry was somewhat larger for individuals than for groups.[^s5]

The main critique is that role-played dissent is a weak substitute for the real thing. Berkeley psychologist Charlan Nemeth, whose research shows that dissent widens the information people consider, reports with colleagues (Nemeth, Rogers and Brown, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2001) that no role-play technique generates as much divergent thinking as authentic dissent.[^s6] The practical implication is that assigning a critic complements, rather than replaces, a climate in which members voice their own concerns, which the OpenStax text also lists as a separate remedy for groupthink.[^s2][^s6]

## In practice

The OpenStax organizational behavior text describes a meeting of McDonald's franchise owners at which an attorney was deliberately seated in the audience as devil's advocate, repeatedly disputing whether a National Labor Relations Board joint-employer ruling helped franchisees, so that attendees heard the franchisors' arguments and could answer them.[^s2] The example shows the method's typical use: forcing the strongest opposing case into the room before a group commits to a position.[^s2]

## Connections

Devil's advocacy is a form of **group decision-making** (its broader node) designed to **mitigate groupthink**, the concurrence-seeking failure that the technique is most often prescribed against.[^s2] It is an **alternative to dialectical inquiry**, the other conflict-based method compared with it in the strategy literature, and it applies chiefly to **strategic decision-making**.[^s3][^s4][^s5] It **contrasts with authentic dissent**, which research suggests is more effective at broadening thinking.[^s6] It is related to **psychological safety**, which makes real dissent possible; to the **nominal group technique**, another structured method for keeping a few voices from dominating; and to the **board of directors**, where assigned critics are one way to challenge management proposals.[^s2]
