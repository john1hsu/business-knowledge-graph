# J. Richard Hackman

**J. Richard Hackman** (1940–2013) was an American social and organizational psychologist who spent his career studying how the design of work and of work groups shapes motivation and performance. A Harvard Business School tribute describes him as the leading scholar in two separate fields, work design and team effectiveness, and notes that he wrote or co-wrote ten books on group effectiveness.[^s1] His influence reached job design in services, manufacturing, education, health care and the performing arts.[^s1]

## Career

Hackman studied mathematics at MacMurray College in Illinois and earned a PhD in psychology at the University of Illinois.[^s2] He joined Yale in 1966, in the psychology and administrative sciences program that later became the School of Organization and Management, and moved to Harvard in 1986, holding appointments in psychology, at Harvard Business School and later at the Kennedy School of Government; he finished his career as Edgar Pierce Professor of Social and Organizational Psychology.[^s2] His honours included the Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award of the American Psychological Association's industrial-organizational division, the Academy of Management's Distinguished Scholar and Lifetime Achievement awards, and, posthumously, the American Psychological Foundation Gold Medal.[^s2] His book *Leading Teams* (2004) won the Academy of Management's Terry Award for the year's best management book.[^s2] He died in January 2013.[^s1]

## Work design and the job characteristics model

With Greg Oldham, Hackman built the theory of job characteristics: an account of how jobs can be redesigned and enriched so that people both perform well and find their work meaningful.[^s1] The model names five measurable features of a job (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback) and combines them into a motivating potential score, in which autonomy and feedback act as multipliers.[^s3] The theory did not claim enrichment works for everyone. In a 1976 study with Jone Pearce of 201 clerical employees across 25 jobs at a large bank, they found that the link between a job's motivating potential and outcomes such as performance and internal motivation was strongest for people with high growth needs who were also satisfied with pay, security, co-workers and supervision; for dissatisfied people with low growth needs, richer jobs were sometimes associated with worse results.[^s3] The authors acknowledged that the design was correlational and could not prove causation.[^s3]

## Team effectiveness

Hackman later turned to groups, reasoning that many important features of behaviour appear only when people work together, and focused on teams that must solve hard problems in real time under demanding conditions.[^s2] Rather than looking for the right leadership style, he asked under what conditions effective team processes emerge, and his model was applied to crews and teams in aviation, surgery, orchestras and intelligence analysis.[^s1] A study of symphony orchestras in the United States, Britain and Germany found that many musicians were surprisingly unhappy at work and pointed to governance arrangements that left players out of decisions; the team presented these findings to the main U.S. orchestra and musicians' associations in 1995.[^s5] Later books included *Senior Leadership Teams* (with Ruth Wageman and others) and *Collaborative Intelligence*, on using teams for difficult analytic work.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The job characteristics model is among the most tested ideas in organizational behavior. A 2007 meta-analysis of 259 studies and nearly 220,000 participants found that motivational job features explained about a third of the variance in job satisfaction and a quarter of the variance in subjective performance.[^s4] The same analysis also exposed the model's main limitation: social features of work (such as support and interdependence) and the physical work context added substantial explanatory power, including 40% more explained variance in organizational commitment, which the original five-dimension model largely ignored.[^s4]

## Connections

Hackman is a figure within **organizational behavior**. He **influenced** the **job characteristics model** and the practice of **job enrichment**, which tie specific, measurable job features to internal work motivation and satisfaction; that makes his work a neighbour of other accounts of **employee motivation** such as **motivation-hygiene theory**.[^s3][^s1] His second career founded much of the modern study of **team effectiveness** and of **high-performance teams**.[^s1] His Harvard team research, centred on the conditions that let groups learn and perform, is the tradition in which later work on team **psychological safety**, notably by **Amy Edmondson**, took shape; her own node records that influence.[^s1][^s2]
