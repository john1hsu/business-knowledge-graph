# Psychological Safety

**Psychological safety** is a team-level belief that members can take interpersonal risks (asking for help, admitting an error, raising a concern or challenging a plan) without being embarrassed, rejected or punished. Amy Edmondson's 1999 paper defined team psychological safety as a shared, usually tacit belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking, and positioned it as the climate that lets teams learn.[^s1] Because it is a property of a group rather than a personality trait, two teams in the same company can differ sharply in how safe it feels to speak up.[^s1]

## Origin

Edmondson traced the idea to Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis, who argued in 1965 that people need psychological safety to feel secure enough to change.[^s1] Her contribution was to define it at the level of the work team and test it empirically. In a multimethod field study at an office-furniture manufacturer of about 5,000 employees, she surveyed 51 teams (427 members) spanning functional, self-managed, product-development and project teams, and paired the survey with qualitative case comparisons.[^s1]

## How it works

The 1999 model links context to performance through learning. Team-leader coaching and organizational support raise psychological safety; psychological safety raises **learning behavior**, meaning seeking feedback, asking questions, experimenting, discussing errors and sharing information; and learning behavior in turn predicted observer-rated team performance, mediating the link from safety to results.[^s1] Team efficacy, a belief in the team's capability, did not explain learning once safety was accounted for.[^s1]

Edmondson distinguished the construct from its neighbours. It builds on interpersonal trust but adds mutual respect and a climate in which people can be themselves; it is not the same as cohesiveness, which can actually suppress disagreement in the way groupthink describes; and it does not imply permissiveness or constant positivity.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

A 2017 meta-analysis by Frazier and colleagues pooled 136 independent samples covering more than 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups.[^s2] At the individual level, psychological safety correlated with learning behaviors (corrected ρ = .62), engagement (.45) and task performance (.43); among antecedents, interdependence, role clarity and peer support were stronger than positive leader relations, while openness to experience showed no meaningful relation.[^s2] Psychological safety also explained variance in task performance beyond engagement and beyond leadership measures such as transformational leadership.[^s2]

The same study flags real limits. Most correlations came from a single source at a single time, and such designs produced effects roughly 27% larger; published studies showed larger effects than unpublished ones; and links to subjectively rated performance (.40) dwarfed those to objective performance (.07).[^s2] The authors did not claim strong causal inference, and group-level evidence was thin.[^s2]

A second line of critique concerns misuse. Writing in Harvard Business Review in 2025, Edmondson and Michaela Kerrissey argued that the concept's popularity has bred six misconceptions: that it means being nice, getting your way, job security, lower performance standards, a policy, or something only top leaders can create.[^s4] A Harvard Chan summary of that work stresses that candid debate and accountability remain necessary, and that safety grows from everyday team interactions rather than from policies such as Rhode Island's 2024 workplace psychological safety law.[^s5]

## In practice

The concept reached a mass managerial audience through Google's Project Aristotle, which studied 180 teams (115 engineering teams and 65 sales pods) and, in a guide first published in 2015, ranked psychological safety first among five dynamics of effective teams, ahead of dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact.[^s3] Google adopted Edmondson's definition directly.[^s3] Managers typically act on the idea by framing work as a learning problem, inviting input explicitly and responding to bad news or errors productively, while still holding people to high standards.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

Psychological safety sits under **team learning**, since Edmondson introduced it to explain why some teams learn and others do not, and under **organizational culture**, as one dimension of a group's shared climate.[^s1] It was **created by Amy Edmondson**, **introduced in** her 1999 *Administrative Science Quarterly* paper, and **influenced by** Edgar Schein's work on change.[^s1] It **contrasts with groupthink**: cohesive groups may feel comfortable yet discourage dissent, whereas psychological safety is defined by the freedom to dissent.[^s1] It is **exemplified by Google's Project Aristotle**.[^s3] Related nodes include **employee voice** and **organizational learning**, the behaviors and outcomes it enables, and **high-performance teams**, where it is treated as a foundational condition.[^s2][^s3]
