# Employee Motivation: Content and Process Theories of Work Motivation

**Employee motivation** is the set of forces that shape which work behaviors people choose and how much effort they put into them. Organizational behavior organizes the field into two families. **Content theories** ask *what* people need and treat a need as active when a person feels deficient in it; once satisfied, it stops driving behavior and another need takes over.[^s1] **Process theories** ask *how* people decide on the direction and intensity of effort, a question content theories largely leave open.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin: content theories

Abraham Maslow's 1943 *Psychological Review* paper proposed five needs ordered by prepotency: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem and self-actualization, with a higher need usually emerging only once lower ones are fairly well met.[^s3] Maslow himself said the order was less rigid than it sounded and that most people are only partially satisfied at every level, offering illustrative figures that fall from about 85% for physiological needs to 10% for self-actualization.[^s3] Clayton Alderfer's ERG theory compressed the list to existence, relatedness and growth and allowed movement down as well as up the hierarchy, while David McClelland emphasized learned needs for achievement, affiliation and power.[^s1]

Frederick Herzberg's 1968 *Harvard Business Review* article made the most managerial argument. He separated **motivators** intrinsic to the job (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth) from **hygiene factors** surrounding it (policy, supervision, working conditions, salary, status, security), so that removing dissatisfaction does not by itself create satisfaction.[^s4] He dismissed raises and perks as "KITA" that produce movement but must be repeated, and advocated *vertical* job loading (more authority, accountability and whole units of work) over simply adding more tasks.[^s4]

Self-determination theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, holds that people have innate needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness, and that contexts satisfying them foster intrinsic motivation and well-being.[^s5] It treats extrinsic motivation as a continuum running from external and introjected regulation to identified and integrated regulation, and reports that tangible rewards contingent on performance, threats, deadlines and evaluative pressure tend to undermine intrinsic motivation.[^s5]

## How it works: process theories

**Expectancy theory** models motivational force as the belief that effort produces performance, multiplied by the summed product of beliefs that performance yields outcomes (instrumentality) and the value of those outcomes (valence); a weak link anywhere depresses effort.[^s2] **Equity theory** says people compare their own outcome-to-input ratio with that of referent coworkers and respond to underreward by cutting inputs, distorting perceptions, switching referents or leaving.[^s2] Reinforcement (operant conditioning) and goal-setting complete the usual set, and OpenStax notes how the theories interlock: need theories explain valences, reinforcement history shapes instrumentality beliefs, and inequity alters the value of outcomes.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Maslow's hierarchy remains popular with managers, but organizational behavior researchers find little support for a fixed five-step sequence; ERG's three categories have stronger evidence.[^s1] A general weakness of need theories is that a need can be posited for almost any behavior; by the 1920s, scholars had proposed some 6,000 human needs.[^s1] Herzberg's sharp split between motivators and hygiene factors rests on his own interview data, in which 81% of satisfaction factors were motivators and 69% of dissatisfaction factors were hygiene factors.[^s4]

The sharpest live debate concerns pay. Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford's 2014 meta-analysis of 183 samples and more than 212,000 participants found intrinsic motivation a medium-to-strong predictor of performance (corrected correlation .26), stronger for quality than for quantity of work.[^s6] Incentives explained more of the variance in quantity, and incentives tied directly to performance weakened the intrinsic motivation link, consistent with partial crowding out; the authors concluded that the two jointly predict performance rather than competing.[^s6]

## In practice

Herzberg described a six-month experiment at a large corporation in which shareholder correspondents received vertically loaded jobs while a control group did not. After an initial dip, the enriched unit outperformed the controls, reported better job attitudes, had lower absenteeism and was promoted at a much higher rate.[^s4] Expectancy theory gives managers a diagnostic checklist: tie rewards to measured performance, build self-efficacy through training, and make sure the rewards offered are ones employees value.[^s2]

## Connections

Employee motivation is a core topic of **organizational behavior**, and its main theories (**Maslow's hierarchy of needs**, Herzberg's **motivation-hygiene theory**, **self-determination theory**, **expectancy theory** and **equity theory**) are treated as its parts.[^s1][^s2] It sits next to **goal-setting theory**, which OpenStax covers alongside expectancy theory as a process theory.[^s2] The question of how far performance-contingent pay raises or crowds out effort links it to **performance management** and **executive compensation**.[^s2][^s6] Herzberg's case for vertical loading is the root of **job enrichment**, later developed by **J. Richard Hackman**, and his rejection of pay-and-perks motivation is a counterpoint to purely economic views of work that the **Hawthorne studies** had also questioned.[^s4]
