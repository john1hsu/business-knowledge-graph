# Organizational Learning

**Organizational learning** is the study of how organizations, rather than only the individuals in them, change what they know and do as a result of experience. In the influential formulation of Barbara Levitt and James G. March (1988), organizations learn by encoding inferences drawn from history into routines (rules, procedures, technologies, beliefs and cultures) that guide later behavior and that persist even as the people who created them leave.[^s1] On this view learning is routine-based, history-dependent and oriented toward targets, so what counts as success or failure shapes what gets learned.[^s1]

## Origins and core processes

The field draws on several traditions. Chris Argyris distinguished *single-loop* learning, which detects an error and corrects the action, from *double-loop* learning, which also questions the assumptions and goals behind the action; his illustration is a thermostat that not only switches on heat below 68 degrees but asks why it is set at 68.[^s3] The Carnegie tradition of Levitt and March emphasized learning from direct experience through trial and error and search, as well as learning from others through diffusion of practices across organizations.[^s1]

George Huber's 1991 review in *Organization Science* organized the literature into four constructs: knowledge acquisition, information distribution, information interpretation and organizational memory.[^s2] He split acquisition into five routes: knowledge present at founding, learning from experience, learning vicariously by observing other organizations, grafting on members or units that already hold the knowledge, and searching or noticing information about the environment.[^s2] Linda Argote and colleagues later framed the field around search, knowledge creation, knowledge retention and knowledge transfer.[^s4]

## Evidence

The most robust empirical finding is the learning curve: organizations generally improve performance as cumulative experience grows, at a decreasing rate, a pattern found across many industries.[^s4] Levitt and March traced the evidence to aircraft production studies in the 1930s and noted that cumulative output usually explains cost declines better than scale or elapsed time.[^s1] Yet organizations learn at very different rates, which is why research has turned to the conditions that speed or slow each process.[^s4]

Memory is one such condition. Experience is stored in documents, procedures, structures and culture, but recording is costly, and lessons can erode through turnover when socialization is weak.[^s1] Garvin, Edmondson and Gino's diagnostic adds that learning needs a supportive environment, including psychological safety and time for reflection, alongside concrete learning processes and leaders who reinforce them.[^s6]

## Critiques and pathologies

Learning from experience is not the same as becoming more intelligent. Levitt and March identified *competency traps*, in which good results with an inferior routine build so much experience with it that a superior alternative is never adopted, and *superstitious learning*, in which organizations draw confident but wrong conclusions about what caused their outcomes.[^s1] Argyris argued that highly educated professionals are often poor double-loop learners: because they rarely fail, they react to failure defensively and blame others rather than examine their own reasoning.[^s3] Huber, for his part, judged much of the literature fragmented, noting that experiential learning had been studied extensively but with little cumulative integration across research groups.[^s2]

## In practice

David Garvin's 1993 *Harvard Business Review* article translated the research into management terms, defining a learning organization as one skilled at creating, acquiring and transferring knowledge and at changing its behavior to reflect new insight.[^s5] He listed five activities: systematic problem solving, experimentation, learning from past experience, learning from others' best practices and transferring knowledge quickly, with examples drawn from companies including Xerox and General Electric.[^s5] Argyris described a consulting firm whose project review stalled because the team attributed problems to the client rather than to its own behavior, illustrating how defensive routines block double-loop learning even when a manager invites candor.[^s3]

## Connections

Organizational learning is a core topic in **organization theory**. Its prescriptive offshoot is the **learning organization**, popularized by Garvin's activities and diagnostic,[^s5][^s6] and its best-known distinction is Argyris's **double-loop learning**.[^s3] **Organizational memory** and **knowledge transfer** are components of the process in both Huber's and Argote's frameworks.[^s2][^s4] The learning curve connects it to the **experience curve** used in strategy,[^s1] while the competency trap anticipates March's exploration-exploitation tension and the remedy of **organizational ambidexterity**.[^s1] The importance of a supportive climate links the concept to **psychological safety**.[^s6]
