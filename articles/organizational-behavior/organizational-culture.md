# Organizational Culture

Organizational culture is the set of taken-for-granted assumptions, shared values and visible practices that tell members of a firm how things are really done there. Edgar Schein of MIT Sloan gave the concept its most cited formal definition in a 1990 *American Psychologist* article: culture is a pattern of basic assumptions that a group invents or discovers while solving its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, which works well enough to be taught to newcomers as the right way to perceive, think and feel.[^s1] Because those assumptions are learned through shared success, culture is a property of a group with a history, not something a manager can simply announce.[^s1]

## Origin and levels

Schein separated culture into three levels that differ in how visible they are. **Artifacts** — office layout, dress, rituals, stories and jargon — are easy to see but hard to interpret correctly. **Espoused values** are what members say they believe and can be studied through interviews and surveys. **Basic underlying assumptions** are unconscious, taken for granted and the true core of culture; uncovering them requires intensive joint inquiry.[^s1] A gap between espoused values and assumptions explains why a firm's stated values can differ sharply from its behavior; the open textbook literature uses Enron's published values, contradicted by its conduct, as the warning case.[^s6]

## How culture forms and persists

Founders supply the first model: their beliefs are tested against reality and the parts that work are kept and passed on.[^s1] Schein listed the mechanisms by which leaders embed assumptions, including what they pay attention to, measure and control, how they react to crises, what they role-model, how they allocate rewards and status, and whom they recruit and promote.[^s1] Textbook treatments add the attraction-selection-attrition cycle (people drift toward and stay in organizations that fit them), structured onboarding, reward systems and symbols such as mission statements, rituals and stories; Southwest Airlines' practice of hiring for attitude is a standard illustration.[^s6] Industry also matters: banking and insurance tend toward stable, rule-oriented cultures, while high-technology firms lean toward innovation.[^s6]

Culture **strength** is how widely and intensely members share values. A strong culture is an asset when its values fit the environment, but a liability when values are harmful or when the environment shifts, because strong cultures resist change and can clash in mergers, as in DaimlerChrysler.[^s5] Large organizations also develop **subcultures** by department or location, and occasionally **countercultures** that openly oppose the dominant values.[^s5]

## Measuring culture

Two survey frameworks dominate management practice. Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn's **Competing Values Framework** classifies cultures as clan, adhocracy, market and hierarchy, and their Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument profiles a firm's current and preferred culture across six dimensions.[^s2] O'Reilly, Chatman and Caldwell's **Organizational Culture Profile** describes culture along seven dimensions: innovative, aggressive, outcome-oriented, stable, people-oriented, team-oriented and detail-oriented.[^s5] Schein himself warned that questionnaires prejudge which dimensions matter and may miss an organization's critical themes, so he favored clinical and ethnographic inquiry first.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Cameron and Quinn argued that firms with sustained success, such as Southwest Airlines and Walmart, owed it more to distinctive values than to market position, and reported that in their studies of more than 100 organizations, TQM and downsizing programs succeeded only when embedded in broader culture change.[^s2] The most rigorous test is Hartnell, Ou and Kinicki's 2011 meta-analysis of 84 studies (94 samples): clan, adhocracy and market cultures were all positively associated with employee attitudes, operational performance and financial performance, but the pattern was not always as theory predicted and the framework's assumed internal structure was largely unsupported.[^s3] Clan cultures were most closely linked to job satisfaction and commitment, market cultures to profit, and clan and market traits correlated positively even though the framework treats them as opposites.[^s4] Critics therefore caution that culture is easier to invoke than to measure, that culture-performance links are correlational, and that change is slow: Kinicki suggested deliberate culture change takes three years or more.[^s4]

## In practice

The GM Fremont plant is the classic example. Reopened as NUMMI, a joint venture with Toyota, it achieved far fewer grievances, higher output with fewer workers and better quality, which Cameron and Quinn attribute mainly to changed culture rather than new technology.[^s2] In the reverse direction, Home Depot under Bob Nardelli is cited as a strategy that failed when a centralized, metrics-driven approach collided with a store-level culture of empowerment.[^s4]

## Connections

Organizational culture sits within **organizational behavior** and **organization theory** as a group-level explanation of how firms behave. It was shaped above all by **Edgar Schein**, and is operationalized through the **Competing Values Framework** and the **Organizational Culture Profile**.[^s1][^s2][^s5] Because culture is itself a product of group learning, it is linked to **organizational learning**, and to **psychological safety**, a team-level climate that leaders' reactions to mistakes and dissent help create.[^s1][^s6] Because deep assumptions resist change and Schein's prescriptions for guided change include unfreezing the system and replacing key people, culture is central to **change management**.[^s1] **Southwest Airlines** is a recurring example of culture as a source of advantage, and the **IBM turnaround** node is linked as a case of deliberate culture change at a large incumbent.[^s2][^s6]
