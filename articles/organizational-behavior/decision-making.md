# Decision Making in Organizations: Rational, Bounded and Intuitive Choice

Decision making is the process of recognizing that a choice is needed, developing and evaluating alternatives, committing to one and then checking whether it worked. Organizational behavior textbooks separate **programmed decisions**, which recur often enough that rules or habits can handle them, from **nonprogrammed decisions**, which are novel, unstructured and made with ambiguous or incomplete information. Restocking or staffing up before a routine sale is programmed; deciding whether to adopt an unfamiliar technology is nonprogrammed and calls for judgment.[^s1]

## The rational model

For nonprogrammed decisions, the standard prescription is a six-step sequence: recognize that a decision must be made, generate several alternatives, analyze them, select one, implement it and evaluate its effectiveness.[^s1] The model descends from the "economic man" of classical economics, a decision maker assumed to know the environment fully, hold a stable ordering of preferences and compute the best option.[^s4] In practice, people skip steps, rush through generating alternatives, or fail to acknowledge that a problem exists at all.[^s1]

## Bounded rationality and satisficing

Herbert A. Simon's 1955 paper "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" attacked the economic-man assumption. Simon found no evidence that people perform the computations global rationality requires in complex choices, and proposed models that build the chooser's limited information and computing capacity into the definition of rationality.[^s4] In his account a decision maker sets an **aspiration level**, searches only until an alternative clears it, and raises or lowers the aspiration depending on how easy acceptable options are to find, much as a chess player seeks a good move rather than the provably best one.[^s4] This "good enough" rule became known as satisficing. Management texts now treat it as a legitimate time-saver for routine, low-stakes choices, while warning that complex nonprogrammed decisions still deserve fuller search.[^s3]

Bounded rationality explains why managers rarely see every alternative or its consequences: information is incomplete and human attention can process only so much.[^s2] Time pressure compounds the problem by pushing people from deliberate analysis toward mental shortcuts.[^s2]

## Heuristics and biases

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1974 *Science* article showed that people judging under uncertainty rely on a few heuristics: **representativeness** (judging category membership by resemblance), **availability** (estimating frequency by how easily examples come to mind) and **anchoring and adjustment** (starting from an initial value and adjusting insufficiently). These shortcuts are economical and usually serviceable, but they produce systematic, predictable errors.[^s5] Organizational research adds biases with direct managerial costs: confirmation bias (favoring information that fits existing beliefs), "similar to me" bias in hiring and appraisal, a preference for familiar suppliers and technologies, and **escalation of commitment**, in which decision makers keep investing in a failing course of action as results worsen.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques: when to trust intuition

Intuition is not simply the enemy of good decisions. Kahneman and the naturalistic-decision-making researcher Gary Klein, representing traditions that had long disagreed, concluded in 2009 that intuitive judgment can be trusted only when the environment is regular enough to be predictable and the decider has had adequate opportunity to learn its patterns through practice and feedback. They also warned that the feeling of confidence says little about whether an intuition is accurate.[^s6] That finding supports experienced managers' quick calls in stable, feedback-rich settings, such as routine operations, and counsels caution about gut instinct in one-off strategic bets.

Critics of the rational model note that its steps describe an ideal rather than actual behavior, while critics of the biases literature argue that heuristics are often adaptive; the Kahneman-Klein synthesis reconciles the two by making the reliability of intuition depend on the environment.[^s6] Textbook remedies include generating more alternatives with people who bring different perspectives, evidence-based decision making that weighs data, stakeholder input and costs and benefits rather than intuition alone, explicit ethical review, and evaluating outcomes afterward so the organization learns.[^s3]

## Real-world example

OpenStax uses Ford's 1970s Pinto as a cautionary case: the car could catch fire when struck from behind, yet the company initially chose not to recall it because it framed the problem only in financial terms and ignored the ethical dimension, and people died as a result.[^s3] The case shows how narrowing the decision frame, a symptom of bounded attention, can turn an analytically tidy choice into a moral and reputational failure.[^s3]

## Connections

Decision making sits within **organizational behavior** and is a core function of **management**; the textbook treatments of programmed and nonprogrammed choice come from both fields.[^s1][^s2] Its building blocks are **bounded rationality** and satisficing, credited to **Herbert A. Simon**,[^s4] the **heuristics-and-biases** program,[^s5] and **escalation of commitment**.[^s2] **Group decision making** extends the topic from individuals to teams, **structured problem solving** supplies consulting-style procedures for nonprogrammed choices, and **evidence-based management** formalizes the call to rely on data rather than intuition alone.[^s3] **Prospect theory** is a related descriptive model of choice under risk that grew out of the same research tradition on systematic departures from rational choice.[^s5]
