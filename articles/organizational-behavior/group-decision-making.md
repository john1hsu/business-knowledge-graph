# Group Decision Making

**Group decision making** is the process by which several people, rather than one manager, weigh alternatives and settle on a shared choice. Management textbooks present it as a trade-off: involving more people can improve a decision and its acceptance, but it can also add conflict, delay and pressure to conform.[^s1] Committees, boards, hiring panels, steering groups and executive teams all decide this way, so the quality of group process directly shapes organizational outcomes.

## Why organizations decide in groups

The case for groups rests on information and commitment. Members bring different knowledge, preferences and biases, so a diverse group tends to generate more alternatives and can offset any one person's blind spots; people who take part in a decision also understand it better and are more committed to carrying it out.[^s1] One open organizational behavior text adds that diverse top-management teams that debate issues reach more comprehensive decisions, but also reports the sobering finding that groups rarely outperform their own best member.[^s2]

The same text offers a contingency rule. An individual should decide in emergencies, when one person holds all the relevant information, and when implementation is unlikely to be contested; a group is better when no single person has the needed information, when those affected must help implement the choice, and when time pressure is moderate.[^s2]

## Where group decisions go wrong

The best-known failure is **groupthink**, where cohesion and the wish to keep the peace lead members to stay silent; it can arise from camaraderie or from complacency after past success, and a powerful member may also suppress dissent so that only their ideas survive.[^s1] Coordination problems and slower decisions are further process losses.[^s2]

A second failure is poor **information pooling**. In a classic 1985 experiment, Garold Stasser and William Titus gave four-person groups profiles of three hypothetical candidates, one of them objectively best. When every member saw all the facts, 83% of groups chose that candidate; when the favorable facts were scattered across members so that each person's partial view pointed elsewhere, only 18% did.[^s3] Discussion did not surface the unshared facts. Instead, initial majorities grew, and the authors concluded that unstructured discussion tends to perpetuate rather than correct members' distorted pictures.[^s3] This "hidden profile" paradigm became a standard test of whether groups realize their informational advantage.[^s4]

A third failure is **group polarization**. Stoner's 1961 work found individuals became riskier after discussion (the "risky shift"), and later research showed that discussion more generally pushes like-minded members further in the direction they already leaned; more than 200 studies document the effect.[^s5] Two explanations dominate: members hear new arguments favoring the shared position, and they shift to compare favorably with the group average they discover. Brauer, Judd and Gliner added that simply repeating one's own view during discussion also makes it more extreme.[^s5]

## Evidence on process gains

Groups can still beat their members. Mojzisch and Schulz-Hardt measured the "group potential" implied by members' pre-discussion choices on hidden-profile tasks and found that real groups exceeded it, yet fell short of what was achievable once dysfunctional processes were removed.[^s4] Their data suggest the main obstacle was simply too little pooling of information rather than only a bias toward shared facts, so remedies must address both group dynamics and individual-level constraints on members.[^s4]

## In practice

Common remedies are procedural. Textbooks recommend diverse membership, a designated **devil's advocate**, deliberately drawing out every member's view before deciding, and searching for common ground to defuse personal conflict.[^s1] Structured techniques include the **nominal group technique** (silent idea writing, round-robin sharing, discussion, then a vote), the written, iterative **Delphi** method, decision rules such as majority vote or consensus, group decision support systems that improve information sharing, and the premortem, in which a team imagines its plan has failed and lists why.[^s2] Majority rule is fast but can leave losing voters less committed, while consensus improves satisfaction at the cost of time.[^s2] The 1986 *Challenger* launch decision is a frequently analyzed example of how these safeguards can fail under schedule pressure.[^s2]

## Connections

Group decision making is a branch of **decision-making** studied within organizational behavior.[^s1] **Groupthink** and **group polarization** are its two best-known distortions: the first suppresses dissent, the second amplifies whatever view a group already holds.[^s1][^s5] The **nominal group technique** and **devil's advocacy** are tools designed to counter those distortions and to pool information more fully.[^s2][^s4] The **Challenger** launch decision exemplifies the risks, and because boards are small decision-making groups, the concept applies directly to the **board of directors**.[^s2]
