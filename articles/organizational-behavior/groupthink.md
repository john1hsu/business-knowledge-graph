# Groupthink

**Groupthink** is a pattern of collective reasoning in which members of a small, cohesive group converge on what they take to be the group's consensus even when they privately doubt it, degrading the quality of the group's problem solving.[^s1] Irving Janis framed it as a deterioration of mental efficiency, reality testing and moral judgment that results from in-group pressure, so that the striving for unanimity crowds out a realistic weighing of alternatives.[^s2] The term matters to managers because executive teams, boards and project steering committees are exactly the kind of tight, high-stakes groups the theory describes.

## Origin

Social psychologist Irving Janis set out the theory in his 1972 book *Victims of Groupthink*, asking why groups of highly capable people made poor foreign-policy decisions; his cases included Pearl Harbor, escalation in Vietnam and the Bay of Pigs invasion.[^s1] The model was later elaborated by Janis and Mann in 1977, and the concept was revived to help explain how intelligence on Iraqi weapons of mass destruction was interpreted before the Iraq War.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

The model runs from antecedents through symptoms to defective decisions. Five conditions make groupthink more likely: high cohesiveness, insulation from outside information, weak procedures for searching and appraising information, directive leadership, and high stress combined with a tendency to grab the first acceptable option.[^s2] Britannica groups these structural causes under cohesion, decision rules, leadership style, member homogeneity and the situation the group faces.[^s1]

Janis listed eight symptoms: an illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, belief in the group's inherent morality, stereotyped views of rivals, direct pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, an illusion of unanimity, and self-appointed "mindguards" who screen out unwelcome information.[^s2] The result is a predictable set of process defects: few alternatives considered, narrow goals, ignored risks, biased and incomplete information search, rejected options never revisited, and little contingency planning.[^s1] Prescribed remedies include multiple channels for dissent, preserving openness and diversity of membership, and leadership styles that deliberately discourage premature consensus.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Support is strongest in historical case studies and much weaker in experiments. A 2011 literature review found that case studies almost uniformly reported groupthink, while the roughly fifteen experimental studies tested only a few antecedents, chiefly cohesion, insulation and leader impartiality, and left the full model at best inconclusive.[^s4] The same review summarizes Esser's 1998 assessment: structural faults in the organization tended to predict groupthink, but cohesion defined as mutual attraction did not.[^s4] An early laboratory test by Callaway and Esser found mixed support: groups of intermediate cohesiveness made the best decisions, and highly cohesive groups lacking sound procedures did worst, showing little disagreement and high confidence.[^s3]

Critics go further. Fuller and Aldag called groupthink a compelling myth and urged a broader model of group problem solving, and other scholars argued that the theory's ambiguity makes it hard to test at all.[^s4] Not every poor decision stems from groupthink, and not every instance of it ends in failure; it can even speed decisions and raise members' confidence.[^s1] Turner and Pratkanis responded by recasting the phenomenon as **social identity maintenance**: groups under threat seek concurrence to protect a shared positive image, which implies that some classic prevention tactics can backfire and that interventions should lower identity threat while encouraging constructive intellectual conflict.[^s4][^s5]

## In practice

In business the concept is most often applied to boards and top-management teams. A 2010 case study of the collapse of Swissair drew on group-conformity theory and attributed the failure chiefly to an aggressive alliance and acquisition strategy combined with weak leadership and accountability from the CEO and board chairman.[^s6] Practical safeguards that follow from the theory are procedural: open channels for dissent, deliberately diverse membership, leaders who avoid steering the group, and explicit decision procedures, since in the laboratory it was cohesive groups without adequate procedures that decided worst.[^s1][^s3] The social identity maintenance view adds a caution: when a group feels its image is under threat, reducing that threat and legitimizing intellectual conflict may matter more than any single technique.[^s5]

## Connections

Groupthink is a theory within **group decision-making**.[^s1] It was **created by Irving Janis** and **introduced in** *Victims of Groupthink* (1972), and it is **exemplified by the Bay of Pigs invasion**, Janis's signature case.[^s1] It **contrasts with psychological safety**: both concern a group's climate, but groupthink describes cohesion that suppresses dissent, whereas psychological safety is defined by the freedom to voice it.[^s2] The social identity maintenance reformulation ties it to **social identity theory**, and the recommended remedy of structured dissent links it to **devil's advocacy**.[^s5] Because boards are small, cohesive and often insulated, the theory **applies to the board of directors** and is a recurring concern in **corporate governance**.[^s6]
