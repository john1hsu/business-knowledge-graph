# Organization Theory

**Organization theory** is a large, multidisciplinary body of scholarship that treats the organization itself as the thing to be explained. Its central questions are why organizations have the structures they have, why some perform better than others, and why some survive while others fail. It aims for tools that apply to all formal organizations, public agencies as well as firms, although most of its evidence comes from U.S. businesses, which leaves open how well its findings travel to the public sector.[^s1] Where organizational behavior looks at individuals and teams inside the organization, organization theory works at the level of the whole organization and its relations with other organizations.

## Origins and the three system views

The field grew from two roots. Sociologists built on Max Weber's analysis of bureaucracy to found the study of formal organizations, while economists and management scholars of the early twentieth century studied the business firm, largely to help managers run it more efficiently.[^s1] Over time three broad images of the organization emerged. The **rational system** view sees a formal structure of people pursuing specific goals; the **natural system** view argues that informal relationships, competing interests and the building of consensus matter more than the formal chart; and the **open system** view holds that an organization cannot be understood apart from the environment it is entangled with.[^s1] The open-system view underlies the macro perspectives below, each of which locates the main forces shaping an organization in its environment.

## Main perspectives

**Contingency theory** rejects a single best structure and argues that structure should fit the environment. In the standard textbook version, mechanistic designs, with centralized control, rigid departments and narrow spans of control, suit stable, simple environments, while flatter and more fluid organic designs suit complex and changing ones.[^s2]

**Resource dependence theory**, set out by Jeffrey Pfeffer and Gerald Salancik in *The External Control of Organizations* (1978), starts from the fact that organizations need resources held by others. That dependence makes outside control of their behavior close to inevitable, so managers respond politically, trying to reshape the environment, or by building ties with other organizations that absorb or manage uncertainty.[^s3] Alliances, interlocking board seats and mergers are typical moves in this view.

**Institutional theory** asks why organizations in a field come to look alike even when that does not make them more efficient. Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell's 1983 paper "The Iron Cage Revisited" named three pressures toward this isomorphism: coercive pressure from powerful outside parties such as funders and regulators, mimetic copying of rivals under uncertainty, and normative pressure from professionalization and staff who move between organizations. Stanford GSB reports it became the most-cited paper in its journal's history.[^s4]

**Organizational ecology** shifts attention from adaptation to selection. Michael Hannan and John Freeman argued in 1984 that inertia is a product of selection: environments favor organizations that act reliably and can account for their activities, and those capacities depend on reproducing structure faithfully, so change is hard. They found older organizations more inert than younger ones, with the effect of size less clear-cut.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The perspectives disagree on a basic point: contingency and resource dependence picture managers adapting to their environment, while ecology argues that much change across a population comes from organizations being replaced rather than adapting.[^s2][^s5] A 2010 article in *Organizational Research Methods* questioned whether the field is making cumulative progress at all. It noted that two decades of digital data on millions of organizations had not clearly made theories more precise, general or accurate, and pointed to weak causal designs, the fact that organizations are human-made tools rather than natural objects obeying laws, and regularities that change from one era to the next.[^s6] Powell and DiMaggio themselves have since warned that organizational scholarship has grown insular and narrow.[^s4]

## In practice

Managers rarely cite organization theory by name, but they use its logic constantly. Choosing between a centralized functional structure and a decentralized, flatter design for an uncertain market is a contingency decision.[^s2] Securing a key supplier through a long-term contract or a board seat is resource dependence at work.[^s3] Adopting a practice because peers and regulators expect it, rather than because it pays off, is the isomorphism institutional theory describes, as when New York public broadcaster WNET conformed to avoid upsetting critics in Washington after government funding cuts.[^s4]

## Connections

Organization theory is a branch of the broader study of **management** with deep roots in **sociology**, and it was shaped above all by **Max Weber**'s account of bureaucracy.[^s1] Its parts include **contingency theory**, **resource dependence theory**, **institutional theory** and **organizational ecology**, described above.[^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5] It overlaps with **management theory**, which draws on the same classical and systems ideas to prescribe how managers should design organizations,[^s1] and it is the parent field of **organizational learning** and **organizational culture**. Economic approaches such as **transaction cost economics** offer a rival explanation of organizational boundaries based on efficiency rather than the dependence and legitimacy pressures stressed by resource dependence and institutional theory.[^s3][^s4]
