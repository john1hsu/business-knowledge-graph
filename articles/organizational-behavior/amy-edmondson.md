# Amy Edmondson

**Amy C. Edmondson** is the Novartis Professor of Leadership and Management at Harvard Business School. She holds three Harvard degrees (AB 1981, AM 1995, PhD 1996), and her research centres on teaming, psychological safety, organizational learning and what she calls failing well.[^s1] She has appeared in the Thinkers50 ranking of management thinkers since 2011 and was ranked first in both 2021 and 2023; she also received its Talent Award in 2017 and its Breakthrough Idea Award in 2019.[^s1] Before academia she worked for the designer and inventor Buckminster Fuller, an experience she has linked to her interest in using design to tackle large problems; she names J. Richard Hackman's work on team effectiveness and Chris Argyris's on organizational learning as her main scholarly influences.[^s4]

## Origin of her ideas: the medication-error puzzle

Her best-known construct grew out of an anomaly. In a study of eight units in a university hospital, published in the *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* in 1996, Edmondson combined staff surveys with covert observation of nursing teams. Units with better relationships, stronger manager coaching and higher perceived performance recorded *more* medication errors, not fewer.[^s3] The resolution was that a detected error rate reflects both how many errors occur and how willing people are to report them; better-led units were surfacing mistakes that other units hid. She concluded that managers should deliberately build environments that make error reporting normal.[^s3]

## Psychological safety (1999)

Edmondson formalised the idea in a 1999 *Administrative Science Quarterly* paper, defining team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking.[^s2] In a multimethod field study of 51 work teams at a manufacturing company, psychological safety was associated with team learning behavior; team efficacy had no association with learning once safety was controlled for; and learning behavior mediated the link between safety and team performance.[^s2] The paper also argued that structures such as context support and leader coaching, not only shared beliefs, shape team outcomes.[^s2] The construct itself is treated in its own node; this article concerns the researcher and her broader programme.

## Teaming

In *Teaming: How Organizations Learn, Innovate, and Compete in the Knowledge Economy* (2012), Edmondson shifted attention from stable, well-designed teams to "teaming", which she treats as a verb: a skill set for reaching out across disciplines, getting up to speed quickly and making progress without a fixed plan.[^s4] Her argument is that uncertain, fast-changing work makes the classic plan-then-execute team less practical, so organizations must learn and execute at the same time. One example she uses is a hospital CT scan that took four days instead of a few hours because every specialist did their own job well inside a siloed hierarchy.[^s4] She stresses that psychological safety is not a lowering of standards and should be paired with accountability and high expectations.[^s4]

## Failing well

*The Fearless Organization* (Wiley, 2019) brought her safety research to practitioners and has been translated into 15 languages; *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023) offers a framework for failing well and was selected for the Financial Times and Schroders Business Book of the Year award.[^s1] The later book separates three kinds of failure: basic failures are preventable slips; complex failures have multiple causes and can be reduced by catching small problems early; and intelligent failures are well-intentioned experiments in new territory that avoid unnecessary harm and generate useful knowledge.[^s5] The practical implication is that "celebrating failure" is too blunt: managers should prevent the first kind, monitor for the second and encourage the third.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The empirical core of Edmondson's work rests on field studies rather than experiments, and the 1996 hospital finding shows why reported error data can mislead managers who read low numbers as good performance.[^s3] Later research has tested the limits of her prescriptions. Pearsall and Ellis, studying 126 teams, found that psychological safety amplified unethical team behavior when members held a utilitarian, outcome-focused ethical orientation, implying that safety magnifies whatever norms a team already has.[^s6] Edmondson's own insistence on pairing safety with high standards anticipates part of this critique.[^s4]

## Connections

Edmondson is a researcher within **organizational behavior**. She **influenced** the concepts of **psychological safety**, **teaming** and **intelligent failure**, and her work is a branch of **organizational learning**, the tradition she traces to **Chris Argyris**, with **J. Richard Hackman** shaping her view of team effectiveness.[^s4][^s2] Her finding that safety drives team learning and performance relates her to the study of **high-performance teams**, and Google's **Project Aristotle**, a practitioner study of team effectiveness covered in its own node, is linked here for that reason.[^s2] Her call for people to raise concerns rather than stay silent, illustrated by hesitancy to question risky models before the financial crisis, positions her work as a counterweight to **groupthink**.[^s4]
