# Overconfidence Bias: Overestimation, Overplacement and Overprecision

**Overconfidence bias** is the tendency to believe one's own performance, standing or knowledge is better or more certain than the evidence supports. It is among the most cited judgment errors in organizational decision making, but the label has been used loosely. In an influential 2008 review, Don Moore and Paul Healy argued that "overconfidence" actually covers three distinct phenomena that research had routinely conflated.[^s1]

## Three kinds of overconfidence

Moore and Healy define the three types as follows:[^s1]

- **Overestimation**: believing you did better than you actually did, such as expecting five right answers on a quiz when you got three.[^s1]
- **Overplacement**: believing you are better than others when you are not, the "better-than-average" effect.[^s1]
- **Overprecision**: excessive certainty that your beliefs are correct, seen when 90% confidence intervals contain the truth far less than 90% of the time.[^s1]

Their literature count showed how uneven the research had been. Of the empirical studies returned by a PsycINFO search for "overconfidence," roughly 64% examined overestimation, about 31% overprecision and only about 5% overplacement; most overestimation studies used an item-confidence design that mixes overestimation with overprecision.[^s1]

## How it works

The distinction matters because the types do not always move together. Moore and Healy documented a **hard–easy effect**: on hard tasks people overestimate their own scores but *under*place themselves relative to others, while on easy tasks they underestimate their scores yet *over*place themselves.[^s1] They explained the pattern with imperfect information. People know their own performance only roughly and others' performance even less, so their estimates regress toward prior expectations, and estimates of others regress further.[^s1] Overprecision behaved differently: it was the most persistent of the three, and in their experiment 90% confidence intervals contained the true score only 73.1% of the time; the authors conceded that their model does not explain why it persists.[^s1]

## Evidence in business and finance

Two finance studies made overconfidence measurable with field data. Barber and Odean used psychological evidence that men tend to be more overconfident than women in financial matters, then compared the brokerage accounts of about 37,664 US households from 1991 to 1997. Men traded about 45% more than women, and trading cut men's annual net returns by 2.65 percentage points versus 1.72 for women; the gap was largest among single investors.[^s3]

Malmendier and Tate carried the idea into the boardroom. Using Forbes 500 CEOs, they classified a chief executive as overconfident if he repeatedly kept company-specific risk he could have shed, for example by holding deep in-the-money options until their final year (their "Longholder" measure).[^s2] Overconfident CEOs overestimate the returns to their projects and see outside financing as unduly expensive, so they invest heavily when internal cash is plentiful and cut back when they must raise funds. Their investment was significantly more sensitive to cash flow, especially in equity-dependent firms.[^s2] This made CEO overconfidence a standard explanation in capital-budgeting and financing research.

## Critiques and limits

The concept's main weakness is the one Moore and Healy identified: findings labelled "overconfidence" often measure different things, and some apparent bias may be a rational response to noisy information.[^s1] A second critique concerns attribution. When Dan Lovallo and Daniel Kahneman blamed forecasting failures on executives' optimism and recommended an outside view, Bent Flyvbjerg replied in *Harvard Business Review* (December 2003) that in politically pressured projects deliberate misrepresentation explains the data better. In his transport sample, errors did not shrink over 70 years even though forecasters should have learned.[^s4] For managers the practical lesson is that debiasing tools such as reference-class forecasting help honest optimists, but they do little where incentives reward inflated estimates.[^s4]

## Connections

Overconfidence is one of the **heuristics and biases** that shape organizational **decision making**.[^s1] Through Barber and Odean's evidence it **causes** **excessive trading**, a core finding of **behavioral finance**, and through Malmendier and Tate it **influenced** **behavioral corporate finance** and research on **capital budgeting**.[^s3][^s2] Overprecision is **measured by** **calibration**, comparing stated confidence with hit rates.[^s1] Flyvbjerg's argument sets it in **contrast with** **strategic misrepresentation** and ties it to **reference-class forecasting**; **scenario planning** and devil's-advocate methods that counter **groupthink** are often used to temper it.[^s4]
