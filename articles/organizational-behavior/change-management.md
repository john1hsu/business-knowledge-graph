# Change Management: From Lewin's Unfreezing to Kotter's Eight Steps

**Change management** is the body of theory and practice concerned with moving an organization from its current way of operating to a new one, whether the change is in technology, structure, culture or procedures, and with getting the people involved to adopt and sustain the new behavior.[^s5] Organizational behavior textbooks frame it as a response to external pressures such as shifting workforce demographics, new technology, globalization and changing market conditions, while stressing that such pressures do not trigger change by themselves: what matters is how decision makers interpret them.[^s5] Polaroid, the leading instant-film producer in 1994, went bankrupt within a decade after failing to adapt to digital photography, a standard illustration of the cost of not changing.[^s5]

## Origin: Lewin and the planned approach

The field traces its roots to the social psychologist Kurt Lewin, whose planned approach to change combined field theory, group dynamics, action research and the so-called three-step model, and which shaped change thinking for more than four decades.[^s2] In its textbook form the model has three stages: *unfreezing*, which prepares employees and makes them receptive to change; *changing*, in which the new technology, structure or procedures are implemented; and *refreezing*, which makes the new behaviors and norms permanent.[^s5] Bernard Burnes notes that Lewin's own motivation was resolving social conflict, and that his approach was participative rather than a management technique imposed from above.[^s2]

## Kotter's eight steps

The most widely taught modern model comes from John Kotter's 1995 *Harvard Business Review* article, which drew on his observation of more than 100 companies attempting transformation, including Ford, General Motors, British Airways and Bristol-Myers Squibb.[^s1] Kotter reported that a few efforts were very successful and a few were outright failures, with most landing in between and tilted toward the lower end.[^s1] He framed his advice as eight errors to avoid: insufficient urgency, a weak guiding coalition, no vision, undercommunicating the vision (by "a factor of ten"), failing to remove obstacles, no planned short-term wins, declaring victory too soon, and not anchoring changes in the corporate culture.[^s1] He judged urgency sufficient only when roughly 75% of a company's management is convinced that business as usual is unacceptable, and he found that well over half of the companies he watched failed at that first phase.[^s1]

## Resistance to change

Much of the practice focuses on resistance. Textbook accounts list disrupted habits, personality, uncertainty about outcomes such as job losses, fear of failure, personal impact, change fatigue from repeated initiatives, and loss of power or status as its main sources.[^s5] Resistance is costly: resisting employees report more negative emotions, take more sick days and are more likely to quit.[^s5] Recommended countermeasures follow Kotter's logic: create and communicate a compelling vision, win over opinion leaders first, involve employees early in diagnosing problems, break the change into phases that yield small wins, reward adopters, and embed new behaviors in the culture after the change.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Two critiques undercut the field's received wisdom. First, the claim that about 70% of change initiatives fail is often repeated but poorly supported: Mark Hughes reviewed five published sources for the figure and concluded that none provides valid, reliable empirical evidence for it.[^s4] Second, the field's foundation is contested. Burnes summarized critics' charges that Lewin's approach assumed stable organizations, suited only small-scale change, ignored power and politics, and was top-down; he rejected those charges and argued the approach remains relevant.[^s2] Stephen Cummings, Todd Bridgman and Kenneth Brown went further, arguing that Lewin never actually set out a three-step model, that the model was assembled by later writers after his death, and that its foundational status has rarely been questioned.[^s3]

## In practice

The IBM turnaround under Lou Gerstner is a frequently cited example: leaders deliberately kept the company's crisis visible to sustain a sense of urgency during the transformation, applying the first of Kotter's steps.[^s5] Gap Inc. used a leader-first sequence, training 2,000 senior managers before rolling a shared identity out across its brands, and Kimberly-Clark's CEO repeatedly raised a new performance appraisal system in meetings to signal commitment.[^s5]

## Connections

Change management is studied within **organizational behavior** and **organization theory**.[^s5] Its canonical components are **Lewin's three-step model**, whose provenance Cummings and colleagues dispute, and **Kotter's eight-step model**, which ends by anchoring change in **organizational culture**.[^s1][^s3] It overlaps with **strategy execution**, since many transformations are strategy changes, and with **organizational learning**, which explains how new routines are retained.[^s5] **Resistance to change** is the central obstacle the practice addresses, and the **IBM turnaround** is a standard case.[^s5]
