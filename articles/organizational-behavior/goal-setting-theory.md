# Goal-Setting Theory: Specific, Difficult Goals and Performance

**Goal-setting theory** explains differences in task performance by the goals people pursue. Its central claim is that, given commitment, specific and challenging goals produce higher performance than vague exhortations such as "do your best", and that harder goals generally yield more output than easy ones.[^s3] OpenStax's organizational behavior text calls it one of the strongest theories in the field and summarizes it as difficult, specific, accepted goals leading to better results.[^s4]

## Origin

The theory grew out of Edwin Locke's laboratory research on task motivation in the late 1960s, which found that specific high goals outperformed instructions to do one's best.[^s1] Gary Latham carried the idea into the field, starting with pulpwood logging crews, and the two later consolidated the program into a formal theory published in 1990.[^s1][^s3] By the mid-2000s the authors claimed support across many kinds of people, tasks, countries, time spans and research designs, and for goals that were self-set, jointly set or assigned.[^s2] Seijts and colleagues put the evidence base at more than 500 empirical studies.[^s3]

## How it works

Goals work chiefly by directing attention and raising the intensity of effort and persistence; on complex tasks they also prompt a search for effective strategies.[^s3][^s4] OpenStax describes a process that begins with personal values: people compare their current state with what they value and set goals to close the gap, whether those goals are self-set, participative or assigned.[^s4] Performance peaks when goals are difficult, specific, accepted and committed to, and when the person has the ability and lacks external constraints.[^s4] Recognised moderators include ability, feedback, task complexity and situational constraints, with commitment a precondition.[^s3]

Latham's field work tested whether participation matters. Participatively set goals tended to be higher, but when goal difficulty was held constant, assigned goals performed as well as participative ones, especially when a rationale was given.[^s1] In an R&D setting, praise, public recognition and money produced no gains over a comparison group unless goals were also set.[^s1]

## Learning goals and boundary conditions

A key refinement concerns complex tasks. When people lack the knowledge to perform, a specific high *performance* goal can do worse than "do your best", while a specific *learning* goal, such as discovering a set number of strategies, outperforms both.[^s3] In a 2004 *Academy of Management Journal* study of 170 business students on a business simulation with a mid-game shock, the learning-goal group outperformed both the performance-goal and do-your-best groups.[^s3] Later directions examined goal framing, group and macro-level goals, links between goals and affect, and subconscious goals.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Locke himself warned that goals in one area can crowd out others, encourage easy-goal picking, unhealthy competition or sabotage, and that individual goals can backfire in highly interdependent work.[^s4] A broader challenge came from Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman's 2009 "Goals Gone Wild" paper, which argued that benefits have been overstated and side effects such as narrowed focus, distorted risk-taking, unethical behaviour, weaker culture and reduced intrinsic motivation overlooked.[^s5] They likened goals to a potent medication requiring careful dosing, and recommended learning goals where desired behaviours are unclear.[^s5][^s6] Locke and Latham rebutted the paper, and the critics replied that a single large harm can outweigh many small gains, calling for research on goals, unethical conduct and excessive risk.[^s6]

## In practice

Latham's logging studies are the classic field example: crews given specific production goals were more productive, and in a randomized experiment productivity and attendance rose within the first week, with unionisation not blocking the effect.[^s1] The cautionary counterpart is the Ford Pinto, where a target of a car under 2,000 pounds and under $2,000 contributed to overlooked safety testing.[^s5]

## Connections

Goal-setting theory is a **motivation** theory created by **Edwin Locke** and **Gary Latham**.[^s1] It provided the logic behind **management by objectives**, which OpenStax describes as based on goal theory.[^s4] Its emphasis on challenging targets underpins **stretch goals** and **OKRs**, and its effects run partly through **self-efficacy**, which mediated learning-goal effects in Seijts and colleagues' study.[^s3] It sits alongside **expectancy theory** among process theories of motivation and informs **performance management** and **high-performance teams**.[^s4] The **Ford Pinto** illustrates the side effects its critics emphasise.[^s5]
