# High-Performance Teams

A high-performance team is a small group whose members share a purpose, specific performance goals and a working approach, hold one another mutually accountable, and produce joint "collective work-products" rather than a bundle of individual outputs. The idea was popularised by McKinsey partners Jon Katzenbach and Douglas Smith in their 1993 Harvard Business Review article "The Discipline of Teams", which drew on interviews with hundreds of people on more than 50 teams in 30 companies.[^s1] Management textbooks still use their definition to separate real teams from ordinary work groups.[^s3]

## Origin and definition

Katzenbach and Smith defined a team as a small number of people with complementary skills committed to a common purpose, goals and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable.[^s1] They contrasted this with a *working group*, where a strong leader assigns work, members are accountable only for their own results, and performance is judged indirectly by influence on the wider business. In a team, leadership roles are shared and performance is measured directly by what members create together.[^s1] OpenStax's management text summarises the same contrast and adds the authors' practical advice: establish urgency and direction, select members for skill and skill potential, set clear rules of behaviour, and seize a few early performance wins.[^s3]

## How it works

The "team basics" are deliberately concrete. Effective teams in the study ranged from 2 to 25 members, most had fewer than 10, and groups of 50 or more tended to split into subteams.[^s1] Purpose must be turned into specific, measurable goals, such as halving supplier reject rates, so the team can track progress and win small victories.[^s1]

J. Richard Hackman's research reframed high performance as a design problem rather than a matter of personality or leadership style. With Ruth Wageman and Colin Fisher he set out conditions that make effectiveness more likely: a *real team* with clear boundaries and stable membership; a *compelling direction* that is challenging, clear and consequential; an *enabling structure* (a whole task, a small and well-composed membership, explicit norms); a *supportive organisational context* of rewards, training and information; and *expert coaching*, which helps only once the other conditions are in place.[^s2] They judged effectiveness on three outcomes: the team's service to its clients, how much the team learns, and members' learning and well-being.[^s2] Martine Haas and Mark Mortensen later argued that dispersed, digital and fluid teams also need a fourth enabling condition, a *shared mindset* built through common identity and common understanding.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Kozlowski and Ilgen's 2006 review of more than five decades of research concluded that there is an emerging science of team effectiveness, centred on the cognitive, motivational and behavioural processes through which members pool their resources, and on levers that shape those processes.[^s6] Hackman's own work cautions that the promise of teams is often unmet. In a 2009 interview he argued that teams frequently underperform despite their extra resources, that members often disagree about the team's purpose and even its membership, and that coordination problems grow sharply with size, so teams should usually stay below 10 people.[^s4] His studies also found that well-timed interventions matter: real-time process coaching did not rescue poorly designed self-managing teams, while well-designed teams benefited from modest help, and relationship-focused interventions hurt about as often as they helped.[^s2] A further critique is that small-team practices do not scale: collaboration tends to fall as teams become larger, more virtual and more expert.[^s3]

## In practice

Katzenbach and Smith's examples include a seven-member Burlington Northern team that built a new "piggybacking" (rail–truck intermodal) business, and a 14-person Knight-Ridder newspaper team.[^s1] In each case the group set its own concrete goals and held itself, not just a boss, accountable for results.[^s1] Managers applying Hackman's model typically start by fixing team design (who is on the team, what the task is, what the norms are) and launch, then reserve coaching for natural breakpoints such as the project midpoint.[^s2]

## Connections

High-performance teams are the aspirational end of the broader study of **team effectiveness**, and Hackman's design conditions are the main framework that refines Katzenbach and Smith's original concept.[^s2] Later work on **psychological safety** and **team learning** by **Amy Edmondson** explains *why* interpersonal climate matters, and Google's **Project Aristotle** study is a corporate example that ranked that climate first among team dynamics. The emphasis on specific, measurable goals links the concept to **goal-setting theory** and to goal systems such as **OKRs**.[^s1] High-performance teams contrast with **groupthink**, in which cohesion suppresses the constructive conflict that clear goals are meant to support.[^s1]
