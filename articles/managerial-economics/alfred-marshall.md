# Alfred Marshall

Alfred Marshall (born 26 July 1842 in London, died 13 July 1924 in Cambridge) was the English economist who did more than anyone to turn nineteenth-century political economy into the toolkit of modern microeconomics. Britannica ranks him among the chief founders of the English neoclassical school, in a line running from Adam Smith through David Ricardo and John Stuart Mill.[^s1] Many of the diagrams an MBA student meets in a first economics course, from shifting supply and demand curves to elasticity and consumer surplus, trace directly to his *Principles of Economics* (1890).[^s1][^s2]

## Life and career

Marshall was raised to enter the clergy but instead studied mathematics at St John's College, Cambridge, and turned to economics.[^s1][^s2] He was the first principal of University College, Bristol (1877–81), lectured at Balliol College, Oxford (1883–85), and held the chair of political economy at Cambridge from 1885 until 1908; he also served on the Royal Commission on Labour (1891–94).[^s1] At Cambridge he founded what became the "Cambridge school" of neoclassical economics and, on retiring, recommended A. C. Pigou as his successor.[^s3] His first book, *The Economics of Industry* (1879), was co-written with his wife, Mary Paley Marshall, who historians believe wrote much of it.[^s3]

## Principles of Economics and its ideas

The *Principles* went through eight editions between 1890 and 1920 and was meant as the first of several volumes; the companions eventually appeared as *Industry and Trade* (1919), a study of business organization, and *Money, Credit and Commerce* (1923).[^s3][^s1] Its central contributions include:

- **The scissors.** Price and quantity are set jointly by supply and demand, like two blades of a pair of scissors; asking which blade moved remains the first step in explaining a price change.[^s1][^s2]
- **Reconciling cost and utility.** By bringing time into the analysis, he reconciled the classical cost-of-production view of value with the marginal-utility view of Jevons and the Austrians.[^s1]
- **Time periods.** He separated a market period with fixed stock, a short period in which output can rise with more labour but not more capital, and a long period in which capital itself adjusts.[^s2]
- **Elasticity and surplus.** He is credited with price elasticity of demand and with consumer and producer surplus, which he used to judge the welfare effects of policies such as taxes.[^s2]
- **Firm-level concepts.** He introduced quasi-rent and, in the 1891 second edition, the "representative firm".[^s1][^s3]

Marshall also split the economies of large-scale production into *internal* economies, which depend on a firm's own resources, organization and management, and *external* economies, which depend on the general development of the whole industry and are often gained through geographic localization.[^s4] That distinction underpins later work on industrial districts and clusters.

## Style, evidence and critiques

Though a capable mathematician, Marshall kept equations out of the main text so business readers could follow him, and he hedged his claims with many qualifications and footnotes.[^s2] He saw biology rather than mechanics as the proper model for economics, even while his own tools treated technology, institutions and preferences as given.[^s2] His dominance of British economics lasted from about 1890 to his death, and his influence ran through Pigou, Keynes and later Samuelson.[^s2] The best-known critique came from Piero Sraffa in 1926, who argued that Marshall's partial-equilibrium treatment of increasing and decreasing returns could not be made both logically consistent and empirically relevant; historians of thought still debate how far that critique reaches.[^s5]

## In practice

Managers use Marshall's tools whenever they estimate how a price change will move volume (elasticity), decide whether a cost spike is temporary or permanent (short versus long period), or choose to locate near suppliers and skilled workers to capture external economies.[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Marshall sits under microeconomics and the neoclassical school, both of which his *Principles* helped define.[^s1] He originated price elasticity of demand and consumer surplus, and his internal/external split refines economies of scale.[^s2][^s4] His work on business organization feeds into industrial organization and the study of industry clusters, and his chair passed to Arthur Cecil Pigou, who extended his welfare analysis.[^s1][^s3]
