# Airline Industry

The airline industry comprises the carriers that sell scheduled air transport of passengers and freight. For business and economics students it is one of the most studied industries in industrial organization: it moved from tight economic regulation to open competition, reorganized itself around hub-and-spoke networks, absorbed waves of low-cost entry and consolidation, and still earns margins that are small relative to its revenue and capital. Economists Severin Borenstein and Nancy Rose frame the central questions as whether competition is sustainable given volatile profits, whether dominant carriers hold market power at their airports, and whether airport and air-traffic infrastructure is underinvested.[^s2]

## From regulation to competition

In the United States the Civil Aeronautics Board set routes and fares until deregulation took effect at the end of the 1970s; afterwards any domestically owned carrier judged fit, willing and able could fly any domestic route, and the Department of Transportation's role narrowed mainly to safety.[^s1] Borenstein and Rose date the regulated era from 1938 to 1978.[^s2] Some controls never went away: local governments allocate gates and runways, usually without auctions, slot rules limit flights at key Chicago, New York and Washington airports, and foreign carriers remain barred from U.S. domestic routes.[^s1] A Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco analysis (2002) found that U.S. domestic passenger traffic grew 225% over the 25 years after deregulation, against 80% in Canada, which deregulated later, while average real U.S. fares fell.[^s1]

## Economic structure

**Networks.** After 1978 most large carriers adopted hub-and-spoke networks, in which flights from many smaller "spoke" airports feed a central hub where passengers connect.[^s4] Hubbing lets airlines fill more seats and offer more frequent departures, at the cost of indirect trips and tightly banked waves of arrivals and departures.[^s4] It improved service to small and mid-sized cities but concentrated traffic at hubs.[^s1]

**Costs and volatility.** Labor and fuel are largely fixed in the short run, so moderate swings in demand produce large swings in profit.[^s1] In 2025 labor was 37.8% of the operating expenses of U.S. scheduled passenger airlines, and the industry's net margin was 2.4%, down from 2.7% in 2024.[^s5] Globally, IATA estimated a 3.9% net margin for both 2025 and 2026 (forecast December 2025), with return on invested capital of 6.8% still below its estimated 8.2% cost of capital.[^s6]

**Legacy versus low-cost carriers.** GAO distinguished legacy airlines, which predate deregulation, from low-cost carriers that entered interstate service afterwards with a cheaper point-to-point model.[^s3] Low-cost carriers' share of U.S. passenger traffic rose from 25% in 1998 to 33% in 2006, and in 2006 markets they served averaged round-trip fares of $153 versus $194 in legacy-only markets (2006 dollars).[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Supporters of deregulation point to traffic growth, lower average fares and a competitive structure: the San Francisco Fed estimated the national market behaved like roughly ten equal-sized firms through the 1990s and found little evidence of natural monopoly.[^s1] GAO likewise found the average number of competitors in the top 5,000 U.S. city-pair markets rose from 2.9 in 1998 to 3.3 in 2006, and average fares in those markets fell about 20% in real terms.[^s3] Critics stress the other side: chronic financial instability, with legacy carriers losing nearly $33 billion from 2001 through 2005 and Delta, Northwest, United and US Airways all entering bankruptcy, and persistent airport dominance, such as American carrying 85% of Dallas–Fort Worth passengers in 2006.[^s3] Business travelers buying unrestricted tickets have often paid more since deregulation, although defenders argue those fares buy flexibility.[^s1]

## In practice: merger waves

Recurrent losses push carriers toward consolidation. GAO reported in 2008 that airlines merge mainly to cut duplicate hubs, overhead and capacity and to widen their networks, sometimes with higher fares in overlapping markets, but that integrating workforces, seniority lists, fleets and IT can absorb the gains; US Airways' IT integration after its America West merger took nearly 2.5 years.[^s3] The Department of Justice reviews such deals under its merger guidelines and has opposed several, including United–US Airways in 2000, which was abandoned after the government challenge.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The industry is a core case study within **industrial organization**, used to test how market structure shapes conduct and performance.[^s2] Its regulatory turning point is the **Airline Deregulation Act**, and its network form is the **hub-and-spoke network**.[^s4] Gates, slots and hub dominance make it a textbook setting for **barriers to entry**, while hub density and fleet utilization illustrate **economies of scale**.[^s1][^s3] The rise of the **low-cost carrier**, exemplified by **Southwest Airlines** with its point-to-point routes, single aircraft type and quick turnarounds, shows how a different cost structure can undercut incumbents.[^s1] Merger review ties the industry to **antitrust law**.[^s3]
