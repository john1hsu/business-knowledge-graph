# Computer Industry

The **computer industry** comprises the firms that design and make computers, from mainframes and servers to personal computers, together with the peripherals, components and system software that make them usable. In U.S. statistics it sits within NAICS 334, computer and electronic product manufacturing, whose industry groups include computer and peripheral equipment manufacturing (NAICS 3341) alongside communications equipment and semiconductors.[^s6] For students of industrial organization it is the classic case of **platform competition**: Timothy Bresnahan and Shane Greenstein argue that the unit of competition over three decades was the computer platform rather than the individual firm.[^s1]

## From integrated systems to a compatible platform

Before the mid-1960s each computer line was largely its own world. IBM's System/360, announced on April 7, 1964, unified the company's product lines under a single architecture so that software written for one machine would run on any other in the family.[^s2] IBM reports spending USD 5 billion over four years on the project, which offered six processor models spanning a fiftyfold performance range and 54 peripheral devices.[^s2] More than 1,000 systems were ordered in the first month, and by 1989 products based on the architecture and its extensions accounted for over half of IBM's revenue.[^s2] Because outside firms could build equipment that attached to System/360, the company itself credits it with founding a platform business model and an industry of compatible peripherals.[^s2]

## The personal computer and vertical disintegration

The second structural break came with the IBM Personal Computer, unveiled on August 12, 1981, at a base price of USD 1,565.[^s3] To hit a one-year deadline, the team built it from existing parts rather than in-house designs: an Intel 8088 processor and an operating system supplied by Microsoft that became MS-DOS.[^s3] IBM also published technical references, including circuit designs and source code, so others could write software and build add-ons; competitors then reverse-engineered the boot code and sold "IBM compatible" machines.[^s3] Control of the platform's most valuable layers thus passed to the chip and operating-system suppliers, and IBM eventually sold its PC division to Lenovo in 2005 for USD 1.75 billion.[^s3]

Bresnahan and Greenstein describe two changes that reshaped the industry. Young platforms that began by serving new segments later attacked established platforms across segment boundaries, a pattern they call indirect entry; and vertically disintegrated platforms produced **divided technical leadership**, with different firms leading different layers of the same system. Their conclusion is that the industry ended up with considerably more technological competition than in the mainframe era.[^s1]

## Modularity as the mechanism

Carliss Baldwin and Kim Clark explain the shift through **modularity**: a product built from independently designed modules that conform to shared design rules.[^s4] They argue that modularity lets firms design and build products more flexibly, speeds innovation, and changes relationships between companies, while leaving managers facing more uncertainty.[^s4] In the computer industry, the published interfaces of System/360 and later the IBM PC made it possible for specialist firms to compete module by module, which is the economic logic behind the move from integrated vendors to layered markets.[^s4][^s2][^s3]

## Industry structure today

Personal computers remain a concentrated, scale-driven segment. IDC's preliminary figures put worldwide PC shipments at 284.7 million units in 2025, up 8.1% from 2024.[^s5] Lenovo led with a 24.9% share, followed by HP Inc. at 20.2%, Dell Technologies at 14.4%, Apple at 9.0% and Asus at 7.2% (2025).[^s5] IDC expected 2026 to be volatile because of tight memory supply, with higher average selling prices and larger brands likely to gain share from smaller and regional vendors.[^s5] U.S. employment in the wider NAICS 334 subsector was about 1.008 million in October 2026 (preliminary, seasonally adjusted).[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The platform view explains why dominant firms such as IBM persisted for decades and why their position eroded once platforms disintegrated, rather than attributing the change to firm-level mismanagement alone.[^s1] Notably, Bresnahan and Greenstein find that the basic nature of platform competition changed little over thirty years; what changed was who could enter and how leadership was divided, so the industry's apparent revolution was more a shift in structure than in the competitive mechanism.[^s1] Modular industries also carry costs of their own: Baldwin and Clark caution that the faster innovation modularity enables brings greater uncertainty for the managers who must navigate it.[^s4]

## Connections

The computer industry is studied in **industrial organization**, which supplies its tools for analysing entry, market structure and dominance.[^s1] **IBM System/360** exemplifies the creation of a compatible platform, and its peripheral ecosystem is an early instance of the **platform business model**.[^s2] Software compatibility created the **network effects** and **switching costs** that let platforms persist, while the PC era shows the reversal of **vertical integration** through **modularity**.[^s1][^s4] **Intel**, the supplier of the original PC processor, exemplifies how a component maker can lead a layer of a disintegrated platform.[^s3][^s1]
