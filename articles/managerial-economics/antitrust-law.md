# Antitrust Law

**Antitrust law** is the body of law that protects the competitive process by forbidding agreements that unreasonably restrain trade, monopolization, and mergers or practices likely to weaken competition. In the United States it rests on three federal statutes that remain in force today: the Sherman Act of 1890, and the Clayton Act and Federal Trade Commission Act, both of 1914. Most states add their own antitrust statutes, often modeled on the federal ones and enforced by state attorneys general and private plaintiffs.[^s1] OpenStax describes the laws as deliberately general, leaving courts to apply them as markets and business practices change.[^s2]

## The core statutes

The **Sherman Act** has two main parts. Section 1 bars contracts, combinations and conspiracies in restraint of trade; Section 2 bars monopolization and attempted monopolization.[^s3] It is also a criminal statute: the Department of Justice prosecutes clear, intentional violations such as price fixing and bid rigging, with fines of up to $100 million for a corporation and $1 million for an individual, prison terms of up to 10 years, and fines that can rise to twice the conspirators' gain or the victims' loss.[^s1]

The **Clayton Act** targets specific practices. Its Section 7 forbids mergers whose effect may be to substantially lessen competition; it also covers interlocking directorates, and the 1936 Robinson-Patman amendment added rules against certain kinds of price discrimination between merchants.[^s1] A 1976 amendment, the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act, requires parties to large mergers to notify the government before closing, and the Act lets injured private parties sue for triple damages.[^s1] OpenStax adds that labor unions, agricultural cooperatives and certain export arrangements are exempt so long as they do not restrain US trade.[^s2]

The **FTC Act** created the Federal Trade Commission and bans unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive practices. Every Sherman Act violation also violates the FTC Act, which can reach harmful conduct outside the Sherman Act's terms; only the FTC enforces it.[^s1]

## How courts decide cases

Since *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States* (1911), courts have read Section 1 as prohibiting only *unreasonable* restraints.[^s3] Two modes of analysis follow. Naked agreements among competitors to fix prices, divide markets or rig bids are illegal **per se**, with no justification allowed.[^s1] Other conduct is judged under the **rule of reason**, which balances procompetitive against anticompetitive effects. Monopolization has no per se rule and no fixed market-share threshold; liability turns on how the firm gained or kept its position, because the law protects competition rather than any individual competitor.[^s3] The DOJ Antitrust Division and the FTC share civil enforcement, and agency merger guidelines persuade courts without binding them.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Under the influence of the Chicago School, modern US antitrust has largely judged conduct by its short-term effects on price and output. Lina Khan's 2017 *Yale Law Journal* note argued that this price-focused test cannot see the structural power of platforms such as Amazon, which can rationally price below cost to win lock-in and data, and use control of infrastructure that rivals depend on to favor itself. She urged a return to analysis of market structure and competitive process, or common-carrier duties for dominant platforms.[^s4] Economist Carl Shapiro accepted that profits and some concentration measures have risen, citing corporate profits climbing from roughly 7–8% to 11–12% of GDP over about three decades. Yet he cautioned that national concentration data rarely measure relevant markets and that rising share can reflect efficiency. He backed tougher horizontal merger review and scrutiny of acquisitions of nascent rivals, but warned that using antitrust to target political power or inequality would politicize enforcement.[^s5]

## In practice

The search case against Google shows modern Section 2 enforcement. In August 2024 the US District Court for the District of Columbia held that Google had unlawfully maintained monopolies in general search and search text advertising, chiefly through exclusive default and preinstallation agreements; the DOJ says Google handled about 90% of US search queries for years. On September 2, 2025 the court barred exclusive distribution contracts for Google Search, Chrome, Assistant and Gemini, and required Google to share certain search index and interaction data and offer syndication to qualified competitors (as described by the DOJ, updated April 2026).[^s6] For managers, the practical lessons are to avoid any pricing or market-allocation contact with competitors, to expect premerger review of large deals, and to treat exclusive dealing by a dominant firm as legally risky.[^s1]

## Connections

Antitrust law is the legal arm of **competition policy**, and **industrial organization** supplies its economics, from market definition to the analysis of market power.[^s5] It is made up of the **Sherman Act**, the **Clayton Act** and the agencies' **merger guidelines**.[^s1][^s3] **Barriers to entry** matter because durable monopoly requires that rivals cannot easily enter, and **network effects** and **two-sided markets** sit at the center of current debates over platforms such as Amazon and Google.[^s4][^s6] *United States v. Google* exemplifies how the law is applied to digital markets.[^s6]
