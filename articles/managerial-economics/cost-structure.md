# Cost Structure: Fixed Versus Variable Costs and Operating Leverage

A firm's **cost structure** is the proportion of its costs that are fixed versus variable. Fixed costs, such as rent, insurance, salaried management and straight-line depreciation, stay the same in total over the short term regardless of activity, so their cost per unit falls as volume rises. Variable costs, such as raw materials, direct labor and activity-driven overhead, rise and fall in proportion to a cost driver, so they are constant per unit but change in total.[^s1] Economists frame the same split as the cost of fixed inputs (capital, equipment, space) versus variable inputs (labor, materials), with total cost equal to their sum and average and marginal cost derived from it.[^s2] Because the mix decides how profits respond to volume, cost structure is a core input to pricing, capacity and make-or-buy decisions.

## Origin and intellectual roots

The fixed/variable distinction comes from two traditions. Microeconomics defines the **short run** as the period in which some inputs cannot be changed, so output is adjusted only through variable inputs; a barbershop can add barbers but not floor space.[^s2] Managerial and cost accounting turned the idea into a planning tool: costs are classified by how they behave as activity changes, and real costs are often **mixed** (a fixed base plus a per-unit charge, modeled as Y = a + bx) or **step** costs that jump once activity outgrows a capacity unit, such as hiring a second inspector.[^s1] All of these patterns are valid only within a **relevant range** of activity, outside of which the cost lines must be re-estimated.[^s1]

## How it works: break-even and operating leverage

Each unit sold earns a contribution margin (price minus variable cost) that first covers fixed costs and then becomes profit. A high-fixed-cost structure has a higher break-even volume but keeps more of each additional sales dollar once past it. **Operating leverage** captures this sensitivity, and the **degree of operating leverage (DOL)** is measured as contribution margin divided by net operating income; multiplying DOL by a percentage change in sales predicts the percentage change in operating income.[^s3]

OpenStax illustrates the trade-off with two companies earning identical income. The one with higher fixed costs has a DOL of 2.47 against 1.74 for its rival, so a 10% sales gain lifts its income 24.7% (versus 17.4%), but a 20% sales drop cuts it 49.4% (versus 34.8%).[^s3] Firms can deliberately reshape the mix, for example by automating, converting hourly staff to salary, or installing self-service checkouts, each of which swaps variable cost for fixed cost and raises both upside and downside.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

**Cost structure and risk.** Baruch Lev's 1974 study in the *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* linked operating decisions to stock risk, showing analytically and empirically that higher operating leverage (lower unit variable costs) is associated with greater total and systematic risk. He argued that capital projects which raise operating leverage should be evaluated at a higher cost of capital, and that historical returns can mislead investors when a firm's cost mix is changing.[^s4]

**Costs are not perfectly fixed or variable.** The textbook model assumes costs move symmetrically with activity. Anderson, Banker and Janakiraman (2003), using 7,629 firms over 20 years, found that SG&A costs rose about 0.55% per 1% sales increase but fell only about 0.35% per 1% decrease. They attribute this "stickiness" to managers deliberately retaining committed resources when demand falls, which means a firm's effective cost structure depends on managerial choices, not only on technology.[^s5]

**Shock exposure.** A SUERF policy brief by Allinger and Huljak (January 2022), using data on 96,311 Croatian firms for 2008–2020, found that firms with high fixed-cost shares saw profits and liquidity respond more sharply to sales declines, an effect amplified in the 2020 pandemic year. Staff expenses were the least responsive cost, falling only 0.29% per 1% sales decline, and wage-support programs cushioned the hit.[^s6] The same study observed that high-fixed-cost firms tended to offset this by carrying less debt and larger liquidity buffers.[^s6]

## In practice

Airlines are a standard example of a high-fixed-cost industry: aircraft, gates and crews must be paid for whether or not seats are filled, which makes earnings fragile in downturns. OpenStax notes that carriers such as Delta have sought to shift toward more variable costs to reduce that exposure.[^s3] Managers use cost-structure analysis to set break-even targets, judge whether to lease or buy capacity, outsource production, or adopt automation, always weighing higher potential profit against greater sensitivity to falling demand.[^s3][^s1]

## Connections

Cost structure sits under **managerial accounting** and **microeconomics**, which supply its cost-behavior and short-run cost concepts.[^s1][^s2] It is the foundation for **cost-volume-profit analysis** and is measured through the **degree of operating leverage**.[^s3] **Economies of scale** and the **experience curve** describe how average cost falls as fixed costs are spread over more output, while **activity-based costing** refines how overhead is traced to cost drivers. The **Business Model Canvas** treats cost structure as one of its building blocks. It contrasts with **capital structure**: operating leverage comes from fixed operating costs, financial leverage from fixed financing costs, and Lev's work and the SUERF data suggest firms trade one off against the other.[^s4][^s6] The **airline industry** exemplifies the risks of a heavy fixed-cost base.[^s3]
