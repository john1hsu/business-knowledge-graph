# Bengt Holmström

Bengt Holmström (born 18 April 1949 in Helsinki) is a Finnish economist and the Paul A. Samuelson Professor of Economics, Emeritus, at MIT, with a joint appointment at the MIT Sloan School of Management.[^s2][^s3] He shared the 2016 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel with Oliver Hart for their contributions to contract theory.[^s2] His work asks a practical managerial question with mathematical precision: how should an organization reward people whose effort and judgment it can observe only imperfectly?[^s3]

## Career

Holmström grew up in a Swedish-speaking family and studied mathematics, physics and statistics at the University of Helsinki, graduating in 1972.[^s1] He then spent two years as an operations-research analyst and corporate planner at the Finnish conglomerate Ahlström, where he judged the company-wide planning model unreliable, recommended stopping it, and built smaller models for individual factories instead.[^s1][^s3] He has said that the incentive puzzles he saw there later inspired his models of career concerns.[^s1] A Fulbright-supported move to Stanford led to a doctorate in decision sciences at the Graduate School of Business in 1978, under Robert Wilson.[^s1][^s2]

After a postdoctoral year at CORE in Belgium and a short spell at the Hanken School of Economics, he taught managerial economics at Northwestern's Kellogg school (1979–1982), was Edwin J. Beinecke Professor of Management at Yale (1983–1994), and joined MIT in 1994, chairing its economics department from 2003 to 2006.[^s1][^s2] Unusually for a theorist, he also sat on the board of Nokia from 1999 to 2012, and on the board of Aalto University from 2010 to 2017.[^s2]

## Key ideas

- **Informativeness principle.** His early principal-agent work showed that pay should depend on every outcome that carries information about an employee's effort, which implies judging a CEO against peers' share prices rather than the firm's stock alone.[^s3]
- **Moral hazard in teams (1982).** When output is joint, splitting it among team members invites free riding; a principal who can run a scheme that does not balance the budget can fix this, which helps explain why firms separate ownership from labour. The paper also argued that relative performance evaluation is valuable only as a way of extracting information.[^s4]
- **Career concerns (Review of Economic Studies, 1999).** Even without explicit bonuses, workers exert effort because the market learns about their ability from performance and sets future wages accordingly; these implicit incentives can help or harm the firm, depending on how closely the worker's interest in their own reputation tracks the employer's financial returns.[^s5] One implication is that older workers, with fewer future career payoffs, respond less to this implicit motive.[^s3]
- **Multitasking (with Paul Milgrom).** Strong rewards for easily measured tasks pull effort away from hard-to-measure ones, so muting incentives can be optimal and job design becomes an incentive tool.[^s1][^s3]
- **Liquidity (with Jean Tirole).** In *Inside and Outside Liquidity* (2011) they argued that firms cannot pledge all of their future income to investors, which both creates a demand for liquid assets and leaves a shortage of them; private risk sharing is therefore incomplete, and governments can improve outcomes in a crisis.[^s7]

## Evidence and critiques

Holmström has been candid that his theory is easier to state than to apply. In his Nobel lecture of December 2016 he argued that pay for performance works best in stable settings where success is simple to quantify, that the harder effort is to measure the weaker the link to pay should be, and that firms in high-risk lines of work often do better with fixed salaries.[^s6] He cited the Wells Fargo fake-accounts episode and output pressure linked to safety lapses in the Gulf of Mexico oil spill as cautionary cases of strong incentives on narrow measures.[^s6] He has also written that he revisited his earlier defence of incentive stock options after the role that bonuses played in the 2007–2009 financial crisis.[^s1] With Gary Gorton and Tri Vi Dang he advanced a contrarian claim: that opacity in money markets can be the outcome of optimal contracting rather than a flaw, a view he says has gained support but which runs against the common call for more transparency in banking.[^s1]

## In practice

The managerial lessons he draws are concrete: do not attach incentives to tasks that compete with each other, pay more for the important task and less for its rivals, use little or no incentive pay where measurement is poor, and remember that recognition and job design matter alongside money.[^s6] Judging a manager by the firm's share price relative to similar firms, which strips out market-wide luck, applies his informativeness argument directly.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Holmström is a founder of modern **contract theory**, the field he shares with his co-laureate **Oliver Hart**, and he helped formalize the **principal-agent problem** and **moral hazard**, including its team form.[^s2][^s4] His filtering argument underpins **relative performance evaluation**, and his 1999 paper is the standard reference on **career concerns**.[^s4][^s5] The same models explain the **agency costs** that **corporate governance** tries to contain, a subject he lists among his research areas.[^s2][^s3] His doctoral training under **Robert Wilson** at Stanford shaped his decision-theoretic approach, and his liquidity work with **Jean Tirole** links incentive theory to financial crises.[^s1][^s7]
