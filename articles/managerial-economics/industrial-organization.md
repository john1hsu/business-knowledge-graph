# Industrial Organization

Industrial organization (IO) is the field of economics concerned with markets that are not perfectly competitive. The University of Michigan's economics department summarises it as the economics of imperfect competition, centred on three questions: where market power comes from and what it does, how firm behaviour and market structure influence one another, and how public policy changes market outcomes given those forces.[^s1] For managers, IO supplies the analytical toolkit behind questions of pricing power, entry, mergers and platform competition.

## Origins: structure, conduct, performance

The field's first organising framework was the structure–conduct–performance (SCP) paradigm. Edward S. Mason introduced it at Harvard in 1939 and 1949, initially through detailed case studies of individual industries; Joe S. Bain's work in 1951 and 1956 was its first empirical application, comparing outcomes across many industries.[^s3] SCP holds that an industry's performance depends on the conduct of its buyers and sellers, which depends on market structure (typically measured by the number of firms or the distribution of market shares), which in turn rests on basic conditions such as technology and demand.[^s3]

SCP was later criticised as descriptive rather than analytic, because the causal links between structure, conduct and performance were seldom derived from explicit models. George Stigler argued in 1968 for price-theoretic models built on maximising behaviour, and other economists proposed monopolistic competition, game theory or transaction-cost reasoning as replacements.[^s3]

## The game-theoretic and empirical turns

From the 1980s the field was rebuilt on game theory. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, awarding Jean Tirole the 2014 economics prize on 13 October 2014 for his analysis of market power and regulation, noted that earlier policy thinking leaned on blanket rules such as capping monopoly prices and banning cooperation between competitors.[^s4] Tirole showed that such rules help in some settings and backfire in others: a price cap can push a dominant firm to cut costs yet also leave it excessive profits. From the mid-1980s his work gave a unified framework for policy on mergers, cartels and the regulation of monopolies.[^s4]

Alongside theory, IO developed econometric methods for estimating demand and supply in imperfectly competitive markets, and the modern field is predominantly empirical, combining descriptive work with structural models that marry game theory to data.[^s1][^s2] Successors to SCP include industry models of market power, structural models of differentiated products, dynamic games and the estimation of firm strategies.[^s3]

## Current research and policy relevance

The NBER's 2025 program report describes IO as the study of consumer and firm behaviour, competition, innovation and regulation, and documents its spread into other sectors: the share of IO working papers cross-listed with health economics rose from near zero in 1991–2000 to roughly 20% in 2021–2025.[^s2] Live debates include whether average US markups have risen over the past four decades, with some studies finding increases and Shapiro and Yurukoglu (2024) arguing the evidence does not show a broad decline in competition.[^s2] IO research also informed the 2023 revision of the US merger guidelines, and recent studies examine self-preferencing by Amazon and the effect of default settings on Google's search share, which has held around 80–90% for two decades (as of 2025).[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

IO's own history is a running critique. Cross-industry SCP findings lost authority because they lacked a model of conduct, and the structural methods that replaced them depend on assumptions about demand and firm behaviour that are themselves contested, as the unresolved markup debate shows.[^s2][^s3] Tirole's lesson that good policy is industry-specific also cuts against the appeal of simple, universal rules.[^s4]

## Connections

IO is a subfield of **microeconomics**. It is the home discipline of **economies of scale**, which shapes cost structure and entry, and of **two-sided market** theory, one of the platform questions the field now studies.[^s2] The **structure–conduct–performance paradigm** and the later **new empirical industrial organization** are its two methodological eras; Mason and Bain founded the first.[^s3] Its policy wing feeds **antitrust law** and merger review, while **game theory** and **barriers to entry** are among its core analytical tools.[^s2][^s4]
