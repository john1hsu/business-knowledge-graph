# Transaction Cost Economics: Markets, Hierarchies and the Make-or-Buy Decision

Transaction cost economics (TCE) explains why some economic activity is coordinated by prices in markets and some by managerial authority inside firms. Its answer is that using either mode is costly, and that each transaction tends to be placed in the governance structure that economizes on those costs.[^s1][^s2] The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences credited Oliver Williamson's version of the theory with explaining economic governance and, in particular, the boundaries of the firm when it awarded him a share of the 2009 economics prize with Elinor Ostrom.[^s3]

## Origin

The question comes from Ronald Coase's 1937 article "The Nature of the Firm". Standard theory said the price system allocates resources, yet much allocation happens administratively inside firms. Coase argued that using markets carries costs of preparing, negotiating and monitoring contracts; a firm arises when internal allocation is cheaper, and grows until one more internal transaction would cost more than buying it on the market.[^s1] In the Academy's paraphrase, if transaction costs were zero no firms would arise.[^s1] Coase received the 1991 prize partly for this insight.[^s1]

Williamson turned the idea into a testable theory. In his Nobel lecture he traces a line from Coase's question to his own 1971 paper on vertical integration, and recasts the core question as when a firm should make a component itself rather than buy it.[^s2]

## How it works

TCE takes the **transaction** as its unit of analysis and treats governance as the means of creating order, mitigating conflict and realizing mutual gain.[^s2] Two behavioral assumptions drive it. People are intendedly rational but only limitedly so (**bounded rationality**), so complex contracts are always incomplete; and when unforeseen disturbances arise, some parties will defect strategically (**opportunism**), especially when stakes are high.[^s2]

The key attribute of a transaction is **asset specificity**: investments tailored to one relationship lose value if redeployed elsewhere. Once such investments are made, what began as competitive bidding among many suppliers becomes a small-numbers bargaining relationship in which either side can hold the other up.[^s2] Williamson compares governance modes on incentive intensity, administrative control and contract-law regime. Markets combine strong incentives with weak command; hierarchies combine weak incentives with strong command and settle disputes internally; hybrids such as long-term contracts sit in between.[^s2] The prediction is that generic inputs should be bought, while hierarchy becomes more attractive as asset specificity and disturbances rise.[^s2][^s3] The Academy also noted the trade-off: firms can resolve conflicts by authority, but that authority can itself be abused.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

TCE is among the better-supported theories in organizational economics. Howard Shelanski and Peter Klein's 1995 review found the empirical literature unusually consistent with TCE's predictions, across vertical integration, franchising, transfer pricing, regulation and multinationals.[^s4] Williamson's lecture cites a later count of more than 800 empirical tests by 2006, described as broadly corroborative.[^s2] An early test by Kirk Monteverde and David Teece used probit models on U.S. automobile components and found that parts requiring specialized engineering know-how were more likely to be made in-house.[^s5]

The sharpest critique came from Sumantra Ghoshal and Peter Moran in 1996. They argued that TCE treats organizations merely as substitutes for failed markets and assumes opportunism as a starting point, so its prescriptions could be harmful if managers act on them. In their view, firms have a distinct organizational advantage that works on a different logic from markets.[^s6]

## In practice

The theory frames make-or-buy, outsourcing and alliance decisions. Williamson's own example is Boeing's decision to outsource the 787 fuselage to Vought, a relationship involving substantial specific investments that TCE would have flagged as a risk for market contracting.[^s2] Managers use the same logic when they ask whether a supplier's tooling, location or know-how is so tailored to them that switching later would be costly, and whether a long-term contract, joint ownership or vertical integration would protect those investments.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

TCE is a core strand of the **theory of the firm**, answering Coase's question of why firms exist, and of **new institutional economics**, whose study of economic governance the 2009 prize recognized.[^s1][^s3] It was **created by** **Oliver Williamson** and is **influenced by** **Ronald Coase** and his 1937 paper.[^s1][^s2] It **applies to** the **make-or-buy decision** and **vertical integration**.[^s2] It sits beside the **principal-agent problem** and **information asymmetry**: all three explain organization through incomplete contracts and hidden information or action.[^s2] Asset-specific hold-up is the supplier-side version of **switching costs**, the mechanism Monteverde and Teece measured, and **David J. Teece** co-authored that early test.[^s5] Within **industrial organization**, TCE supplies a governance explanation for why firms integrate vertically, a prediction the Academy said has received empirical support.[^s3][^s4]
