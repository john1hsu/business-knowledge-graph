# Automotive Industry

The automotive industry consists of the firms and activities that design and build motor vehicles, chiefly passenger cars and light trucks, together with most of their components such as engines and bodies; Britannica's definition leaves out tires, batteries and fuel.[^s1] Statistical agencies draw the boundary more widely: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics groups vehicle assembly, bodies and trailers, and parts manufacturing (NAICS 3361–3363) with parts wholesaling, dealers and repair shops.[^s7] For students of managerial economics it is a classic case of how production technology and scale economies shape market structure, and of how incumbents respond when new entrants arrive with a different production system or product technology.

## From craft to mass production

Ford's Model T went on sale in 1908, and by 1913 Ford was assembling whole vehicles on a moving line that brought work to each worker, who repeated a single task.[^s2] The touring car's price fell from $950 in 1909 to $360 in 1916 and $290 in 1926, and by 1926 Ford built half of the world's motor vehicles.[^s2] Ford lost its lead in the mid-1920s by clinging to the aging Model T, while General Motors, founded by William Durant in 1908 and led from 1923 by Alfred P. Sloan, adopted a structure of autonomous divisions coordinated by central staff that other automakers later copied.[^s2] With Chrysler's formation in 1925, the Big Three held about three-quarters of the U.S. market by 1929, and the number of U.S. manufacturers fell from 108 to 44 in under a decade; the Depression then eliminated all but the largest independents.[^s2]

## Economic structure

**Scale and concentration.** Mass production demands heavy capital investment, so output in each major producing country is concentrated in a few very large firms and small independents have nearly vanished.[^s3] Britannica puts the efficient output of a single assembly plant at roughly 200,000 to 400,000 cars a year, and notes that stricter environmental rules added further pressure to consolidate.[^s3] These scale requirements are a textbook barrier to entry.

**Vertical boundaries and suppliers.** By the end of the twentieth century GM and Ford had spun off their component divisions as Delphi and Visteon, and automakers increasingly outsource non-core modules such as seats and wheel assemblies to nearby suppliers that deliver them just in time, often giving those suppliers design responsibility.[^s3] U.S. automakers rely on independent suppliers more than producers elsewhere.[^s3] In October 2026 (preliminary, seasonally adjusted) U.S. motor vehicle and parts manufacturing employed about 965,500 people, while dealers employed about 2.05 million.[^s7]

**Global footprint.** World production reached 96.4 million vehicles in 2025, up 3.9% from 2024, according to the manufacturers' association OICA; China produced 34.53 million, ahead of the United States (10.24 million), Japan (8.41 million) and India (6.49 million).[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Concentration has not meant ever-rising market power. Grieco, Murry and Yurukoglu, estimating demand and costs for U.S. cars and light trucks from 1980 to 2018, find that although prices rose, concentration and market power fell substantially, and consumers captured a growing share of total surplus thanks to better quality and lower marginal costs; they link the trends to import competition, the growth of SUVs and changing vehicle characteristics.[^s4] The industry's history also cautions against reading scale as durable advantage: Ford's early dominance and the later inroads of Japanese producers, whose just-in-time methods U.S. and European rivals adopted in the 1980s and 1990s, show that production systems can matter as much as size.[^s2][^s3] Critics of the industry point to its long product cycles and risk, illustrated by Ford's failed Edsel; development once took about five years, though many makers have cut it below three by using cross-functional teams.[^s3]

## In practice: the electric-vehicle transition

The shift to electric drivetrains is the industry's current structural test. Global electric car sales grew 20% in 2025 to more than 20 million, about one in four new cars; China's sales share approached 55% while the U.S. share stayed just under 10%.[^s5] Chinese brands supplied 60% of global electric car sales in 2025, China produced nearly 75% of electric cars, and its EV exports doubled to more than 2.5 million, with the IEA noting that fierce domestic competition is squeezing margins and pushing makers abroad.[^s5] For incumbents built around combustion engines this resembles earlier episodes of entry by rivals with a different cost structure.

## Connections

The industry sits under **industrial organization**, which uses it to test how scale, entry and conduct shape performance.[^s4] It is the birthplace of **mass production**, exemplified by the **Ford Model T**, and of the multidivisional structure developed at **General Motors**.[^s2] **Toyota Motor Corporation** exemplifies the later wave of **lean manufacturing** and just-in-time supply.[^s3] Plant-level scale links it to **economies of scale** and **barriers to entry**.[^s3] The rise of the **electric vehicle** is reshaping its competitive map.[^s5] Like the **airline industry**, it is a concentrated, capital-intensive sector that economists use to study how new competition erodes incumbents' market power.[^s3][^s4]
