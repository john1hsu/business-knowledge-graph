# Price Discrimination

**Price discrimination** means selling the same good at different prices to different buyers even though serving each of them costs the same.[^s6] It is a pricing practice, not a statement about intent toward particular groups: the seller simply sorts buyers by willingness to pay, whether by observable traits such as age or location or by the choices buyers make.[^s6] Hal Varian's survey for the *Handbook of Industrial Organization* (1989) calls it ubiquitous and argues that most firms with market power attempt some version of it, which is why its forms and welfare effects are central questions in industrial organization.[^s3]

## Origin

The standard taxonomy comes from Arthur Cecil Pigou's *The Economics of Welfare* (first published 1920), in the chapter "Discriminating Monopoly". Pigou distinguished three degrees of discriminating power.[^s1]

- **First degree**: each unit is sold at the buyer's maximum valuation, so buyers keep none of the surplus; Pigou treated this as largely theoretical.[^s1]
- **Second degree**: the seller sets a few price tiers, selling high-valued units at one price and lower-valued units at progressively lower prices.[^s1]
- **Third degree**: buyers are split into a few groups distinguishable by some visible mark, and each group pays its own monopoly price; Pigou saw this as the form actually found in practice.[^s1]

Modern economists use "second degree" more broadly to cover self-selection through nonlinear pricing, where buyers sort themselves by the option they choose, and "third degree" for different linear prices charged to observably distinct segments.[^s2]

## How it works

Lars Stole's 2007 *Handbook* chapter lists three requirements: the firm must have at least short-run market power, consumers must be separable into groups directly or indirectly, and arbitrage between the cheap and expensive versions must be infeasible.[^s2] Pigou had made the same point about resale, noting that services delivered directly to a person, such as a doctor's care, cannot be passed on, while distance, tariffs and resale clauses give partial protection; if buyers can switch freely into the cheaper market, discrimination gains the seller nothing.[^s1] Stole also cautions that discrimination can persist even where long-run profits are zero, so observing it is an imperfect sign of lasting market power.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Economics gives no blanket verdict. Under first-degree discrimination buyers keep no surplus at all,[^s1] while third-degree discrimination is harder to judge. With a fixed number of equally efficient firms, it can raise total welfare only if it increases total output; otherwise it merely reshuffles a given output toward lower-value uses. Joan Robinson (1933) made this argument for monopoly, and Richard Schmalensee (1981) and Varian (1985) generalized it.[^s2] Under oligopoly the results depend on market details: competition can push prices down in every segment, and in some models firms would rather commit to uniform pricing because discrimination intensifies rivalry.[^s2] Varian also warned that elaborate fare and tariff structures, such as those of post-deregulation US airlines, impose search and decision costs on buyers.[^s3] Britannica notes that whether consumers are harmed on balance remains debated.[^s6]

Data now make near-first-degree pricing feasible. Benjamin Reed Shiller (Brandeis working paper, revised 2014) used 2006 Netflix and comScore browsing data to simulate personalized subscription prices. Demographics alone raised variable profit by about 0.14%, but adding web-browsing variables raised it by about 2.14%; some consumers would pay roughly double what others paid for the same plan, and aggregate consumer surplus fell by about 7.75%.[^s4]

## In practice

Common examples include complex airline fare structures, utility tariffs, retail sales, coupons and price-matching offers,[^s3] as well as group prices keyed to traits such as age or location.[^s6] In the United States, the Robinson-Patman Act limits one variant: a seller may violate it by charging competing buyers different prices for commodities of like grade and quality where the difference may injure competition. It does not cover services, and sellers may defend price differences as cost-justified or as a good-faith response to a competitor's price.[^s5] Historically, anti-discrimination laws targeted firms that undercut a rival in one market while charging more elsewhere to drive it out.[^s6]

## Connections

Price discrimination sits within **microeconomics** and **industrial organization**, where its welfare effects are studied.[^s2][^s3] It was **created by** **Arthur Cecil Pigou**, who set out its three degrees in **The Economics of Welfare**.[^s1] It **depends on** **market power** and on separating customers into segments, which links it to **price elasticity**, **segmentation, targeting and positioning** and **pricing strategy**.[^s2] **Bundling** is one of the discriminatory techniques Stole's survey analyses alongside the three degrees.[^s2] In the US it is **regulated by** the **Robinson-Patman Act**, part of **antitrust law**.[^s5] It **applies to** the **airline industry**, whose post-deregulation fare structures Varian singles out,[^s3] and it is related to the **razor-and-blades model**, another pricing design that charges customers differently according to how they use a product.
