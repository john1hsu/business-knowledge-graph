# Game Theory: Strategic Interdependence Among Rival Firms

Game theory is the branch of applied mathematics that studies situations where several decision makers ("players") make interdependent choices, so that each must anticipate the strategies of the others; a solution specifies each player's best decisions and the outcome they produce.[^s1] In business, the players are usually firms, buyers, suppliers, regulators or bidders, and the questions are practical: how a rival will respond to a price cut, whether a threat to fight entry is believable, and how to bid when the value of the prize is uncertain.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

The field was founded by mathematician John von Neumann and economist Oskar Morgenstern, whose 1944 book *The Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* argued that economic life resembles a game in which participants anticipate one another's moves, and so needs different mathematics from the physical sciences.[^s1] John Nash then separated cooperative games, where binding agreements are possible, from non-cooperative games, where they are not, and defined an equilibrium for any number of players: a set of strategies in which each player's expectations about the others are confirmed and nobody gains by changing strategy alone.[^s2] Nash shared the 1994 economics prize with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten for this work on equilibria in non-cooperative games.[^s2]

## How it works

An analyst specifies the players, the strategies open to each, the payoffs for every combination of choices, and what each player knows.[^s1] Games may be constant-sum, where one side's gain is the other's loss, or variable-sum, where both sides can gain or lose together, as in a labour dispute where averting a strike helps both.[^s1] Two refinements matter most for managers. Selten's subgame perfection discards equilibria that rest on threats a player would not actually carry out; for example, a monopolist's threat of a price war deters an entrant only if fighting would pay once entry has happened.[^s2] Harsanyi showed how to model games of incomplete information by giving each player a "type" with private preferences, which underpins much of the economics of information, such as a regulator that cannot observe a monopoly's costs.[^s2]

The standard teaching case is the prisoner's dilemma. OpenStax illustrates it with a duopoly in which each firm earns $1,000 if both restrain output but only $400 if both expand; because expanding is each firm's dominant strategy, both expand and end up worse off than if they had cooperated.[^s3] The same logic explains why cartels are fragile: each member is tempted to sell a little more, agreements cannot be enforced in court, and cartels such as a group of French detergent makers have collapsed into price wars.[^s3]

## In practice

Game-theoretic thinking shapes pricing and price-war analysis, capacity and entry decisions, and negotiation.[^s1] Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff's 1995 *Harvard Business Review* article "The Right Game" pushed the idea toward strategy formulation: instead of only playing the existing game well, managers should map all the players and their interdependencies and consider cooperative as well as competitive ways to change the game.[^s4]

The best-documented applied success is auction design. Robert Wilson showed that when an item has a common but uncertain value, such as future radio-spectrum rights, rational bidders shade bids below their own estimate to avoid overpaying; Wilson and Paul Milgrom then invented formats for selling many interrelated objects at once.[^s5] US authorities first used one of these formats in 1994 to sell spectrum licences to telecom operators, and many countries followed; the two received the 2020 economics prize.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Game theory assumes rational players with well-defined goals, but observed behaviour such as buying lottery tickets or insurance often departs from expected-payoff maximisation, and the utility axioms underlying the theory have been questioned.[^s1] Many games have several equilibria, so the theory may not predict a unique outcome, and there is no single game theory, only different solution concepts suited to different settings.[^s1][^s2] Realistic games can be computationally intractable, and the theory says how to pursue goals rather than what goals to choose.[^s1] On cooperation, results are mixed: a known, finite number of repetitions does not escape the prisoner's dilemma, but with an uncertain horizon cooperation can emerge, and in Robert Axelrod's 1980 tournament "nice," forgiving strategies did best.[^s1]

## Connections

Game theory is a core tool of microeconomics and especially industrial organization, where Nash equilibrium is the standard way to model competition among a few firms that cannot sign binding agreements.[^s2] Its main building blocks are the Nash equilibrium and the prisoner's dilemma, and its chief application area is oligopoly, including collusion and cartel stability.[^s3] Selten's credible-threat logic links it to barriers to entry and first-mover moves, while Harsanyi's incomplete-information games connect it to information asymmetry and contract theory.[^s2] Pricing strategy draws on it for price-war and matching-response analysis, and spectrum auctions are its most cited real-world example.[^s1][^s5]
