# Oligopoly: Markets Dominated by a Few Interdependent Firms

An oligopoly is a market in which a handful of large firms hold all or most of the sales; standard textbook examples include automobiles, cable television and commercial air travel.[^s1] What sets it apart from both monopoly and perfect competition is interdependence: because sellers are few, any one firm's price or output move noticeably affects its rivals, who are likely to react.[^s2] OpenStax describes oligopoly as probably the second most common market structure, after monopolistic competition.[^s1]

## Why oligopolies arise

Oligopolies persist behind barriers to entry. Some are natural: when economies of scale are large relative to total demand, only two or three firms can produce at efficient scale, which is how OpenStax explains the Boeing–Airbus duopoly in large passenger jets.[^s1] Others are built or granted: patents, the cost of building brands such as Coke and Pepsi, expensive equipment, and regulation.[^s1][^s3] The St. Louis Fed points to FDA standards and tariffs in U.S. infant formula, spectrum allocation that limited broadcast television to three dominant networks from the 1950s through the 1980s, and airline startup costs and gate access.[^s3]

## Compete or collude?

Each oligopolist faces two pulls. Jointly, the firms would earn the most by acting like a single monopolist, restricting output and keeping prices high; individually, each wants a bigger share than its rivals.[^s2] OpenStax models this as a prisoner's dilemma: in its two-firm example, both earn more if they hold output down, yet expanding output is each firm's dominant strategy, so both end up worse off unless they can punish defectors.[^s1] Formal cartels are illegal in the United States, so most collusion there is tacit, a set of unspoken understandings; Britannica notes that outright monopoly pricing appears mainly where concentration is very high and entry is blocked, while a fringe of small competitors can cap price rises.[^s2] Firms that cooperate on price may still compete hard on promotion and product development.[^s2]

The kinked demand curve is a classic explanation of why oligopoly prices tend to stick. If rivals match price cuts but ignore price increases, a firm gains little volume from cutting and loses a lot from raising, so it holds its price, an outcome OpenStax calls a quiet form of cooperation.[^s1]

## Measuring concentration

Analysts gauge how oligopolistic a market is with concentration measures. The four-firm concentration ratio is a common benchmark, and a ratio of 100% means a monopoly.[^s3] U.S. antitrust agencies use the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), the sum of squared market shares. Under the 2023 Merger Guidelines, a market with an HHI above 1,800 is highly concentrated. A merger that pushes a market into that range and raises the HHI by more than 100 points is presumed to substantially lessen competition, as is a merger that gives the combined firm more than 30% of the market with the same HHI increase.[^s4] The guidelines warn that such mergers can make coordination among the remaining rivals more likely.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Older price theory dealt mostly with the polar cases of monopoly and perfect competition, which fit poorly the many real markets where a few firms shape prices, volumes and quality. Applying game theory to oligopoly became the core of modern industrial organization. The Nobel committee's 2014 award to Jean Tirole recognized work begun in the early 1980s on oligopoly and regulation. Its main policy lesson was that rules for mergers, vertical contracts and price cooperation should fit the specific industry, with no single standard.[^s5] The caution for managers and regulators is that structure alone does not settle outcomes: a concentrated market can be fiercely competitive or quietly collusive, depending on conduct.[^s3][^s5]

## Real-world examples

OPEC members sign agreements to hold down oil output and keep prices high. These agreements sit in a legal gray area internationally and cannot be enforced.[^s1] The lysine cartel shows the illegal version. Archer Daniels Midland and rivals met in the early 1990s to fix the price of this feed additive, which roughly doubled. ADM pled guilty in 1996 and paid a $100 million fine, and executives went to prison.[^s1] In U.S. airlines, mergers between 2005 and 2015 left four major carriers with about 80% of the market in 2015. Low-cost carriers expanding after 2016 then pushed the industry back toward more competition.[^s3]

## Connections

Oligopoly is a type of market structure and a central subject of industrial organization.[^s5] Game theory supplies its main analytical tools, such as the prisoner's dilemma.[^s1] It rests on barriers to entry and often on economies of scale.[^s1][^s3] Its degree of market power is measured by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, and antitrust law polices it through merger review and cartel enforcement.[^s4] It differs from monopolistic competition, where many firms sell differentiated products and no single firm's move provokes a direct response.[^s1] OPEC and the lysine price-fixing conspiracy are cartel cases, and the airline industry is a modern merger-driven example.[^s1][^s3] Jean Tirole's Nobel-recognized work built much of the modern theory.[^s5]
