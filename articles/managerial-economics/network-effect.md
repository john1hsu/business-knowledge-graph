# Network Effect

A **network effect** exists when the benefit a user gets from a product depends on how many other people use it, or on how many compatible complementary products exist. Michael Katz and Carl Shapiro formalised the idea in 1985, defining network externalities as cases where a consumer's utility increases with the number of other agents consuming the same good.[^s1] Because value grows with adoption, network effects act as economies of scale on the demand side rather than the cost side.[^s1]

## Channels of network effects

Katz and Shapiro identified three channels.[^s1] **Direct** effects arise from physical connection: a telephone, telex or fax machine is worth more the more people it can reach. **Indirect** effects work through complements: as more personal computers or video players are sold, more software or content is produced for them, raising the value of the hardware. A third channel is **post-purchase service**: durable goods with a larger installed base attract denser and more experienced repair networks, which Katz and Shapiro used to explain why early foreign car brands struggled.[^s1] In a 1994 survey they extended the analysis to "systems markets," where buyers assemble compatible components such as hardware and software, and argued that network effects shape strategy and innovation across many high-technology industries.[^s2]

## How it works

Because buyers must commit before they know how large each network will become, **expectations** drive outcomes. In Katz and Shapiro's model, if consumers expect a supplier to dominate, they will pay more for its product, which helps make the expectation come true.[^s1] Markets with incompatible products can therefore have several equilibria, including a "natural monopoly" in which one network wins, whereas full compatibility yields a single symmetric equilibrium with higher total output.[^s1] The model also explains strategic attitudes toward standards: firms with large networks or strong reputations tend to resist compatibility even when it would raise welfare, while firms with small networks tend to favour it.[^s1]

In practice this positive feedback makes **installed base** a strategic asset and lets markets tip quickly toward a leader. Speaking as a Justice Department antitrust official in 1996, Shapiro described demand-side scale economies as making larger networks more attractive and noted that compatibility can be both a source of competitive advantage and a barrier to entry.[^s4] He contrasted "real" networks such as telephone and electricity grids with "virtual" networks of users of a compatible technology, such as Macintosh or VHS owners.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest critique came from Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis, who separated a broad network *effect* from a network *externality*, which they reserved for cases where markets leave gains from trade unexploited.[^s3] They argued that many indirect effects are pecuniary, transmitted through prices, and that owners of a network or its participants can often internalise the benefits. They also noted that many networks need only a critical mass, after which additional users add little.[^s3] Reexamining two famous lock-in stories, they disputed that the QWERTY keyboard was demonstrably inferior to Dvorak and that Betamax was clearly better than VHS. They concluded that network externalities causing real market failure are theoretically fragile and empirically undocumented.[^s3] The debate leaves a practical lesson for managers: network effects are common, but they do not guarantee that an early or larger network cannot be displaced.

## In practice

Shapiro's 1996 address gives period examples of strategy and antitrust in network industries. He cited Nintendo's requirement that third-party developers write exclusively for its console for two years, which made it more likely the market would tip toward Nintendo, and the boom in fax machines as a case of self-fulfilling expectations.[^s4] He listed conduct that enforcers watch for in such markets, including pre-announcing products to freeze buyers, exclusive dealing, and using control of one standard to capture the next generation through tying or bundling.[^s4] Sony and Philips' joint compact-disc standard illustrated how cooperation on standards can help a new technology get established.[^s4]

## Connections

Network effects are a central topic of **industrial organization**, the field in which Katz and Shapiro's models were developed.[^s2] They **contrast with economies of scale**: both reward size, but scale economies lower unit cost on the supply side while network effects raise value on the demand side.[^s1] A **two-sided market** is the special case in which the effect runs across two user groups, such as consoles and game developers.[^s4] Network effects interact with **switching costs** to lock users into an installed base, and together they form a demand-side **barrier to entry**.[^s4] Liebowitz and Margolis's critique ties the concept to debates about **path dependence** and whether markets get stuck on inferior standards.[^s3] Because tipping can entrench dominant firms, network effects are also a recurring issue in **antitrust law**.[^s4]
