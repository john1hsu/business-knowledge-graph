# Contract Theory

**Contract theory** is the part of economics that asks how agreements should be written when the parties want different things and know different things. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, awarding the 2016 economics prize to Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström "for their contributions to contract theory", described it as a framework for analysing contracts between shareholders and a CEO, an insurer and a car owner, or a public authority and its suppliers.[^s1] Its practical reach includes performance pay for executives, deductibles and co-payments in insurance, and decisions about privatising public services.[^s1] Bolton and Dewatripont's graduate textbook presents it as a unifying treatment of agency theory, information economics and organization theory, applied to labor markets, industrial organization and corporate finance.[^s5]

## Two branches

The field is usually split into **complete-contract** (incentive) theory and **incomplete-contract** theory. In the first, the parties can write a contract on anything observable, and the problem is to tie rewards to the best available signals of hidden effort or hidden information. Holmström's late-1970s work modelled a principal, such as shareholders, designing pay for an agent, such as a CEO, whose actions the principal cannot fully observe; the optimal contract trades off risk against incentives, and his informativeness principle says pay should be linked to every measure that reveals something about the agent's effort.[^s1] The standard toolkit covers screening, signaling and moral hazard.[^s5]

The second branch starts from the fact that no contract can anticipate every contingency. Hart's work from the mid-1980s therefore shifts the question from pay formulas to **control rights**: who gets to decide when something unforeseen happens.[^s1] Grossman and Hart's 1986 paper in the *Journal of Political Economy* distinguished *specific* rights, spelled out in a contract, from *residual* rights over everything the contract leaves out, and defined ownership as the purchase of those residual rights.[^s2]

## How it works

**Multitasking.** Holmström and Paul Milgrom (1991) showed that when an agent splits limited effort across tasks, incentive pay decides not only how hard the agent works but where attention goes. Rewarding a measurable task, such as students' scores on basic-skills tests, can crowd out an unmeasured one, such as teaching higher-order thinking; in their contractor model the efficient contract is a flat wage even for a risk-neutral agent.[^s3] The same logic explains asset ownership and job design: employees rather than independent contractors are used when asset upkeep is hard to measure, and each task should have a single responsible agent, with easy-to-measure and hard-to-measure work assigned to different people.[^s3]

**Ownership and the boundaries of the firm.** In the Grossman-Hart model, integration is not free. When firm 1 buys firm 2, it gains control over firm 2's assets, but firm 2's manager loses residual rights and with them the incentive to invest. Integration is worthwhile only when the productivity gain from firm 1's control outweighs the loss at firm 2.[^s2] The authors built on transaction-cost work by Coase and Williamson but argued it did not explain what limits firm size.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Contract theory is mostly a body of formal models, so its claims are tested indirectly. Holmström and Milgrom pointed to franchising and to the choice between in-house and independent sales forces as patterns consistent with measurement-driven ownership, and argued that the multitask model explains why explicit incentive clauses are rarer than single-task theory predicts.[^s3] Hart, Shleifer and Vishny acknowledged that evidence on their prisons application was thin and complicated by differences in how injuries were reported.[^s4] A broader limitation is that results depend heavily on what is assumed to be observable or verifiable; the property-rights approach treats some quality dimensions as non-contractible by assumption, which is exactly what critics of privatisation and its defenders dispute in each case.[^s4] Even so, the Academy credited the two laureates with making contract theory a field of basic research that informs institutional design from bankruptcy law to political constitutions.[^s1]

## Real-world example: contracting out prisons

Hart, Shleifer and Vishny (1997) applied incomplete-contract theory to the choice between government provision and private contracting. A private operator holds residual control, so it can cut costs without approval and keeps the savings, whereas a public employee must seek approval and keeps only part of the gain.[^s4] Private provision therefore cuts costs more, but it may also degrade quality that the contract cannot specify. In the mid-1990s private prisons were roughly 10 percent cheaper per inmate, mostly through lower labor costs, while concerns centred on use of force, guard training and staffing.[^s4] The authors concluded that a plausible case exists against privatising prisons, strongest for maximum-security facilities, and noted that corruption pushes governments toward excessive privatisation while patronage and strong public-sector unions push toward excessive in-house provision.[^s4]

## Connections

Contract theory sits under **information economics** and the **theory of the firm**, and it was **influenced by** both **information asymmetry** and **transaction cost economics**, which it formalized and partly challenged.[^s2] Its complete-contract branch **has part** the **principal-agent problem** and the **informativeness principle**, which together address **moral hazard** and **adverse selection** and the **agency costs** they create.[^s1][^s5] It **applies to** **executive compensation**, one of the Academy's headline examples, and it underpins the incentive logic of **government contract types**, where a public buyer chooses how much cost risk to shift to a supplier.[^s1][^s4] It is **exemplified by** the analysis of **prison privatization**.[^s4] Its two founders in the Nobel citation are **Bengt Holmström** and **Oliver Hart**.[^s1]
