# Microeconomics

Microeconomics is the part of economics that looks at individual decision-makers (households, workers and businesses) rather than the economy as a whole. On the household side it asks how people divide a budget, how much to work and how much to save or borrow; on the firm side it asks what and how much to produce, what price to charge, how many people to hire, and when to expand, shrink or exit.[^s1] Its counterpart, macroeconomics, studies aggregate growth, unemployment, inflation, deficits and trade. OpenStax stresses that the two are complementary: a firm's hiring choice depends on the health of the wider economy, while the macroeconomy is ultimately built from the choices of households and firms.[^s1]

## Core ideas

In its traditional form, microeconomics models the economy as households and firms meeting in two kinds of markets: product markets, and factor markets for labour, capital and land. Prices emerge from demand and supply, and the field's job is to explain the forces behind each side.[^s2] Demand curves usually slope down and supply curves up; where they cross is the equilibrium or market-clearing price. Shifting either curve lets the analyst predict how changes in input costs, incomes, taxes, price ceilings or price supports alter prices and quantities.[^s2]

Behind the curves sits a theory of choice. Households are modelled as having preferences, expressed as utility functions, which they maximise subject to income; firms face technical constraints, expressed as production functions, and maximise profit by choosing the cheapest combination of inputs.[^s2] The unifying tool is marginal analysis. Britannica describes the equimarginal principle, under which an optimal allocation equalises the return on the last unit across every use, as the idea of which most of microeconomics is an extension; diminishing marginal utility is what ensures such an optimum exists.[^s2]

Market structure is the other pillar. Textbooks place markets on a spectrum from perfect competition, where many firms sell identical products and must accept the market price, to monopoly, where a single seller offers a product with no close substitutes. Between them lie monopolistic competition (many sellers of differentiated products, typical of retail) and oligopoly (a few dominant firms behind high entry barriers, such as Boeing and Airbus in commercial aircraft), in which each firm must anticipate its rivals' moves.[^s3]

## Origins

Many of the field's standard tools were codified by Alfred Marshall, whose *Principles of Economics* (1890) became the standard university text of English neoclassical economics.[^s4] Marshall presented the law of demand alongside diminishing marginal utility, measured utility in money as willingness to pay, introduced the price elasticity of demand, and formalised consumer surplus (his "consumers' rent") as the gap between what a buyer would pay and what they actually pay. He added the "representative firm" in the 1891 second edition and is reported to have drawn the familiar price-quantity diagram, with price on the vertical axis, around 1869-70.[^s4] His work built on the marginalist ideas of W. S. Jevons, whose book Marshall reviewed in 1872 and later praised.[^s4] Britannica credits John Maynard Keynes with the later split between microeconomics and macroeconomics.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Two lines of criticism matter most for business use. First, standard price theory concentrates on price competition and says comparatively little about other ways firms compete: on quality, location, service, advertising and internal organisation. Britannica judges it poorly equipped for many modern economic problems.[^s2] Industrial organization and transaction cost economics grew partly to fill this gap. Second, behavioural research challenges the assumption of fully rational, self-interested agents. The 2017 economics prize to Richard Thaler recognised evidence that limited rationality (for example, mental accounting and the endowment effect), concern for fairness and weak self-control systematically shift individual choices and market outcomes.[^s5] The Academy's citation notes, for instance, that consumers may punish firms that raise prices on a demand spike while accepting increases driven by higher costs, a constraint on pricing that a pure profit-maximisation model misses.[^s5]

## In practice

Managerial economics is largely applied microeconomics. Pricing decisions draw on demand and elasticity; make-or-buy, staffing and capacity decisions draw on production and cost theory; and competitive strategy draws on market-structure analysis. An oligopolist such as an aircraft maker cannot set price or capacity without modelling how its main rival will respond, whereas a firm in a near-perfectly competitive market has little pricing discretion and competes chiefly on cost.[^s3] The fairness findings add a practical caveat: surge-style price increases can cost goodwill even when theory says they clear the market.[^s5]

## Connections

Microeconomics sits under **economics** (`broader`) and **contrasts with macroeconomics**, its aggregate-level counterpart.[^s1] **Industrial organization** is the subfield that extends it to imperfectly competitive markets and market power, building on the market-structure spectrum described above.[^s3] **Price elasticity** and **cost structure** are core microeconomic tools for pricing and production decisions, the former introduced by Marshall, whose *Principles* **influenced** the field's textbook form.[^s4] **Behavioral economics** revises its rationality assumptions using psychological evidence.[^s5]
