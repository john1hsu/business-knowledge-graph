# Economies of Scale

**Economies of scale** exist when a larger volume of output can be produced at a lower average cost per unit. Britannica frames the idea as the link between the size of a plant or industry and the lowest attainable unit cost, and traces the savings to deeper specialization of labour, better technology, and fuller use of resources.[^s2] The opposite case, in which growth pushes average cost up, is called **diseconomies of scale**.[^s2] Because scale affects both what a firm can charge and how many rivals a market can support, it is one of the most frequently invoked ideas in competitive strategy and industrial organization.

## How it works

In the long run every input is variable, so a firm can choose the plant size that best fits its intended output. OpenStax's *Principles of Economics* shows the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve as the lower envelope of a family of short-run average cost curves, one for each possible plant size.[^s1] The LRAC typically has three zones: a falling section where economies of scale apply, a flat section of constant returns to scale, and a rising section where diseconomies take over.[^s1] Its illustrative alarm-clock factory sees average cost drop from $12 at 1,000 units to $4 at 5,000 units.[^s1]

Sources of scale economies include spreading fixed costs over more units, specialized equipment and labour, and engineering relationships. A frequently cited physical example: a pipe with double the circumference uses roughly double the material but carries about four times the volume, which is why large chemical plants and warehouses are cheaper per unit.[^s1] Big-box retailers such as Costco and Walmart, and Amazon's automated fulfilment centres, are the textbook commercial illustrations.[^s1]

Diseconomies arise mainly from coordination. As firms grow, layers of management multiply and communication failures disrupt the flow of work and materials; OpenStax notes that inefficiently large plants can persist only when protected from competition, as in the former Soviet economy.[^s1]

## Scale and market structure

The shape of the LRAC curve relative to market demand helps predict industry structure. If minimum average cost is reached at a small fraction of total demand, many firms can coexist; if it is reached at a large share of demand, the market tends toward oligopoly; and if one firm's efficient output exceeds market demand, a single producer is likely.[^s1] A flat-bottomed curve lets firms of very different sizes compete on cost, whereas a sharply defined minimum pushes surviving firms toward a similar size.[^s1]

Business historian Alfred D. Chandler Jr. made scale central to explaining the rise of the modern corporation. His 1990 book *Scale and Scope* compared the largest industrial enterprises in the United States, Britain and Germany and tied their growth to capabilities for exploiting scale and scope.[^s5] In Richard Langlois's reading, Chandler argued that first movers which made a three-pronged investment in high-throughput production, distribution networks and salaried management locked in durable advantages and turned their industries into oligopolies.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical work shows that scale economies are real but often exhausted at sizes smaller than intuition suggests. Christensen and Greene's 1976 study of US electricity generators reported no significant scale economies for firms producing more than about 19 billion kWh per year; later work by Howorka challenged that conclusion, arguing that their cross-sectional data and assumption of full capacity utilization biased the estimates.[^s4] A 2019 replication in *Energy Economics* by Bernstein and Parmeter, using nonparametric methods and updated data, found that measured returns to scale in US electricity generation declined between 1955 and 1996, with magnitudes sensitive to the estimator.[^s3]

Chandler's scale-centred account has also been criticised. Langlois argues that it overstates the corporation's dominance over markets, underplays supplier and dealer networks, and neglects how modular, market-based systems such as Silicon Valley build capabilities without vast integrated firms.[^s6]

## Connections

Economies of scale is a core idea in **industrial organization** and a component of a firm's **cost structure**.[^s1] It **contrasts with economies of scope**, savings from producing several products together, which Chandler paired with scale in *Scale and Scope*.[^s5] The **Ford Model T** is the classic case of mass production driving unit cost down as volume rose. Minimum efficient scale, the bottom of the LRAC curve, determines how many rivals a market can sustain and therefore links scale to **barriers to entry**.[^s1] Scale economies are supply-side; **network effects** are their demand-side counterpart, where value rather than cost improves with size.
