# Price Elasticity

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how strongly the quantity buyers demand responds to a change in price: it is the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price.[^s3] Because price and quantity normally move in opposite directions the ratio is negative, and textbooks usually report its absolute value.[^s3] Demand is *elastic* when the absolute value exceeds 1 (quantity moves proportionally more than price), *inelastic* when it is below 1, and *unitary* when it equals 1.[^s3][^s2] For marketers it is the single number that links a pricing decision to revenue and volume.

## Origin

The concept was formalized by the economist Alfred Marshall in *Principles of Economics* (first edition 1890), in a chapter titled "The Elasticity of Wants."[^s1] Marshall described demand as elastic when the quantity bought rises or falls a great deal for a given change in price, and inelastic when it barely moves.[^s1] He also set out most of the determinants still taught today: necessities such as wheat have very inelastic demand; goods with many possible uses, or with close substitutes whose prices are held constant, are more elastic; elasticity differs by income class, since the rich barely change their purchases of everyday foods; and consumers respond more over time as they find substitutes and change habits.[^s1]

## How it works

Practitioners usually compute elasticity between two price points with the **midpoint (arc) method**, which divides each change by the average of the starting and ending values so that the answer is the same whether price rises or falls.[^s3] In one OpenStax example, a segment of a demand curve yields 0.45 (inelastic) while another yields 1.47 (elastic), showing that elasticity varies along a single curve.[^s3]

The managerial payoff is the link to **total revenue** (price × quantity). When demand is elastic, cutting price raises revenue because the volume gain outweighs the lower price; when demand is inelastic, raising price raises revenue; at unitary elasticity revenue is unchanged.[^s2] Elasticity also governs cost pass-through: firms facing inelastic demand, as in coffee or food, can pass higher input costs on to buyers, and the burden of a tax falls mostly on the less elastic side of the market.[^s2] Elasticities are typically smaller in the short run than the long run, because buyers need time to switch cars, appliances or habits; OpenStax uses the 1973 oil shock to show that prices swing more than quantities in the short run.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Marketing science has produced large meta-analyses of brand-level elasticities. Gerard Tellis (1988) analyzed 367 econometric estimates covering more than 220 brands or markets and found an average price elasticity of about −1.76; after bias corrections, price was roughly ten times as powerful as advertising, whose average elasticity was 0.22.[^s4] He also showed that estimates depend on method: omitting distribution makes elasticities more negative, omitting quality makes them less negative, and demand is less price-sensitive early in the product life cycle and for pharmaceuticals.[^s4] Bijmolt, van Heerde and Pieters (2005) updated this with 1,851 elasticities from 81 studies and found a mean of −2.62; sales elasticities had grown in magnitude over four decades, and correcting for price endogeneity made estimates substantially larger.[^s5]

These findings carry a warning: a reported elasticity is partly an artifact of data and model choices, so firms should not plug a published average into their own pricing.[^s4][^s5] Elasticity is also local — it changes along the demand curve and over time — and Marshall himself stressed that it depends on whether rivals' prices are assumed fixed.[^s1][^s3]

## In practice

Because most products are price-sensitive, small price moves have outsized profit effects. Marn and Rosiello of McKinsey, using the average economics of 2,463 Compustat companies, estimated in 1992 that a 1% price increase with no volume loss would lift operating profit by 11.1%, versus 3.3% for a 1% volume gain.[^s6] Elasticity is what tells managers whether "no volume loss" is realistic. Public policy shows the other side: cigarette demand among adult smokers has an elasticity of about 0.3, so a 10% price rise cuts smoking by only about 3%, which is why tobacco taxes raise revenue but change consumption less than hoped; young smokers are more price-sensitive.[^s2]

## Connections

Price elasticity is the quantitative core of **pricing strategy** and a basic tool of **microeconomics**.[^s2] It was introduced by **Alfred Marshall** in his **Principles of Economics**.[^s1] It underpins **segmentation** and **price discrimination**, since Marshall already observed that different income classes respond differently to the same price.[^s1] It shapes **customer lifetime value** calculations through the margin a firm can sustain, and it is the basis for analyzing **tax incidence**.[^s2] Its sibling measures include **cross-price elasticity**, which captures the substitute effects Marshall described, and **advertising elasticity**, which meta-analyses find to be far smaller than price elasticity.[^s1][^s4]
