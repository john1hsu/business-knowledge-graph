# Brand Equity: The Marketing Value of a Brand Name

**Brand equity** is the extra value a brand adds to a product compared with a substitute that lacks it. OpenStax's marketing text puts it simply: if buyers pay more for one of two similar products because of its name, the difference is brand equity, which is why a category leader such as Tide can charge more than near-identical detergents and why national brands typically price above private labels.[^s5] The concept treats a brand as an asset that marketing builds over time and that can be measured, managed and valued, rather than as a logo or a cost of advertising.[^s4]

## Origin

Two Berkeley-linked scholars shaped the idea. David Aaker and Kevin Lane Keller co-wrote research on brand extensions at Haas in the mid-1980s, and Aaker went on to develop a multi-dimensional, managerial view of brand strength.[^s1][^s4] Keller's 1993 *Journal of Marketing* article, "Conceptualizing, Measuring, and Managing Customer-Based Brand Equity", was the first comprehensive treatment of the customer-based view; Tuck reported it had been cited more than 4,000 times as of August 2019. Keller later codified the field in *Strategic Brand Management*, described as the best-selling branding textbook.[^s1]

## How it works

The **customer-based** view defines brand equity as the differential effect that brand knowledge has on how consumers respond to a brand's marketing.[^s1] A brand has positive equity when people react more favourably to its price, advertising or distribution than they would to the same offer from an unnamed product. Keller's later brand-resonance model arranges this as a pyramid: identity (who the brand is and what need it meets), meaning (performance and imagery), responses (judgments and feelings), and at the top, resonance, the loyalty and engagement customers feel.[^s5]

Aaker's measurement scheme groups ten measures into five categories: loyalty, perceived quality, associations, awareness and market behaviour, so that managers can track brand strength across products and markets.[^s4]

The **financial** view instead defines brand equity as the incremental cash flow that branded products earn over unbranded ones. Simon and Sullivan estimated it from a firm's market value by separating brand value from the firm's other assets, then traced how Coca-Cola's and Pepsi's brand equity moved through three major soft-drink events between 1982 and 1986.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Measurement is the central difficulty. Aaker concedes that his measures are hard to apply and must be interpreted carefully, though he argues they still give managers useful tools.[^s4] Customer-mindset measures can be rich but subjective, while market-value methods depend on assumptions needed to isolate the brand from other intangible assets.[^s2] As a middle path, Ailawadi, Lehmann and Neslin proposed the **revenue premium**, the extra revenue a brand earns over a comparable private-label product. They found it reliable, sensitive to real changes in brand strength, correlated with other brand equity measures, and related to advertising, promotion, price sensitivity and category risk as theory predicts.[^s3]

Brand equity can also be lent. Licensing and co-branding let a partner borrow another brand's equity, but licensing fees and revenue sharing mean the borrowed value is not free.[^s5]

## In practice

Consultancies publish annual brand valuations that blend financial results with brand strength. In Interbrand's Best Global Brands 2025 ranking, released in October 2025, Apple ranked first for the 13th straight year with an estimated brand value of $470.9 billion, down 4% from 2024, ahead of Microsoft ($388.5 billion) and Amazon ($319.9 billion).[^s6] The same ranking showed how fast estimated equity can shift: Nike's value fell 26% and Tesla's 35% in a year, while Nvidia's rose 116%.[^s6] Such estimates are proprietary and not comparable across valuation firms, which is one reason academics favour transparent measures like the revenue premium.[^s3][^s6]

## Connections

Brand equity sits under **brand management** and is one of a firm's **intangible assets**, a value that financial methods try to separate from the firm's other assets, which links it to accounting concepts such as **goodwill**.[^s2] Its customer-side mechanism depends on clear **segmentation, targeting and positioning**, since associations must be distinctive in a chosen segment.[^s5] Strong equity lowers buyers' **price elasticity**, letting a brand sustain a premium, and it raises **customer lifetime value** through loyalty.[^s3][^s5] Kevin Lane Keller formalised customer-based brand equity, drawing on his early work with **David Aaker**; the **revenue premium** is one validated outcome measure; and **Apple Inc.** is the leading current example of a brand valued in the hundreds of billions.[^s1][^s3][^s6]
