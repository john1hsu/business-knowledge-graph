# Service Quality: Customer Perceptions of Technical, Functional and Environmental Excellence

Service quality, in marketing, is the customer's own assessment of how good a service was. Because a service is a performance rather than an object, its quality cannot be inspected against an engineering specification before it reaches the buyer; it exists largely in the customer's perception, which is formed by comparing experience with prior expectations. OpenStax's marketing text frames this as the "customer gap": reality is fixed, but perception varies with word of mouth, personal needs and past experience.[^s7] The concept therefore differs from the manufacturing-centred notion of conformance quality that underpins total quality management, even though both aim at defect-free delivery.

## Origins: the Nordic and North American schools

The first influential model came from Christian Grönroos. In a 1984 article in the *European Journal of Marketing*, based on a survey of business executives, he proposed that customers perceive service quality along two interrelated dimensions: technical quality (what the customer receives) and functional quality (how it is delivered), filtered through the firm's corporate image. His main managerial conclusion was that functional quality, the manner of delivery, is a very important part of the perceived service.[^s1] This "Nordic" view emphasised the buyer-seller interaction.

The "North American" school, led by Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry, instead defined quality as the gap between expected and perceived performance on a set of generic attributes. Its textbook form is the RATER list: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy and responsiveness, with reliability meaning doing what was promised, accurately and on time.[^s7] These dimensions became the basis of the SERVQUAL instrument.

## Measurement debate and hierarchical models

In 1992 J. Joseph Cronin Jr. and Steven A. Taylor argued in the *Journal of Marketing* that gap-based measurement confused satisfaction with attitude. They tested a performance-only measure, later known as SERVPERF, and concluded that it may better capture service quality, that service quality is an antecedent of satisfaction, and that satisfaction had a stronger effect on purchase intentions than service quality did.[^s2] The disagreement over whether expectations need to be measured at all remains a central critique of difference-score approaches.

Michael Brady and Cronin later reconciled the two schools. Using qualitative work and surveys across four service industries, they modelled perceived service quality as a third-order construct with three primary dimensions: outcome quality, interaction quality and physical environment quality, each with three subdimensions. In their model, reliability, responsiveness and empathy act as modifiers of each subdimension rather than as standalone dimensions.[^s3] The outcome and interaction dimensions map closely onto Grönroos's technical and functional quality, while the environment dimension captures the servicescape.

## Evidence on consequences

The managerial case for service quality rests on its effect on customer behaviour. Zeithaml, Berry and Parasuraman's 1996 multi-company study proposed a model linking service quality to behavioural intentions that signal whether customers will stay or defect, and found strong evidence that quality influences those intentions, although the strength of the link varied by type of intention.[^s4] Roland Rust, Anthony Zahorik and Timothy Keiningham responded to executives who doubted that quality programmes paid off, after publicised financial failures at firms known for quality. Their 1995 "return on quality" approach treats quality as an investment that must be financially accountable, recognising that quality spending can be excessive and that not all quality expenditures are equally worthwhile.[^s5]

## In practice

Service firms rarely manage frontline quality in isolation. The service-profit chain, developed by Harvard researchers in the 1990s, holds that internal service quality (the support frontline staff receive from the rest of the organisation) drives employee satisfaction, which creates value, customer satisfaction, loyalty and finally profit. OpenStax illustrates the chain with Taco Bell, whose low-turnover restaurants had twice the sales and 55 percent higher profits than high-turnover stores.[^s6] A hotel, for example, may train friendly front-desk staff (interaction quality) yet still lose guests if housekeeping fails to clean rooms on time, an outcome-quality failure rooted in poor internal support.[^s6]

## Connections

Service quality sits within [[kt:services-marketing]] and the broader [[kt:marketing]] discipline. It is `measuredBy` [[kt:servqual]] and its performance-only rival [[kt:servperf]],[^s2] and its causes inside the provider are diagnosed by the [[kt:gaps-model-of-service-quality]].[^s7] It is `influencedBy` [[kt:christian-gronroos]]'s technical/functional model,[^s1] contains [[kt:internal-service-quality]] as a part of the service-profit chain,[^s6] and `causes` [[kt:customer-loyalty]] through behavioural intentions.[^s4] It is closely related to [[kt:customer-satisfaction]], which most evidence treats as its downstream consequence,[^s2] to [[kt:total-quality-management]], its manufacturing-oriented counterpart, and to [[kt:customer-lifetime-value]], the metric through which return-on-quality arguments are usually made.[^s5]
