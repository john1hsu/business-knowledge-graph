# Net Promoter Score

The **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** is a single-question measure of customer loyalty. Customers are asked how likely they are to recommend a company (or product) to a friend or colleague on a scale from 0 to 10. Those answering 9 or 10 are classed as *promoters*, 7 or 8 as *passives*, and 6 or below as *detractors*; the score is the share of promoters minus the share of detractors, so it can range from −100 to +100.[^s2] The metric sits within the wider family of **marketing metrics** alongside satisfaction, retention and lifetime value.

## Origin

Fred Reichheld, a Bain Fellow and advisory partner at Bain & Company, introduced the idea in the December 2003 *Harvard Business Review* article "The One Number You Need to Grow."[^s1] He reported a two-year research effort that tested a range of survey questions against customers' actual purchasing and referral behaviour and against company growth. In most of the industries studied, the share of customers willing to recommend a company tracked its growth rate relative to competitors, and the recommendation question outperformed satisfaction and loyalty questions.[^s1] His argument was that recommending a firm costs the customer something — their own reputation with a friend — so willingness to do it is a stronger loyalty signal than stated satisfaction.[^s1] The article also argued that companies were spending heavily on complex satisfaction surveys that measured the wrong thing.[^s1]

## How it works

Beyond the arithmetic, Bain presents NPS as the core of a management routine, the *Net Promoter System*: firms survey customers regularly, close the loop with individual detractors, and use the open-ended follow-up answers to find and fix the causes of dissatisfaction.[^s2] Reichheld and colleagues reported that by 2021 about two-thirds of the Fortune 1000 used NPS, and they cited earlier Bain analysis showing that a group of public NPS leaders had generated median shareholder returns far above the US median.[^s2] Because a single number is easy to communicate, NPS spread rapidly into board dashboards, executive pay plans and earnings calls.

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest academic challenge came from Timothy Keiningham and colleagues in the *Journal of Marketing* (2007). Using longitudinal data from 21 firms and more than 15,500 interviews in the Norwegian Customer Satisfaction Barometer, and comparing results with the American Customer Satisfaction Index, they could not replicate Reichheld's claim that NPS was clearly superior to other loyalty measures in predicting growth, even in the industries he had used as exemplars.[^s3]

Later work gives NPS partial support without vindicating the "one number" claim. A 2024 validity study in the *International Journal of Market Research* found that NPS responses aligned with reported word-of-mouth for most product categories and behaved consistently across demographic groups; both NPS and customer satisfaction predicted financial performance, with satisfaction explaining slightly more variance.[^s4] A companion 2024 study linking post-visit surveys to casino transaction records found that promoters spent more and were retained at higher rates than detractors, but satisfaction and likelihood-to-revisit fit the data about as well, and the spending gap between promoters and detractors was small.[^s5]

Reichheld himself has acknowledged misuse. In "Net Promoter 3.0" (2021) he and two Bain partners wrote that tying scores to frontline bonuses invited pleading and manipulation, and that firms reporting NPS to investors rarely disclosed sample sizes, response rates or methods, turning it into a vanity statistic for some.[^s2] Their proposed remedy is *earned growth*, an accounting-based complement that measures revenue from returning customers and their referrals using audited figures rather than survey answers.[^s2]

## In practice

The 2003 article framed the idea around a daylong forum at which chief executives from Vanguard, Chick-fil-A, State Farm and other loyalty-focused firms heard Enterprise Rent-A-Car's CEO, Andy Taylor, describe how his company measured customer loyalty, the kind of practitioner example Reichheld drew on in generalising the recommendation question.[^s1] Typical users today pair the score with verbatim comments, segment it by customer type or touchpoint, and treat changes over time as more informative than the absolute level, which varies widely by industry.[^s2] The research above suggests managers should validate NPS against their own retention and spending data before treating it as a growth forecast.[^s4][^s5]

## Connections

NPS is a specific instrument within **marketing metrics** and an operational proxy for **customer loyalty**, which is why both are its broader nodes.[^s1] It is closely tied to **customer satisfaction**: the two are the main rival survey-based measures, and studies repeatedly find them similarly predictive.[^s4][^s5] Keiningham et al. benchmarked it against the **American Customer Satisfaction Index**, the leading alternative national satisfaction measure.[^s3] Its behavioural counterparts are **churn rate** and **customer lifetime value**, which capture the retention and spending that NPS is meant to anticipate.[^s5] It was created by **Fred Reichheld** in the 2003 HBR article and later influenced the **earned growth rate** metric designed to correct its gaming problems.[^s1][^s2]
