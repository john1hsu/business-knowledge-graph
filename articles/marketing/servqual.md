# SERVQUAL

**SERVQUAL** is a standardized questionnaire for measuring how customers judge the quality of a service. Developed by A. Parasuraman, Valarie Zeithaml and Leonard Berry and published in the *Journal of Retailing* in 1988, it defines perceived service quality as the direction and size of the gap between a customer's expectations and their perceptions of a firm's actual performance.[^s1] It became one of the most widely adopted tools for measuring and managing service quality, and also one of the most debated.[^s5]

## Origin

The instrument grew out of the authors' 1985 *Journal of Marketing* article, which observed that quality in tangible goods had been well described while service quality was largely undefined, and which built a conceptual model of service quality from an exploratory study of four service businesses.[^s2] That qualitative work used twelve focus groups and found consumers judged service on ten criteria, including reliability, responsiveness, competence, courtesy, credibility, security, access, communication, understanding the customer, and tangibles.[^s1] The focus groups also supported the core idea that quality is assessed by comparing expectations with perceived performance.[^s1]

## How it works

Scale development began with a pool of 97 items, which were purified first to 34 and then to the final 22 statements, using samples of customers of banking, credit card, appliance repair, long-distance telephone and securities brokerage services.[^s1] The ten original criteria collapsed into five dimensions, with assurance and empathy absorbing items from seven of the original criteria:[^s1]

- **Tangibles**: the physical facilities, equipment and appearance of staff.[^s1]
- **Reliability**: performing the promised service dependably and accurately.[^s1]
- **Responsiveness**: willingness to help customers and to provide prompt service.[^s1]
- **Assurance**: employees' knowledge and courtesy and their ability to inspire trust and confidence.[^s1]
- **Empathy**: caring, individualized attention to each customer.[^s1]

Each statement appears twice, once to capture the customer's expectations of firms in that service category and once to capture perceptions of the specific firm, rated on a seven-point agree–disagree scale. The item score is perception minus expectation, and dimension and overall scores average those differences, so a negative score signals a shortfall.[^s1] When the authors regressed overall quality ratings on the five dimension scores, reliability was the most important dimension at all four firms studied, assurance came second, and empathy ranked last.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

SERVQUAL attracted strong criticism within a few years. Cronin and Taylor argued that the expectation-minus-perception formulation blends satisfaction with attitude, and found that a performance-only measure (later called SERVPERF) captured service quality better; they also concluded that service quality is an antecedent of customer satisfaction, and that satisfaction had the stronger effect on purchase intentions.[^s3] Babakus and Boller found that the five-factor structure did not hold up cleanly and that the number of dimensions may depend on the type of service, and they questioned both the mixed positive and negative wording of items and the reliance on difference scores.[^s4] Buttle's 1996 review organized these into theoretical and operational objections and set out a research agenda, while noting how widely the tool had already spread.[^s5] The original authors themselves warned that the scale is limited to current or past customers and that some items relevant to a particular firm may have been dropped during item reduction.[^s1]

## In practice

Parasuraman and colleagues positioned SERVQUAL as a diagnostic best run periodically to track trends, ideally paired with employee surveys and complaint data. They suggested using it to rank dimensions by importance, segment customers into high, medium and low perceived-quality groups, compare branches or stores in a chain, and benchmark against competitors by adding perception statements about rival firms.[^s1] They designed it to work across many services and expected users to reword or add items for a specific setting.[^s1]

Healthcare is a common application. In a 2014 survey of 306 outpatients at a hospital in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, researchers used an Arabic, five-point version of the 22 items and found that expectations exceeded perceptions on every dimension. Tangibles showed the largest gap and empathy the smallest, and the single worst item concerned being seen promptly at the appointment time.[^s6]

## Connections

SERVQUAL is the best-known way of measuring **service quality**, a central construct in **services marketing**.[^s5] It was built from the **gaps model of service quality** set out in 1985, and it operationalizes that model's customer-facing gap between expectations and perceptions.[^s2][^s1] It was introduced in the 1988 *Journal of Retailing* paper by **A. Parasuraman** and co-authors.[^s1] **SERVPERF**, the performance-only scale, is its main alternative, and the debate between them also shaped how researchers relate service quality to **customer satisfaction**.[^s3] The competitor-comparison use makes it a **benchmarking** tool, and grouping customers by perceived quality links it to **segmentation, targeting and positioning**.[^s1]
