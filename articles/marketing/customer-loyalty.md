# Customer Loyalty

Customer loyalty is a customer's tendency to keep buying from the same brand, store or supplier rather than switching to rivals. Textbook treatments describe it as an ongoing positive relationship that produces repeat purchases over competitors' offerings, and stress that keeping a loyal customer is usually cheaper and easier than winning a new one.[^s5] Academic work refines this by insisting that loyalty is more than repeat buying: Alan Dick and Kunal Basu defined it as the strength of the link between a customer's relative attitude toward a brand and their repeat patronage, a link moderated by social norms and situational factors.[^s1]

## Behavioural and attitudinal loyalty

Dick and Basu's framework separates two dimensions. Repeat patronage is the behavioural side; relative attitude, built from cognitive, affective and conative (intention) components, is the attitudinal side. Loyalty in their view yields motivational, perceptual and behavioural consequences, such as resistance to competitors' messages.[^s1] Crossing the two dimensions separates customers who repurchase out of genuine preference from those who repurchase out of habit, convenience or lack of alternatives; Reinartz and Kumar later found that purchase behaviour alone is a misleading indicator of who will actually advocate for a firm.[^s4]

Richard Oliver sharpened the attitudinal view, defining loyalty as a deeply held commitment to rebuy a preferred product consistently despite situational influences and marketing efforts that could cause switching.[^s2] He argued that loyalty develops in four phases: cognitive (based on information about price and features, and the shallowest), affective (liking built from satisfying use), conative (a committed intention to rebuy) and action (intention converted into habitual repurchase that overcomes obstacles). Each phase has its own vulnerabilities, from a better price at the cognitive stage to simple unavailability at the action stage.[^s2]

## Satisfaction, switching costs and loyalty

Satisfaction and loyalty are related but distinct. Oliver treats satisfaction as a necessary early step toward loyalty that matters less once loyalty is anchored by personal determination and social support; dissatisfaction remains the main opening competitors exploit.[^s2] He also lists obstacles that make loyalty unattainable for many firms, including variety seeking, multibrand buying, changing needs, and rivals' incentives such as price cuts, coupons and free trials.[^s2] Behavioural loyalty can therefore rise because switching is costly rather than because customers are delighted, which is why Dick and Basu stress that situational factors moderate the attitude–patronage link.[^s1]

## The profitability case and its critics

The managerial enthusiasm for loyalty owes much to Frederick Reichheld and W. Earl Sasser's 1990 Harvard Business Review article, which argued that service firms could raise profits by almost 100% by retaining 5% more customers, and that defection rates are a leading indicator of profit.[^s3] Their example was MBNA, a credit card issuer that from 1982 studied every defecting customer; by 1990 its defection rate was about 5%, roughly half the industry average, and its profits had grown sixteenfold.[^s3]

Later research challenged the generality of that claim. Werner Reinartz and V. Kumar analysed more than 16,000 customers at four companies over four years and found only weak to moderate correlations between loyalty and profitability, ranging from 0.20 at a mail-order firm to 0.45 at a French grocer.[^s4] Long-tenure customers were not consistently cheaper to serve and often paid less, not more; only customers who were loyal both in behaviour and attitude were markedly more likely to recommend the firm.[^s4] They proposed segmenting customers by profitability and expected longevity (strangers, butterflies, barnacles and true friends) and managing loyalty and profit together.[^s4]

A second critique comes from empirical marketing science. The "double jeopardy" law, identified by William McPhee and developed by Andrew Ehrenberg, shows that small brands have fewer buyers and that those buyers are also slightly less loyal; behavioural loyalty varies little across brands of different sizes.[^s6] The implication is that brands grow mainly by increasing penetration rather than by deepening the loyalty of existing buyers, which tempers expectations for loyalty-led growth.[^s6]

## In practice

Firms try to build loyalty through service quality, personalisation and loyalty programs that reward repeat purchases, such as frequent-flyer schemes and coffee-shop rewards cards, often run through CRM systems that track members and trigger retention messages.[^s5] Starbucks is a prominent example: in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025 it reported 34.2 million 90-day active Starbucks Rewards members in the U.S., whose spending accounted for 58% of U.S. tender dollars.[^s7] Such figures show the behavioural reach of a program, but, per the critiques above, they do not by themselves show attitudinal commitment or higher profitability per customer.[^s4]

## Connections

Customer loyalty sits within [[kt:marketing]] and is the central objective of [[kt:relationship-marketing]], which shifts attention from single transactions to long-term relationships.[^s5] It `dependsOn` [[kt:customer-satisfaction]], which Oliver treats as a necessary but insufficient precursor,[^s2] and on [[kt:switching-costs]], which can sustain behavioural loyalty without attitudinal commitment.[^s1] It is `measuredBy` [[kt:churn-rate]], its behavioural inverse,[^s3] and by [[kt:net-promoter-score]], a recommendation-based proxy rooted in Reichheld's work on defections.[^s3] It feeds [[kt:customer-lifetime-value]], whose retention assumptions Reinartz and Kumar caution against overstating,[^s4] and is pursued operationally through the [[kt:loyalty-program]].[^s5]
