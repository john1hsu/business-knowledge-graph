# Marketing

Marketing is the part of business administration concerned with understanding what customers need and organising the firm to satisfy those needs at a profit. The American Marketing Association's widely taught definition frames it as the activity, institutions and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society at large.[^s1] Britannica stresses its economic core: marketing directs the flow of goods and services from producers to consumers, and its central purpose is to promote and facilitate exchange.[^s2]

## Exchange and value

An exchange requires at least two parties, each with something the other values, the ability to communicate and deliver, and the freedom to accept or refuse; voluntariness is what separates marketing from self-production, begging, theft or force.[^s2] Exchanges are usually money for goods or services but can be non-monetary, as when a volunteer gives time in return for a T-shirt.[^s3] OpenStax describes customer value as the ratio of benefits received to the sacrifices made to obtain them, so price is only one part of the judgement customers make.[^s3] Marketing is therefore broader than selling: selling is the act of completing a transaction, while marketing works earlier to shape what is offered and to communicate its value so that it sells.[^s3]

## The marketing process

Introductory texts present marketing as a five-step process: understand the marketplace and customers; design a customer-driven strategy; deliver superior value; build profitable relationships through acquisition, retention and repeat purchase; and capture value in return, measured as customer equity, the potential profit from current and prospective customers.[^s1] Marketing also creates form, time, place and possession utility, and it addresses internal audiences (employees, owners, investors) as well as external ones (customers, suppliers, distributors, government and society).[^s1]

The organising philosophy behind the process is the *marketing concept*: research customer needs first, then integrate production, promotion and service around satisfying them while meeting long-term organisational goals, rather than making a product and then asking marketing to sell it.[^s3] Britannica notes that marketing can be described at several levels at once: a set of functions, a department, a managerial process, a management philosophy and a social process.[^s2]

## Origins and development

Marketing emerged as a field in the early 20th century as an offshoot of economics, which had paid little attention to middlemen and to non-price influences on demand.[^s2] Early marketing economists studied agricultural and industrial markets through commodity analysis (how a product reaches market), institutional analysis (the wholesalers, retailers and other firms involved) and functional analysis (tasks such as transport, which many institutions can perform).[^s2] During the century large manufacturers came to treat market research, product design, distribution and consumer communication as keys to brand success, and the approach spread to industrial goods and services.[^s2]

A landmark in the field's scope was Philip Kotler and Sidney Levy's 1969 *Journal of Marketing* article "Broadening the Concept of Marketing," which argued that marketing is a pervasive societal activity and asked whether its principles of product development, pricing, distribution and promotion apply to non-business organisations, people and ideas.[^s4] Britannica records the outcome: hospitals, schools, museums, places, personalities, events and ideas are now routinely marketed.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest quantitative evidence that the marketing philosophy pays comes from research on *market orientation*, the organisational form of the marketing concept. A meta-analysis by Kirca, Jayachandran and Bearden pooled 418 effects from 130 samples in 114 studies (published through June 2004) and found a corrected mean correlation of 0.32 between market orientation and performance, stronger for overall business performance than for profit or sales.[^s5] The link was partly mediated by innovativeness, customer loyalty and quality, was stronger in manufacturing than in service firms, and was stronger in low power-distance and low uncertainty-avoidance cultures.[^s5] The authors caution that the evidence is correlational, dominated by US studies, and stronger when performance is self-reported.[^s5]

Social critics charge that marketing raises prices through promotion, distribution costs and prestige margins, uses deceptive or high-pressure tactics, and fosters materialism and "false wants," with children's advertising a frequent target; defenders reply that brands carry symbolic and quality-signalling value, that harmful practices are poor marketing rather than marketing itself, and that regulation and self-regulation curb abuses.[^s6] The growth of societal marketing, which balances profit, customer satisfaction and long-term public interest, is partly a response to these criticisms.[^s6]

## In practice

OpenStax illustrates the customer-driven approach with Enterprise Rent-A-Car, which delivers cars to customers' homes or workplaces, and with Southwest Airlines, whose budget model is built around on-time departures that customers value as much as low fares.[^s3] Relationship programmes such as airline frequent-flyer schemes reflect the economics of retention; the same text reports that a 5 percent increase in customer retention can raise profits by 25 to 95 percent.[^s3]

## Connections

Marketing sits under **business administration** alongside finance and operations, and grew out of **microeconomics** as economists turned to intermediaries and non-price demand.[^s2] Its core managerial activities include **market research**, which identifies the needs that marketing then anticipates and satisfies,[^s1] and **marketing strategy**, where segmentation, targeting, positioning and the marketing mix are chosen. **Market orientation** is the organisational embodiment of the marketing concept whose performance effects are summarised above.[^s5] **Relationship marketing** extends the field's focus from single transactions to long-term customer partnerships,[^s3] and **services marketing** is a sub-field that adapts marketing principles to intangible offerings, part of the broadening Kotler and Levy called for.[^s4]
