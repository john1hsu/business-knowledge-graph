# Gaps Model of Service Quality

The **gaps model of service quality** is a framework for diagnosing why customers judge a service as poor. It treats quality as the distance between the service a customer expected and the service they believe they received, and traces that distance back to four breakdowns inside the provider.[^s1][^s3] The model was set out by A. Parasuraman, Valarie Zeithaml and Leonard Berry in a 1985 *Journal of Marketing* article, which argued that while quality in physical goods had been described and measured, service quality had been left largely undefined.[^s1] OpenStax's marketing text also calls it the Customer Service Gap Model or the Five-Gap Model.[^s3]

## Origin

The authors built the model from an exploratory study of four service businesses, combining what managers and customers told them into a single picture of where quality is won or lost.[^s1] A 1988 follow-up in the same journal extended it inward: Zeithaml, Berry and Parasuraman catalogued organizational factors that widen or narrow the four provider-side gaps, most of them tied to the communication and control systems used to manage staff, such as how clear front-line roles are and how much those roles conflict.[^s2] They drew on organizational-behavior research as well as marketing to do this.[^s2]

## How it works

In the original labels, the five gaps are:[^s6]

- **Gap 1 (knowledge or listening gap):** customer expectations versus what management believes customers expect.[^s6][^s5]
- **Gap 2 (standards or policy gap):** management's perceptions versus the service-quality specifications it actually sets.[^s6][^s3]
- **Gap 3 (delivery or performance gap):** those specifications versus the service actually delivered.[^s6][^s5]
- **Gap 4 (communication gap):** the service delivered versus what external communications promised.[^s6][^s3]
- **Gap 5 (customer gap):** expected service versus perceived service.[^s6][^s5]

Gaps 1 to 4 sit within the firm's control, while Gap 5 is the customer's verdict; the logic of the model is that the customer gap closes only when the provider gaps do.[^s6][^s5] The textbook illustration is a hotel. A manager who assumes guests want a hot breakfast when they care more about clean rooms and fast internet has a knowledge gap. A rule that phones be answered "quickly" is too vague and creates a standards gap. Staff who let phones ring past a clear two-ring rule create a delivery gap. Advertising a feature the business cannot supply creates a communication gap.[^s3] Typical causes of a delivery gap include weak training, staff who lack the needed skills or motivation, and understaffing.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Most empirical work on the model has gone into measuring Gap 5, through the SERVQUAL instrument the same authors developed, so criticism of that instrument has shaped how the model is used. Carman argued that SERVQUAL's dimensions do not carry over to every industry, and Cronin and Taylor argued that the expectations side may be unnecessary. The original authors responded in 1994 by splitting expectations into desired and adequate levels, with a "zone of tolerance" between them.[^s6] A 2005 review of 19 service-quality models concluded that how quality should be measured depends on the setting, timing and customer needs, and that expectations shift with repeat encounters and competition, which complicates any fixed expectations benchmark.[^s7] Bitner, Zeithaml and Gremler acknowledged in 2010 that the model was conceived when most services were delivered face to face with little technology; they still present it as a foundational framework for service science, but argue that technology now changes how firms close each gap.[^s4]

## In practice

The model has been applied across industries and countries for more than two decades as a way to plan service strategy and to align marketing, operations and human resources around the customer.[^s4] In a 2007 interview, Mary Jo Bitner of Arizona State University's Center for Services Leadership gave examples by gap. IKEA used customer focus groups to design a Chicago store, addressing the listening gap. A pizza chain's 30-minute delivery promise without the operations to back it illustrates the communication gap.[^s5] OpenStax cites Southwest Airlines as a firm that avoids overpromising and so avoids a gap between what it promises and what it delivers.[^s3]

## Connections

The model is a core framework of **services marketing** and the standard account of **service quality**.[^s4][^s1] It was created by **A. Parasuraman**, **Valarie Zeithaml** and **Leonard Berry** in their **1985 conceptual-model article**.[^s1] **SERVQUAL** grew out of it and is the usual way to measure its customer gap, which in turn ties the model to research on **customer satisfaction**.[^s6][^s3] **Southwest Airlines** is a textbook case of keeping the communication gap closed.[^s3]
