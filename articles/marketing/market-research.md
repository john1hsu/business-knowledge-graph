# Market Research: Primary and Secondary Data for Business Decisions

Market research (often called marketing research) is the disciplined collection and interpretation of information about customers, competitors and market conditions to support marketing decisions. OpenStax's *Principles of Marketing* adopts the American Marketing Association's framing of it as the function linking consumers, customers and the public to the marketer through information, and stresses its purpose: letting managers decide on evidence rather than assumption, from long-range strategy down to tactics such as staffing and inventory timing.[^s1] Much of this information today flows from point-of-sale systems, connected devices, social media and location data, which the textbook groups under "big data" and its three Vs of volume, velocity and variety.[^s1]

## Origin

Organized commercial research is usually traced to 1911, when Curtis Publishing created a Division of Commercial Research inside its advertising department and put Charles Coolidge Parlin in charge.[^s3] Parlin coined the term "commercial research," and his staff interviewed consumers, wholesalers and dealers and even examined pantries and household trash to see which brands people actually bought.[^s3] The Advertising Hall of Fame, which inducted him in 1953, describes the unit as the first marketing research organization in the United States and credits his industry studies of farm implements, department stores and automobiles as without precedent in American business.[^s4] Parlin's goal was partly commercial: the research helped Curtis show advertisers how advertising could move goods through wholesalers and retailers to consumers.[^s4]

## How it works

OpenStax lays out a seven-step plan: define the problem, develop the research plan, choose the data collection method, design the sample, collect the data, analyze and interpret it, and prepare the report.[^s2] Studies fall into three broad types. Exploratory research is mostly qualitative and maps an unfamiliar market; descriptive research, usually a quantitative survey, measures attitudes and behaviors; and causal research uses experiments such as A/B tests of two ads or package designs to isolate cause and effect.[^s2]

The central design choice is between data sources. Secondary data already exists, having been gathered for another purpose (census figures, journal articles, internal sales records, or syndicated data bought by subscription); it is cheap and quick but may be dated, ill-fitting and equally available to rivals.[^s2] Primary data is collected fresh for the question at hand, so it is current and tailored but slower and more costly.[^s2] Primary methods include observation, one-on-one interviews, focus groups in which a moderator guides roughly eight to twelve participants, mail, phone, in-person and electronic surveys, and experiments; each trades depth, cost, speed and response rate differently.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The profession is large and growing. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 952,700 jobs for market research analysts and marketing specialists in 2025, with a May 2025 median wage of $78,760, and projects 7% employment growth from 2025 to 2035; consulting services and finance and insurance are the largest employers.[^s5]

The best-known critique is that conventional research asks the wrong question. Clayton Christensen, Scott Cook and Taddy Hall argued in Harvard Business Review that profiling buyers by demographics and asking them which product attributes they want often yields clear answers that do not change sales.[^s6] In their milkshake case, a chain's panel-driven tweaks to flavor, thickness and price had no effect, while direct observation revealed that about 40% of shakes were bought early in the morning by lone commuters who wanted something filling and slow to finish on a long drive.[^s6] The lesson they draw is that averaging stated preferences across segments can produce a product suited to no one, and that observing the circumstances of purchase can matter more than surveying the purchaser.[^s6] The focus-group format has its own documented weakness: a few dominant participants can sway the group, and its qualitative output is hard to quantify.[^s2]

## In practice

A typical program blends both data types: a firm scans syndicated and internal sales data to size a market, runs focus groups or interviews to generate hypotheses, then fields a survey or a controlled A/B test to measure them before committing to launch.[^s2] LEGO, for example, has studied how children and parents actually play with its products to guide design decisions.[^s1]

## Connections

Market research is a core practice within **marketing** and supplies the data for **segmentation, targeting and positioning**, though Christensen and colleagues warn that demographic segments alone can mislead.[^s6] Their alternative unit of analysis, the job a customer hires a product to do, is the **jobs-to-be-done** lens, and they cite **Procter & Gamble**'s Swiffer as a product built around such a job.[^s6] Analysts also measure the effectiveness of marketing programs, linking the practice to **marketing metrics**.[^s5] The field's founding figure is **Charles Coolidge Parlin**,[^s3] and the **focus group** is one of its standard primary-data tools.[^s2]
