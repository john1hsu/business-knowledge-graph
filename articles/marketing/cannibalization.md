# Cannibalization

**Cannibalization** is the loss of sales, revenue or profit that a firm's existing products suffer when the same firm introduces a new product, line extension, brand or sales channel that wins over their customers.[^s1][^s4] The sales are not lost to a rival; they are shifted inside the firm. Marketers therefore judge a launch by its *incremental* contribution — the new product's sales minus what it takes from the parent — rather than by its gross volume.[^s1] The idea matters in three recurring decisions: extending a product line, adding a channel such as online retail, and adopting a new technology that will make the current one obsolete.[^s1][^s2][^s4]

## Origin

The concept comes from marketing management, where it was first applied to brand and line extensions that risk stealing share from their parent brand; by the 1990s researchers had turned to measuring it.[^s1][^s4] Wendy Lomax of Kingston Business School, writing in *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* in 1996, defined cannibalization and compared three ways managers could quantify it.[^s1] In parallel, innovation scholars reframed it as a strategic barrier: Rajesh Chandy and Gerard Tellis argued in 1998 that incumbents' *willingness to cannibalize* their own past investments shapes whether they lead with radical innovations, a theme they extended in their 2000 *Journal of Marketing* study of incumbency and firm size.[^s5]

## How it works

At the product level, cannibalization depends on how close a substitute the new offering is for the old one; the closer the substitute, the larger the share of the new item's buyers who would otherwise have bought the parent.[^s1][^s4] Lomax tested three measurement techniques — gains-loss analysis, duplication-of-purchase tables, and deviations from expected share movements — on consumer panel data for three detergent line extensions in the UK and Germany.[^s1] All three extensions cannibalized their parent brand, and because the effect changes over time she advised managers to combine methods and measure repeatedly rather than rely on one snapshot.[^s1]

At the strategic level the mechanism is about incentives. An incumbent that launches a new technology mostly replaces its own profit stream, so its net gain is modest, whereas a start-up has nothing to lose and captures the full gain.[^s3] Economic theory pits this cannibalization effect, which slows incumbents, against *preemption*, the motive to launch first so that rivals cannot take the new market, which speeds them up.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Mitsuru Igami's structural model of hard disk drive makers, published in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2017, offers the strongest quantitative evidence. Despite strong preemptive motives and a cost advantage over entrants, cannibalization explained at least 57% of the gap between incumbent and entrant innovation.[^s2] Igami reads this as rational behaviour by incumbents rather than the managerial blindness Clayton Christensen emphasized, and he notes that shareholders who prefer existing profits can put owners and managers at odds.[^s3]

The fear of cannibalization can also be overstated. Deleersnyder, Geyskens, Gielens and Dekimpe studied 85 internet-channel additions by British and Dutch newspapers over roughly a decade and found that only 5 of 67 UK papers showed a significant slowdown in circulation growth, while a meta-analysis found no significant collective effect.[^s4] Papers whose websites closely copied the print edition were far more likely to suffer losses (78% average content overlap, versus 45% for those without losses), suggesting that differentiation, not abstention, is the remedy.[^s4] Chandy and Tellis likewise found that, after World War II, large incumbents introduced most radical innovations in their sample, so the "incumbent's curse" is not inevitable.[^s5]

## In practice

Managers treat cannibalization as a design variable. Before a line extension, they estimate how many buyers will come from the parent brand and position the newcomer on a different price point, benefit or segment to limit overlap.[^s1] When adding a channel, they differentiate the content or service, as the newspaper evidence implies.[^s4] For technology shifts, the prescription runs the other way: Howard Yu and Thomas Malnight argued in *HBR* in 2016 that self-cannibalization is the only way to fend off start-up competitors, and Igami's advice to executives was blunt — "destroy yourself."[^s6][^s3] Kodak, Borders and Nokia are commonly cited as firms that hesitated.[^s3]

## Connections

Cannibalization is a core input to **product line strategy** and **new product development**, where it decides whether a launch adds net value.[^s1] It is the economic mechanism behind **The Innovator's Dilemma** and a driver of incumbent lag in **disruptive innovation** and **creative destruction**, as Igami's hard disk drive study shows.[^s2][^s3] **Organizational ambidexterity** and a deliberate willingness to cannibalize are the organisational responses.[^s5][^s6] Its size is captured by the **cross-price elasticity** between old and new offerings, and it arises in **line extensions** and **multichannel retailing**.[^s1][^s4] The same tension appears in **freemium** and **SaaS** models, where a free tier or subscription can eat into paid or licence revenue.
