# Customer Relationship Management (CRM): Building Profitable Long-Term Customer Relationships

**Customer relationship management (CRM)** is the set of processes and systems a firm uses to track, manage and analyse its interactions with customers so it can win, keep and grow the relationships worth having. OpenStax's *Principles of Marketing* describes CRM at the working level as the means by which companies follow and interpret customer interactions, with software that stores linked records of contacts, calls, emails, purchases and returns.[^s1] Marketing scholars push for a broader view: Adrian Payne and Pennie Frow, writing in the *Journal of Marketing* in 2005, defined CRM as a strategic approach that turns customer insight into both customer value and shareholder value, and warned that treating it as a software purchase is too narrow.[^s2]

## Origin and definitions

CRM has no single agreed definition. A University of Reading review finds that the literature treats it variously as a business strategy, as the management of customer life-cycle activities, or simply as information technology.[^s5] The review argues that the purely technological reading is a shortfall and adopts Francis Buttle's framing of CRM strategy as a plan that aligns people, processes and technology behind customer-related goals.[^s5] Payne and Frow's framework names three alternative perspectives on CRM, from narrow technology projects up to enterprise-wide customer strategy, and argues for the strategic end.[^s2]

## How it works

Payne and Frow organise strategic CRM into five cross-functional processes: strategy development, value creation, multichannel integration, information management and performance assessment.[^s2] Each runs across departments rather than sitting inside marketing, sales or IT.[^s2] Werner Reinartz, Manfred Krafft and Wayne Hoyer offered a complementary lifecycle view in the *Journal of Marketing Research* (2004): CRM activity falls into relationship **initiation**, **maintenance** and **termination**, so a firm deliberately ends or downgrades unprofitable relationships as well as nurturing valuable ones.[^s3]

On the systems side, the OpenStax text sorts CRM software into three families.[^s1] **Operational** CRM automates marketing, sales and service workflows that turn leads into customers. **Analytical** CRM collects and models customer data to guide market and strategic decisions. **Collaborative** CRM shares customer data across organisations to produce insight that none could reach alone.[^s1] Typical uses include triggered offers on birthdays or purchase anniversaries, automated thank-you and survey messages, purchase-history targeting, review requests and loyalty-programme management.[^s1] Some authors add a fourth, *strategic* CRM category above these three.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence on CRM payoffs is mixed. Reinartz, Krafft and Hoyer surveyed firms in four industries and three countries and found that implementing CRM processes had a moderate positive association with both perceived and objective company performance.[^s3] That is a real effect, but not the dramatic gain vendors often promise.

Practitioners have long reported high failure rates. In a February 2002 *Harvard Business Review* article, Bain's Darrell Rigby, Fred Reichheld and Phil Schefter reported that 55% of CRM projects failed to deliver expected results, some even pushing customers away and diluting earnings. This came as CRM software spending was projected to grow from $20 billion in 2001 to $46 billion in 2003.[^s4] Their diagnosis was that firms rolled out software before settling on a customer strategy, when technology should simply support that strategy.[^s4] The Reading review lists similar failure causes in later research: no long-term CRM vision, strategy shaped around vendor tools, and too little user involvement before implementation.[^s5] A deeper critique is that CRM can become relationship management *of* customers, not *with* them, optimising contact frequency and data capture at the cost of trust. Payne and Frow's insistence on value creation for the customer partly answers this.[^s2]

## In practice

The OpenStax text describes everyday CRM in airline frequent-flyer schemes, Starbucks and DSW loyalty programmes, and Amazon's post-purchase review requests. In each, interaction data feeds back into targeted offers and product decisions.[^s1] The same text names Salesforce as the leading CRM software vendor across several world regions as of its 2023 edition. It also reports that a large majority of customers say loyalty programmes keep them with a brand, though it gives no date or source for that figure.[^s1] Rigby and colleagues drew their recommendations from firms including GE Capital, BMC Software and the New York Times Company. Their advice is to define which customers to serve and how before choosing technology.[^s4]

## Connections

CRM is the operational form of **relationship marketing** and sits within **marketing strategy**. Its focus on the whole relationship rather than the individual offer is why it contrasts with the **marketing mix**, and Payne and Frow place it at the level of company strategy rather than campaign tactics.[^s2] Its results are judged through **customer lifetime value** and **churn rate**, since initiating, maintaining and ending relationships is an exercise in managing customer profitability over time.[^s3] It builds on **customer satisfaction** and **loyalty programmes** to keep customers, and uses **segmentation, targeting and positioning** to decide which relationships deserve investment.[^s1] Its retention aims link it to **switching costs**, and it depends on **customer data analytics**, the analytical CRM layer that turns interaction records into decisions.[^s1] Enterprise suites often pair CRM with **enterprise resource planning**, which handles the back-office side of the same customer orders.[^s5]
