# Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning

**Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP)** is the core sequence of strategic marketing. A firm first splits a heterogeneous market into segments whose members share needs or respond similarly to marketing actions, then selects the segment or segments it will serve (its target market), and finally positions its offering so that those customers see it as distinct from competing products.[^s1][^s2] Marketing scholars treat segmentation and positioning as interdependent decisions rather than independent steps: the best segment depends on the positioning a firm can credibly hold, and vice versa.[^s1]

## Origin

The segmentation idea is usually traced to Wendell R. Smith's July 1956 *Journal of Marketing* article, "Product Differentiation and Market Segmentation as Alternative Marketing Strategies," which argued that products would find their best markets by recognising differences in the requirements of market segments rather than treating demand as one mass market.[^s6] Wind and Bell note that segmentation has been regarded as a key marketing concept since the early 1960s and that its basic definition has changed little since then.[^s1] Positioning came from advertising practice: Al Ries and Jack Trout first published the idea in *Industrial Marketing* in June 1969, popularised it in a three-part *Advertising Age* series in 1972, and expanded it in the 1981 book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*.[^s5]

## How it works

**Segmentation.** A useful segmentation basis is one that explains differences in how customers buy or use the product, such as usage patterns, benefits sought, or response to price promotions and loyalty programmes. Wind and Bell distinguish these *bases* from *descriptors* (demographics, firmographics) used to find and reach segments, and caution that age, sex or generic psychographics are often weak bases because they do not reliably predict response to marketing. Segments should be measurable, substantial, accessible and actionable.[^s1]

**Targeting.** Firms then evaluate segments on attractiveness, likely responsiveness, fit with the firm's competencies, cost of reaching them and the strength of competition, and manage the chosen set as a portfolio.[^s1] Targeting choices range from undifferentiated mass marketing, which sells one offering to everyone, to differentiated marketing that tailors product, price or promotion to selected groups, and to one-to-one marketing built around individual customer relationships.[^s2]

**Positioning.** Positioning is how buyers perceive a product relative to rivals. Marketers commonly use a two-dimensional perceptual map built on attributes buyers care about, such as price and quality, to look for open space away from clusters of competitors; repositioning moves an existing product to a new place in customers' minds, often to reach a different segment.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Critics argue that much segmentation research is too narrow, treats segment membership as static and deterministic, and is poorly linked to actual strategy; responses include overlapping and sub-segmentation, probabilistic membership models, and tying segments to sales-force and portfolio decisions.[^s1] In a 2006 *Harvard Business Review* article, Daniel Yankelovich and David Meer argued that demographic segmentation had become too weak to guide strategy and that psychographic segments, often reduced to advertising personas, predicted purchase behaviour little better. They urged firms to separate segmentations meant to build brand identity from those meant to decide which markets to enter and which products to make.[^s4] Information technology has also pushed practice toward ever finer segments, down to "segments of one," blurring the line between targeting and personalisation.[^s1]

## In practice

The classic contrast is the US car industry. Henry Ford's identical, black-only Model T exemplified mass marketing, and by 1918 half of America's cars were Model Ts. General Motors under Alfred P. Sloan instead segmented buyers by price and income and offered a car for each target market, and GM overtook Ford as the leading US automaker.[^s2] A later example is ConAgra's Healthy Choice frozen-dinner line, launched in the late 1980s for health-conscious buyers, which the textbook reports reached about 10 percent of the frozen-dinner market within two years.[^s2] On the positioning side, Wendy's tagline "It's better than fast food" sought to set the chain apart from McDonald's and Burger King.[^s3]

## Connections

STP sits under **marketing strategy** and is the step that precedes designing the **marketing mix**: once a target and position are chosen, product, price, place and promotion are set to deliver them.[^s1] Its intellectual root is Smith's 1956 segmentation article, and **positioning** is its final component, developed by Ries and Trout.[^s6][^s5] It contrasts with **mass marketing**, the approach that grew alongside **mass production** and is exemplified by the **Ford Model T**, while **General Motors** under Sloan is the textbook example of segment-by-segment targeting.[^s2] Well-defined segments also underpin later metrics and assets such as **customer lifetime value** and **brand equity**, since both are measured and built segment by segment.[^s1][^s4]
