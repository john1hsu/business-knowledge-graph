# Marketing Mix: The Four Ps of Product, Price, Place and Promotion

The **marketing mix** is the combination of controllable tactics a firm uses to reach a target market and persuade it to buy. In most textbooks it is taught as the **4Ps**: the product offered, the price charged, the place (channels) through which it reaches customers, and the promotion that makes buyers aware of it and persuades them to purchase.[^s1] The framework is the operational half of marketing strategy: once a firm has chosen whom to serve and how to position itself, the mix is how it delivers on that choice.[^s1]

## Origin

Neil H. Borden of Harvard Business School popularised the term. He credited Professor James Culliton's 1948 study of marketing costs, which pictured the marketing executive as a blender of ingredients who sometimes follows a recipe and sometimes improvises; Borden began using "marketing mix" in his teaching roughly fifteen years before writing his best-known account of it.[^s2] That essay, "The Concept of the Marketing Mix," was prepared for George Schwartz's 1964 volume *Science in Marketing* and later reprinted by the *Journal of Advertising Research* (1984).[^s2] Borden listed twelve ingredients: product planning, pricing, branding, channels of distribution, personal selling, advertising, promotions, packaging, display, servicing, physical handling, and fact finding and analysis. He paired them with four market forces that shape the right blend: consumers, the trade, competitors and government.[^s2] E. Jerome McCarthy then condensed Borden's list into the four Ps, which became the dominant teaching framework.[^s3][^s4]

## How it works

Each P is a decision area. **Product** covers the features, quality and variants customers want, informed by demand and competitor analysis. **Price** must reflect real or perceived benefits while covering costs, landing between the lowest profitable price and the highest price that does not drive customers away. **Place** concerns how the offering gets from the firm to the customer's location, through retail, wholesale or online channels. **Promotion** spans advertising, public relations, social media, trade shows and search or email marketing.[^s1] Borden stressed that the elements are interdependent, so changing one (say, cutting price) alters what the others must do, and that advertising should be judged as part of the whole programme rather than in isolation. He also argued that no single formula fits all firms: his cost studies found no common spending standards even among manufacturers in the same field.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The 4Ps are descriptive rather than empirically derived, and scholars have long questioned the classification. Van Waterschoot and Van den Bulte, writing in the *Journal of Marketing* in 1992, noted the scheme's wide acceptance while reviewing the growing criticism of how its categories are defined.[^s4] Constantinides' 2006 review of criticism across consumer, relationship, services, retail, industrial and electronic marketing found two weaknesses common to every area: the mix is internally oriented, reflecting the seller's view rather than customer needs, and it has no room for personalisation because it was built for mass markets.[^s3] Services researchers responded by adding elements; Booms and Bitner (1981) proposed three more Ps (participants or people, physical evidence and process), giving the "7Ps" used for services.[^s3] In business-to-business markets, Ettenson, Conrado and Knowles argued in *Harvard Business Review* (2013) that the 4Ps push sellers toward narrow, product-focused strategies when customers want solutions.[^s5]

## In practice

OpenStax illustrates the mix with a backpack maker: deciding which pockets and straps to include (product), setting a price that covers costs and fits perceived value (price), moving stock from warehouse to retail and online channels (place), and choosing advertising outlets matched to buyer demographics (promotion). It also cites a 2022 collaboration in which Netflix and JanSport released *Stranger Things*-branded backpacks timed to that year's season launch, an example of using a product partnership as promotion.[^s1]

## Connections

The mix sits under **marketing strategy** and follows **segmentation, targeting and positioning**: the chosen segment and position determine what product, price, place and promotion must deliver.[^s1] Its price lever links to **pricing strategy**.[^s1] The concept was **created by Neil Borden** and set out in **"The Concept of the Marketing Mix" (1964)**.[^s2] Its gaps in services prompted the extended 7Ps of **services marketing**, and it **contrasts with relationship marketing**, whose critics see the 4Ps as product-focused, one-way and acquisition-oriented rather than collaborative.[^s3] B2B critics make a similar case for solution-centred marketing.[^s5]
