# Pricing Strategy

A **pricing strategy** is the logic a firm uses to set and change the prices of its offerings so that they serve its business objectives. OpenStax's *Principles of Marketing* frames the task as a five-step procedure: set pricing objectives, estimate demand, estimate costs, scan the external environment (using PESTLE), and only then choose specific strategies and tactics that fit the marketing mix and create customer value.[^s1] Price is the one element of the mix that directly produces revenue, so the choice of reference point matters as much as the number itself.

## Origin and the three reference points

Across industries and countries, pricing practice clusters around three anchors.[^s5] **Cost-based** pricing adds a margin to unit cost; OpenStax's example marks up a smartphone costing $3,000 by 10% to sell at $3,300, and warns that buyers may not see such a price as fair value.[^s1] **Competition-based** pricing sets price relative to rivals, as when Amazon prices popular items just below competitors.[^s1] **Customer value-based** pricing sets price from the benefits customers perceive; OpenStax notes that GoToMeeting chose a $49 flat rate only after dozens of customer interviews.[^s1] Andreas Hinterhuber, averaging roughly two dozen published studies from 1983 to 2006 covering the U.S., Europe and Asia, found that about 44% of firms priced off the market or competition, 37% off cost, and only 17% off customer value.[^s6]

## How it works: strategies and tactics

Within those anchors, firms choose strategies for particular situations.[^s2]

- **Price skimming:** launch high and cut price as rivals arrive, as with game consoles and early flat-screen TVs.[^s2]
- **Penetration pricing:** launch low to win volume and pull buyers from rivals; it typically requires mass-production scale.[^s2]
- **Leader pricing:** sell items such as milk or rotisserie chickens at or below cost to draw shoppers who buy higher-margin goods.[^s2]
- **Bundling:** sell related products together at one combined price, such as a washer and dryer.[^s2]
- **Psychological and prestige pricing:** odd prices like $49.95 signal a bargain, while high prices can signal quality and status.[^s2][^s1]
- **Dynamic pricing:** algorithms raise prices as demand rises; Amazon's price increases on generators and water before the 2017 hurricanes drew scrutiny.[^s2]

Objectives shape the choice. A firm may price for sales volume, market share, a target return on investment, or let buyers set price through auctions and bids.[^s1] Demand conditions matter too: availability of substitutes, buyers' income, time to adjust and cross-elasticity all determine how far price can move before volume reacts.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Pricing scholars generally argue that value-based pricing is the strongest approach, particularly for new products and in competitive markets, yet it remains the least used.[^s5][^s6] Hinterhuber's focus groups traced the gap to five obstacles: difficulty assessing customer value, difficulty communicating it, weak needs-based segmentation, a sales force that leaks value through discounts, and lack of senior-management support.[^s6] He recommended limiting discount authority and aligning sales compensation, training and monitoring with price realization.[^s6]

Empirical work also questions the textbook choice between skimming and penetration. Spann, Fischer and Tellis studied 663 digital cameras from 79 brands in a large European market and found that skimming and penetration each described about 20% of launch paths, while three variants of plain market pricing accounted for about 60%.[^s3] Skimmers launched about 16% above the market price and penetrators about 18% below.[^s3] Firms mixed strategies across their portfolios; late entrants and crowded markets leaned toward market pricing, established brands toward skimming, and firms with more cumulative sales toward penetration.[^s4] Tellis concluded that market conditions often leave little room for strategic pricing.[^s4] The lesson is that a pricing strategy is constrained by competitive structure and capabilities, not chosen freely.

## In practice

The MIT Sloan Management Review authors describe a large European supermarket chain that priced a new private-label yogurt at €1.99 from cost accounting: its goods cost €1.29 and the leading brand sold for €2.99.[^s5] They argue the chain should instead have started from what shoppers perceived the product to be worth, since a cost-plus anchor can leave money on the table or misjudge demand.[^s5] The case shows the usual sequence in practice: cost sets a floor, competitors' prices set a reference, and customer value sets the ceiling a strategy tries to approach.[^s1][^s5]

## Connections

Pricing strategy is the price element of the **marketing mix**.[^s1] It depends on **price elasticity** to predict how volume responds to price, and it relies on **segmentation**, since Hinterhuber found poor needs-based segmentation to be a key barrier to value pricing and since charging segments differently shades into **price discrimination**.[^s1][^s6] Its main variants, **value-based pricing**, **cost-plus pricing**, **price skimming** and **penetration pricing**, are each candidate nodes in their own right.[^s2][^s3] Cost-based pricing is only as good as the cost data behind it, which links it to **activity-based costing**, and the price a firm can sustain feeds **customer lifetime value**.[^s1] Business models such as **razor-and-blades** and **freemium** are pricing strategies built into the model itself, pricing one element low to monetize another.[^s2]
