# Customer Satisfaction

Customer satisfaction is the judgment a buyer forms after consumption about whether a product or service lived up to what they wanted from it. Richard L. Oliver later framed it as a pleasurable sense of fulfillment: the consumer feels that consumption met a need, desire or goal, and that this fulfillment was pleasant.[^s2] It is distinct from **service quality**, which is an evaluation of the offering itself, and from **loyalty**, which is a commitment to buy again; much of the research on satisfaction is about how these three relate.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin: the expectancy-disconfirmation model

The dominant account comes from Oliver's 1980 article in the *Journal of Marketing Research*. He proposed that satisfaction is a function of two things: the expectations a customer brings to a purchase, and the degree to which actual experience departs from those expectations (disconfirmation). Satisfaction, in turn, feeds attitude change and the intention to buy again.[^s1] Oliver tested the model in a two-stage field study around a flu inoculation programme, surveying both people who took the shot and people who did not, and the data supported the proposed chain.[^s1] The logic implies that the same performance can satisfy one customer and disappoint another, depending on what each expected, which is why managing expectations is as much a marketing lever as improving the product.

## How it is measured

Firms usually survey customers directly, but the most influential standardized measure is the American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI), introduced by Claes Fornell and colleagues in 1996 as a market-based performance measure for firms, industries, sectors and the national economy.[^s3] Its model treats customer expectations, perceived quality and perceived value (quality relative to price) as drivers of satisfaction, and it supports benchmarking both across organizations at one point in time and for the same organization over time.[^s3][^s4] Early ACSI results found satisfaction highest for manufactured goods, lower for services and lowest for government agencies; customization mattered more than reliability, and quality drove satisfaction more than value or price did.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

**Link to profit.** Using Sweden's national satisfaction index and accounting returns such as ROI, Anderson, Fornell and Lehmann found in 1994 that quality raises satisfaction and that satisfaction in turn raises profitability. They also found that customers' expectations of a firm's quality were largely rational, and they offered preliminary evidence that growing market share can lower satisfaction, a trade-off they flagged as an issue for further study.[^s4]

**Link to stock returns.** Fornell, Mithas, Morgeson and Krishnan (2006) reported that a back-tested portfolio of firms with high and above-average ACSI scores returned 40% cumulatively between February 1997 and May 2003, against 13% for the S&P 500, and that a live long-short portfolio gained 75% from April 2000 to December 2004 while the S&P 500 fell 19%. Both portfolios had betas below the market, so the authors argued the excess returns were not a reward for risk, and they read the slow price adjustment as a market imperfection.[^s5]

**Challenge to that finding.** Jacobson and Mizik (2009) re-ran the analysis and found that statistically significant mispricing appeared only among computer and Internet firms, driven by a small group of high-satisfaction companies in that sector during the study window rather than by a systematic failure of markets to price satisfaction.[^s6]

**Satisfaction is not loyalty.** Oliver himself argued in 1999 that satisfaction is a necessary step toward loyalty but an unreliable predictor of it, because many satisfied customers still defect. In his preferred model satisfaction is a seed that grows into loyalty only with other ingredients, such as the customer's own commitment and social bonds with the firm or community; without them it stays dormant, and competitors actively try to manufacture dissatisfaction.[^s2]

## In practice

The ACSI-based stock portfolio is the clearest real-world test of the claim that satisfaction is an economic asset: the index's founders put money behind the idea and reported outperformance from 2000 to 2004.[^s5] For managers, the research suggests tracking satisfaction alongside its drivers (expectations, perceived quality, value) and its outcomes (complaints, repurchase), and not treating a high score as proof of retention.[^s3][^s2]

## Connections

Customer satisfaction sits within **marketing metrics** and **customer relationship management**, and it is the outcome that **service quality** tools are designed to improve: **SERVQUAL** and the **gaps model of service quality** apply the same expectations-versus-perceptions logic to services.[^s1][^s3] It is **measured by** the **American Customer Satisfaction Index**.[^s3] It **causes** (but does not guarantee) **customer loyalty**, which links it to **customer lifetime value**, to **switching costs** that keep even dissatisfied customers in place, and to the **net promoter score**, a loyalty-focused alternative measure.[^s2] The debate over whether satisfaction data predict stock returns made it a test case for the **efficient-market hypothesis**.[^s5][^s6]
