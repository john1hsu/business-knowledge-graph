# Relationship Marketing

Relationship marketing is the view that a firm's marketing task is not only to win a sale but to attract, retain and enhance ongoing relationships with customers and other stakeholders. Leonard L. Berry introduced the term in a 1983 American Marketing Association conference volume on services marketing, and in a 1995 review he described the idea as "an old idea but a new focus" for services practice and research.[^s1] Robert Morgan and Shelby Hunt gave it a widely used definition in 1994: all marketing activity aimed at establishing, developing and maintaining successful relational exchanges, a scope wide enough to include suppliers, employees and partners as well as buyers.[^s2]

## Origin and the paradigm debate

The idea grew out of services marketing, where production and consumption overlap and the customer experiences the provider repeatedly. Berry attributes its rise to the maturing of services marketing and its emphasis on quality, to growing recognition that both firm and customer gain from lasting relationships, and to information technology that made tracking individual customers practical.[^s1] Christian Grönroos pushed the argument further in 1994, claiming the marketing mix had become a straitjacket that encouraged toolbox thinking, and proposing relationship building as an emerging paradigm for the whole discipline. He placed firms on a "marketing strategy continuum" running from transactional to relational, with different implications for pricing, quality management and internal marketing at each end.[^s3]

## How it works

Morgan and Hunt's commitment-trust theory supplies the main mechanism. Relationship commitment (wanting the relationship to continue) and trust (confidence in a partner's reliability and integrity) act as key mediating variables between a firm's relational behaviours and the success of the exchange. They tested the model on automobile tyre retailers and found it outperformed a rival model without the mediators; they also catalogued ten forms of relationship marketing, from buyer partnerships to supplier and internal relationships.[^s2] Berry's practical agenda pointed in the same direction: concentrate on profitable customers, build strong bonds, market to employees as well as customers, and use trust itself as a marketing tool.[^s1] Operationally, firms pursue retention through customer data, service and loyalty schemes; OpenStax notes that keeping a loyal customer is usually easier and cheaper than acquiring a new one, citing airline frequent-flyer programmes and retail loyalty cards such as Starbucks' as everyday examples.[^s7]

## Evidence and critiques

The largest synthesis of the evidence, a 2006 meta-analysis by Robert Palmatier, Rajiv Dant, Dhruv Grewal and Kenneth Evans covering about 38,000 relationships across 94 studies, found broad support for the premise that relationship marketing improves performance. Relationship investment had a large direct effect on sellers' objective performance, and relationship quality mattered more than commitment alone. Effects were stronger in services, channel and business markets, and when relationships were built with an individual salesperson rather than with the firm as a whole.[^s4]

Critics argue the concept outran its evidence. Michael Saren and Nikolaos Tzokas described relationship marketing as over-conceptualised and empirically underdeveloped, and identified five "dangerous axioms" that scholars had accepted without enough scrutiny.[^s5] In Harvard Business Review, Susan Fournier, Susan Dobscha and David Glen Mick warned that many consumer-marketing programmes chasing the cost savings of retention were in practice subverting the very relationships they sought, leaving the concept short of its promise.[^s6] The meta-analysis's moderator findings support a narrower reading: relational investment pays most where relationships matter to customers, and less in low-involvement consumer markets.[^s4]

## In practice

Firms translate relationship marketing into retention targets, account management, loyalty programmes and customer-relationship-management systems that record every interaction. OpenStax describes the typical goals as better customer service relationships and higher retention, with loyalty programmes used to reward repeat purchasing.[^s7] The research above suggests managers should match the intensity of relational effort to the market: deep investment in business-to-business and service relationships, and caution against relationship programmes that customers experience as intrusive in mass consumer markets.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

Relationship marketing sits within **marketing** and is a core choice in **marketing strategy**, the decision of how far along Grönroos's transactional-to-relational continuum a firm should operate.[^s3] It was **created by Leonard Berry** and **influenced by services marketing**, where it first took hold.[^s1] It **contrasts with the marketing mix**, whose 4Ps Grönroos criticised as transaction-oriented.[^s3] **Customer relationship management** is one of its parts, the operational and technological form of the approach.[^s7] Its central goal is **customer loyalty**, and its economic case rests on **customer lifetime value**, the value of retained relationships over time.[^s1][^s7] Its theoretical core is **commitment-trust theory**,[^s2] and its emphasis on ongoing exchange relates it to **service-dominant logic**; Grönroos had already floated a general theory of marketing built on relationship building.[^s3]
