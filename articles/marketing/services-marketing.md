# Services Marketing: Managing Intangible, Inseparable, Variable and Perishable Offerings

**Services marketing** is the part of marketing concerned with offerings that are performances or activities rather than physical objects: education, banking, medical treatment, transport, insurance and the like.[^s1] Its importance tracks the shape of modern economies. In the OpenStax *Principles of Marketing* textbook's breakdown of 2020 US GDP, service industries together accounted for about 67% of output against 10.8% for manufacturing, with finance, insurance and real estate the largest single contributor at 22%.[^s1]

## Origin and development

Services marketing grew out of a long argument about whether services needed their own marketing at all. Fisk, Brown and Bitner, analysing more than 1,000 English-language services marketing publications in a 1993 *Journal of Retailing* review, divided the field's history into three stages: "Crawling Out" (1953–79), when the core debate was whether services differ from goods; "Scurrying About" (1980–85), a burst of rapid growth; and "Walking Erect" (from 1986), when topics such as service quality and service encounters matured and gained legitimacy in leading marketing journals.[^s2]

## Core ideas

**The four characteristics.** The field's organising claim is that services differ from goods in four ways, often abbreviated IHIP.[^s5] Services are *intangible*, so buyers cannot see or try them before purchase and must rely on the seller's promises; *inseparable*, because they are produced and consumed at the same time by a person or machine; *variable* (heterogeneous), since quality depends on who delivers them, when and how; and *perishable*, because an unsold hotel night or airline seat cannot be stored and resold.[^s1] The canonical four-item list comes from a systematic literature review by Zeithaml, Parasuraman and Berry published in the *Journal of Marketing* in 1985; earlier writers had proposed longer lists, Rathmell (1966) naming 13 differences and Lovelock (1991) seven.[^s5]

**Managerial responses.** Each characteristic implies a tactic. Firms counter intangibility by offering physical evidence, such as virtual try-on tools, and manage perishability by shifting demand with off-peak prices like happy-hour discounts.[^s1] Services are also classified by how they are delivered: people-based (unskilled, skilled or professional labour) versus equipment-based, or by Lovelock's four process types directed at people's bodies, possessions, minds or information.[^s1]

**The 7Ps.** Because the 4Ps of the marketing mix were built for goods, Booms and Bitner (1981) proposed an extended mix of seven Ps for services, adding participants (people), physical evidence and process.[^s3] Bitner's 1992 "servicescape" paper developed the physical-evidence element: since a service is consumed on the firm's premises and is intangible, customers and employees read the built environment, including ambient conditions, spatial layout, and signs and symbols, as cues to quality and positioning.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The IHIP framework won near-universal acceptance among marketing scholars, yet by the 2000s it drew sustained criticism.[^s5] Vargo and Lusch argued in 2004 that the four traits do not actually separate services from goods. Many services have tangible results, airline travel and retail banking are highly standardised, and goods too can perish or be co-produced by users. In their view the traits reflect a manufacturing mindset and lead to misguided strategies, such as minimising customer involvement when firms should seek it.[^s5] Lovelock and Gummesson, writing the same year, likewise questioned the IHIP paradigm and proposed a different dividing line: services are exchanges that do not transfer ownership, delivering benefits through rental of or access to labour, skills, facilities or systems.[^s6] On the mix itself, Rafiq and Ahmed's 1995 survey found widespread dissatisfaction with the 4Ps among UK and European marketing academics and broad acceptance of the 7Ps, leading them to suggest the extended mix could serve as a generic framework even for consumer goods.[^s3]

## In practice

Hotels and airlines illustrate the logic. With rooms and seats that perish each night or flight, they use pricing to fill capacity at off-peak times, and they invest in staff, lobbies and cabins because guests judge an intangible stay largely through people and surroundings.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

Services marketing sits within the broader field of **marketing** and was **influenced by the marketing mix**, whose goods-centred 4Ps it extended into the 7Ps.[^s3] The **servicescape** is one of its parts, formalising the physical-evidence element.[^s4] Its maturing research stream on **service quality** and service encounters is where the **gaps model of service quality** and the **SERVQUAL** scale belong, and it relates closely to work on **customer satisfaction** and **relationship marketing**.[^s2] Critiques of its IHIP foundations **influenced service-dominant logic**, Vargo and Lusch's proposal that service, not goods, is the fundamental unit of exchange.[^s5][^s6]
