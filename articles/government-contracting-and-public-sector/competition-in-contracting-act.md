# Competition in Contracting Act (CICA)

The **Competition in Contracting Act of 1984 (CICA)** is the US statute that made **full and open competition** the default rule for federal purchasing. Enacted on July 18, 1984 as title VII of division B of Public Law 98-369, it applied to solicitations issued after March 31, 1985.[^s2] Its civilian-agency core now sits at 41 U.S.C. § 3301, which tells an executive agency to obtain full and open competition through competitive procedures and to pick the procedure best suited to the circumstances, subject only to listed statutory exceptions; the defense counterpart is 10 U.S.C. § 3201.[^s1][^s3] In practice CICA is what a contracting officer has in mind when asking, "can I award this without competing it?"

## Origin

Before CICA, the main procurement statutes dated from 1947 and 1949 and asked for competition only "whenever practicable." Senate reports blamed unnecessary sole-source awards on year-end spending rushes, weak planning, overly detailed specifications and unsolicited proposals.[^s2] CICA replaced the old preference for formally advertised (sealed-bid) buying with a single competition standard, required advance procurement planning and market research, required public notice of proposed contract actions and reporting of awards to the Federal Procurement Data System, and extended certified cost or pricing data requirements to civilian agencies.[^s2]

## How it works

The statute lets agencies choose among competitive procedures: **sealed bids** when award turns on price, discussions are unnecessary and more than one bid is expected, and **competitive proposals** otherwise.[^s1] The FAR implements the rule in Part 6. Subpart 6.2 allows "full and open competition after exclusion of sources," which is how small business, 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran-owned and women-owned set-asides fit inside the CICA framework.[^s3]

Subpart 6.3 lists the seven circumstances that permit **other than full and open competition**: only one responsible source; unusual and compelling urgency; industrial mobilization or essential research or expert capability; international agreement; authorization or requirement by statute; national security; and public interest, which needs an agency-head determination and 30 days' written notice to Congress.[^s3] Each use needs a written **justification and approval (J&A)** certified by the contracting officer. As of FAC 2026-01 (effective March 13, 2026), approval rises with dollar value: the contracting officer up to $900,000; the competition advocate above that to $20 million; the head of the procuring activity to $90 million ($150 million for DoD, NASA and the Coast Guard); and the senior procurement executive above that.[^s3] Justifications are generally posted publicly within 14 days after award.[^s3]

CICA also required each agency and procuring activity to designate a **competition advocate** and to report annually on competition.[^s2] Its best-known side effect is on protests: it gave GAO an express statutory basis to decide bid protests, set decision deadlines, required agencies to stay many protested procurements and let GAO award protest costs. The executive branch initially called the stay provisions unconstitutional and told agencies not to comply until a federal district court upheld them in May 1985.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

GAO's case for the policy is that competition saves taxpayers money, improves contractor performance, curbs fraud and promotes accountability.[^s4] Yet compliance on paper has not guaranteed real rivalry. In a July 2010 review, GAO found noncompetitive contracts fell from about 35.6% of federal obligations in FY2005 to 31.2% in FY2009, while competed contracts receiving only **one offer** held at roughly 13% of obligations each year.[^s4] Among the noncompetitive contracts it sampled, the only-one-source exception was cited most often (57%), followed by 8(a) sole-source awards (27%). GAO traced the barriers to the government's lack of proprietary technical data, reliance on incumbents, program-office preferences, short timelines and teaming that leaves a single bidder, and it found about 18% of sampled contracts miscoded in federal data.[^s4] The critique, then, is that CICA polices the *form* of competition more effectively than its *substance*.

## In practice

A program office that wants to extend an incumbent's proprietary system without competing it must document why only one source can meet the need, show its market research, obtain approval at the right dollar tier and post the J&A on SAM.gov.[^s3] GAO describes an Air Force case in which the mere threat of competition led an incumbent to cut its price.[^s4] Under the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul**, the model deviation text for Part 6 renumbers these rules (for example, the exceptions become 6.103-1 through 6.103-7) while keeping the same approval tiers and posting deadlines.[^s5]

## Connections

CICA is a pillar of **public procurement** and **influenced the Federal Acquisition Regulation**, whose Part 6 implements it.[^s2][^s3] It **applies to sole-source contracting**, which it permits only by exception and J&A, and it **depends on market research** to show whether other sources exist.[^s2][^s3] The **contracting officer** certifies justifications, **small business set-asides** operate as competition after exclusion of sources, and **bid protests** at the **GAO** rest on the authority CICA created.[^s2][^s3] It works alongside the **Truth in Negotiations Act**, whose cost-data rules CICA extended to civilian agencies, and is being rewritten in form, not substance, by the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul**.[^s2][^s5]
