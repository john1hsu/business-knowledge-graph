# Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) Contract

An indefinite delivery, indefinite quantity (IDIQ) contract lets a US federal agency set up the terms of a buying relationship before it knows exactly how much it will need or when. The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) groups three kinds of indefinite-delivery contracts: definite-quantity contracts, requirements contracts and indefinite-quantity contracts. The last of these, the IDIQ, covers an indefinite quantity within stated limits over a fixed period.[^s1] The government must order, and the contractor must supply, a stated minimum. That minimum has to be more than nominal but should not exceed what the agency is fairly certain to order. The contracting officer also sets a reasonable maximum based on market research or similar past buys.[^s1] Work is then authorised through task orders for services and delivery orders for supplies. These orders obligate the funds, and prices are often settled when each order is placed.[^s2]

## Origin

Indefinite-quantity contracting is older than the FAR, but its modern form comes from the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act of 1994 (FASA, Public Law 103-355). FASA told agencies planning a task- or delivery-order format to consider awarding several contracts rather than one. It created a preference for multiple awards and required that each holder get a fair opportunity to be considered for orders, with limited exceptions.[^s4] Those exceptions included unusual urgency, unique or specialised needs, logical follow-on orders and orders placed to meet a minimum guarantee. They still form the core of today's rules.[^s1][^s4]

## How it works

Under FAR 16.504(c), contracting officers must favour multiple awards to the maximum extent practicable. They must not use multiple awards in certain cases, such as highly specialised work or when the estimated value is at or below the simplified acquisition threshold.[^s1] As of FAR FAC 2025-06 (effective October 1, 2025), a single-award task- or delivery-order contract above $150 million needs a written head-of-agency determination. Allowed grounds include integrally related work, only one qualified source, or the public interest; for the public-interest ground, Congress must be notified within 30 days.[^s1] Under multiple-award contracts, every holder must get a fair opportunity to compete for orders above the micro-purchase threshold, and price or cost must be an evaluation factor.[^s1] Orders above $7.5 million carry extra duties, including a clear notice, disclosed evaluation factors and debriefings. Protests of orders are generally barred. The exceptions are orders that increase the contract's scope, period or maximum value, and orders above $10 million at civilian agencies or $25 million at DoD, NASA and the Coast Guard, which can be protested at GAO.[^s1]

Governmentwide acquisition contracts (GWACs) are a prominent variant: IDIQ contracts for information technology that one agency awards for use across government. Under the Clinger-Cohen Act, OMB designates which agencies may run them, currently GSA, the National Institutes of Health and NASA.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

IDIQs are a large share of federal buying. GAO found that from FY2011 to FY2015 agencies obligated more than $130 billion a year through IDIQs, about one-third of federal contract obligations. About two-thirds of that went to services, and DoD accounted for about 68%.[^s3] For FY2024, CRS reported that indefinite delivery vehicles, the broader category that includes IDIQs, took about 56% of DoD and 63% of non-DoD contract award dollars.[^s2] Officials mainly value speed and flexibility: placing an order is easier than awarding a new contract, and the government owes nothing beyond the minimum.[^s2][^s3]

The main critique is weak competition. Despite the FAR's preference for multiple awards, about 60% of IDIQ dollars in FY2011–FY2015 went through single-award contracts, and 10 of the 18 DoD single-award contracts GAO sampled were not competed.[^s3] Defence Acquisition University warns that single awards raise the risk of vendor lock-in. Industry groups argue that large multiple-award vehicles can disadvantage small businesses.[^s2] The problem is long-standing. In 1998 GAO found that several agencies gave holders little real competition for orders, partly by naming preferred contractors, a practice OMB asked agencies to stop.[^s4]

## In practice

A firm that wins a place on an IDIQ has bought a hunting licence, not revenue. It is guaranteed only the minimum and must then win orders against the other holders. As a result, capture strategy covers two stages: winning the vehicle, then winning the orders under it. The rules are also changing. On September 30, 2025, the FAR Council released Revolutionary FAR Overhaul model deviation text for Part 16, and agencies are adopting it through class deviations, so section numbering and details can differ by agency during the transition.[^s5]

## Connections

The IDIQ is one of the **government contract types** used in US **public procurement** and is **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation**, Subpart 16.5.[^s1] Its multiple-award preference and fair-opportunity rule were **influenced by** the **Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act** of 1994.[^s4] It **contrasts with** the **requirements contract**, which commits an agency to buy all of its actual needs from one contractor.[^s1] Orders may be evaluated through a **best-value tradeoff**, can be set aside for **small businesses**, and are placed by a **contracting officer**.[^s1] The **governmentwide acquisition contract** is a specialised IDIQ for IT.[^s2]
