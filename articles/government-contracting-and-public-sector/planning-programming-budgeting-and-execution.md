# Planning, Programming, Budgeting and Execution (PPBE)

**Planning, Programming, Budgeting and Execution (PPBE)** is the U.S. Department of Defense's (DoD) system for allocating resources among the military departments, defense agencies and other components. The Congressional Research Service (CRS) describes it as the department's primary decision process for translating strategic guidance into resource allocation decisions, and its main output is DoD's share of the President's annual budget request.[^s1] Defense budgets are among the largest single resource-allocation exercises in any organization, so PPBE is the upstream process that determines which programs, and therefore which contracts, are funded at all.[^s2]

## Origin

Before 1961 each military service built its own budget with little overall guidance or check for duplication. Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara and his comptroller Charles Hitch replaced that system with the Planning, Programming, and Budgeting System (PPBS), which grouped spending into mission-oriented program categories, applied analytic techniques to costs and benefits, and looked across several years rather than one.[^s6][^s2] Hitch had led the economics division at the RAND Corporation,[^s2] where he and Roland McKean wrote *The Economics of Defense in the Nuclear Age* (1960), which treated defense as a problem of the most efficient allocation of scarce national resources, analysed at three levels: total national resources, the share devoted to security, and how efficiently defense agencies use their share.[^s4]

PPBS was briefly exported across the federal government. On August 25, 1965, President Lyndon Johnson directed every agency to introduce an integrated PPBS, and the Bureau of the Budget issued implementing instructions in Bulletin 66-3 that October.[^s5] Inside the Pentagon, Secretary Melvin Laird kept the system in the early 1970s but loosened its centralized, top-down character by giving the services fiscal and planning guidance and letting them build their own budgets for review.[^s6] DoD renamed the process PPBE in 2003 to give more emphasis to execution.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

PPBE runs as four interrelated phases, each led by a different office in the Office of the Secretary of Defense:[^s1]

- **Planning** (Under Secretary for Policy, with the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs): produces the Defense Planning Guidance, which sets force-development priorities.[^s1]
- **Programming** (Director of Cost Assessment and Program Evaluation): each component submits a Program Objective Memorandum recommending five years of resources, and CAPE's review updates the Future Years Defense Program (FYDP).[^s1]
- **Budgeting** (Comptroller): components convert the first FYDP year into a Budget Estimate Submission; the result goes to the Office of Management and Budget in December and to Congress, usually in February.[^s1]
- **Execution** (OSD and components): officials assess how funds are obligated and spent and shift money through transfers and reprogrammings, many needing congressional notice or approval.[^s2]

Because planning starts more than two years before money is spent, several cycles overlap. In June 2022, for example, DoD was executing FY2022 funds, watching FY2023 appropriations, programming and budgeting FY2024 and planning FY2025.[^s2] Execution is further constrained by appropriation "colors of money" with different periods of availability, as of 2022: one year for military personnel and operations and maintenance, two for research and development, three for most procurement and five for military construction.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Defenders credit the system with exposing duplication, weighing costs against benefits over multiple years and giving stakeholders a voice, a clear improvement on pre-1961 practice.[^s6] Critics call it an industrial-era process suited to aircraft carriers and bombers rather than software. The Defense Innovation Board noted in 2019 that it needs two or more years of lead time, and a 2021 Hudson Institute estimate put some decision cycles at up to six years.[^s2] Former DoD comptroller Robert Hale adds that it considers too few alternatives, generates heavy approval workloads, and has resisted streamlining efforts such as biennial budgeting.[^s6]

Congress created a Commission on PPBE Reform in Section 1004 of the FY2022 National Defense Authorization Act.[^s1] Its March 2024 final report made 28 recommendations in five areas. The first was to replace PPBE with a Defense Resourcing System built on three processes (strategy, resource allocation and execution) rather than four.[^s1][^s3] Others included restructuring appropriations around capability areas instead of lifecycle categories and letting up to 5% of operating funds carry over into the next fiscal year.[^s3]

## In practice

Contractors and program managers track PPBE because a program absent from a service's Program Objective Memorandum rarely reaches the budget request, and "new start" programs can wait years for funding. The gap between a promising prototype and a funded program of record is the "valley of death" that critics, including former Google CEO Eric Schmidt in congressional testimony, attribute to the budgeting process.[^s2]

## Connections

PPBE is a specialised form of **public budgeting** within **public administration**, and it sits upstream of **public procurement** and the **Federal Acquisition Regulation**: PPBE decides what is funded, while acquisition rules govern how the money is contracted.[^s2] Its multiyear, analysis-driven logic parallels corporate **capital budgeting**, and its link between objectives and resources resembles **management by objectives**.[^s4][^s6] It was created by **Robert McNamara** and **Charles Hitch**, drew on Hitch and McKean's RAND book, relies on **cost-benefit analysis**, and may be followed by the proposed **Defense Resourcing System**.[^s2][^s4][^s3]
