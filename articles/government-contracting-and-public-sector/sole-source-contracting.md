# Sole-Source Contracting

A **sole-source contract** is a contract awarded to a single firm without competition from other firms.[^s2] In US federal procurement the default, set by the Competition in Contracting Act of 1984, is "full and open competition," so a sole-source award is always an exception that must rest on a specific legal authority.[^s2] FAR Subpart 6.3 implements that rule: every noncompetitive contract must cite its statutory basis (41 U.S.C. 3304 for civilian agencies, 10 U.S.C. 3204 for DoD, NASA and the Coast Guard), and the contracting officer must still solicit offers from as many sources as practicable.[^s1] Two excuses are expressly ruled out: poor advance planning and the looming expiration of agency funds.[^s1]

## The seven exceptions

FAR 6.302 lists seven authorities for other than full and open competition.[^s1]

1. **Only one responsible source** (6.302-1) — unique supplies or services, follow-on work on a major system where a new source would bring substantial duplicated cost or unacceptable delay, or a unique and innovative unsolicited research proposal. Brand-name specifications also fall here.[^s1]
2. **Unusual and compelling urgency** (6.302-2) — when delay would seriously injure the government; performance generally may not exceed one year, including options, without a separate determination.[^s1]
3. **Industrial mobilization, essential engineering or research capability, or expert services** (6.302-3), such as keeping a supplier warm for emergencies or hiring expert witnesses.[^s1]
4. **International agreement** (6.302-4) — a treaty, international agreement or a reimbursing foreign government's written direction precludes competition.[^s1]
5. **Authorized or required by statute** (6.302-5) — mandatory sources such as AbilityOne and UNICOR, and sole-source awards under the 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran and women-owned small business programs.[^s1]
6. **National security** (6.302-6) — disclosing the need would compromise security; mere classification is not enough.[^s1]
7. **Public interest** (6.302-7) — a last resort needing a written agency-head determination and at least 30 days' written notice to Congress.[^s1]

## Justification and approval

Before negotiating, the contracting officer must prepare and certify a written **justification and approval (J&A)** stating the authority, the unique qualifications that support it, the market research done, the efforts to find other sources, why the price will be fair and reasonable, and the barriers to competition and plans to remove them; technical staff certify the supporting data.[^s1] Under FAC 2026-01 (effective March 13, 2026) approval escalates with value, options included: the contracting officer's certification suffices up to $900,000, the competition advocate approves to $20 million, the head of the procuring activity to $90 million ($150 million for DoD, NASA and the Coast Guard), and the senior procurement executive above that.[^s1] J&As are generally posted on SAM.gov within 14 days after award, or 30 days for urgency awards, with brand-name justifications posted alongside the solicitation.[^s1]

## Small business sole-source authorities

Separate statutes let agencies sole-source to small firms within dollar caps. As of the September 2026 CRS summary, the limits are $5.5 million ($8.5 million for manufacturing) for 8(a), HUBZone and women-owned firms and $5 million ($8.5 million for manufacturing) for service-disabled veteran-owned firms.[^s2] An 8(a) participant stops receiving new 8(a) sole-source awards once its cumulative total reaches $168.5 million, while firms owned by Alaska Native Corporations, tribes and Native Hawaiian Organizations may exceed the individual caps subject to a justification above a higher threshold.[^s2] In FY2025, sole-source awards to small businesses totaled nearly $30 billion, about 3.7% of federal contract obligations.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The core critique is that sole-sourcing entrenches incumbents. GAO found that DoD's competition rate fell from 62.6% in FY2008 to 57.1% in FY2012, that most noncompetitive awards cited the only-one-source exception, and that long reliance on original equipment manufacturers was a chronic barrier.[^s3] Many J&As contained the required elements but gave little insight into market conditions or plans to compete later; DoD responded in August 2014 by requiring follow-on justifications to include the prior J&A.[^s3] The economics literature adds nuance: in a study of private building contracts in Northern California (1995–2000), Bajari, McMillan and Tadelis found that auctions perform poorly for complex projects, incomplete designs and thin markets, and that negotiation lets buyers draw on contractor expertise.[^s4] Sole-sourcing is therefore not inherently wasteful; the policy problem is using it where competition was feasible.

## In practice

A program office extending a proprietary weapons-support system typically writes a 6.302-1 follow-on J&A, documents market research showing no other qualified source, and lists actions, such as buying technical data rights, to enable competition next time.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Sole-source contracting is a practice within **public procurement**, **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** (Part 6), and it exists as the exception that the **Competition in Contracting Act** permits.[^s1][^s2] It **depends on market research** to show no other source exists, and its J&A is certified by the **contracting officer**.[^s1] Because no price competition disciplines the offer, large sole-source negotiations trigger the **Truth in Negotiations Act**'s cost-data rules; small business sole-source authorities sit beside **small business set-asides**.[^s1][^s2] Repeated follow-on awards build **switching costs** around incumbents, and **GAO** reviews both agency competition practices and **bid protests** that challenge improper sole-source awards.[^s3] It **contrasts with competitive bidding** and is an alternative to a competed **best-value tradeoff**.[^s4]
