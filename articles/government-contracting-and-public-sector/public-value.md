# Public Value: Mark Moore's Strategic Triangle for Public Managers

**Public value** is the idea that the job of a public manager is to create value for the public, much as the job of a private manager is to create economic value for shareholders. Mark H. Moore of Harvard's Kennedy School set it out in *Creating Public Value: Strategic Management in Government*, published by Harvard University Press in 1995.[^s1][^s2] The concept works on three levels: as a practical guide for public executives, as a yardstick for what a policy or agency adds beyond its monetary costs and benefits, and as a view of what the proper ends of government are.[^s2] Its defining claim is that the value is judged collectively, by citizens, taxpayers and their elected representatives, rather than by individual service users treated as customers.[^s2]

## How it works: the strategic triangle

Moore's central tool is the **strategic triangle**, which asks a manager three questions at once.[^s2]

1. **Public value** — what valuable outcome does the manager believe the organization can produce?[^s2]
2. **Legitimacy and support** — what authority, mandate and political backing can the manager draw on? Moore calls the source of this backing the *authorizing environment*: the institutions and processes that turn private interests into a public able to decide what to tax, spend and regulate.[^s2]
3. **Operational capacity** — what people, money, skills and partners are available to deliver results?[^s2]

A strategy is sound only when the three corners line up; misalignment destroys value.[^s2] Moore's shorthand for two failure modes is "big hat and no cattle," where political support exists but the organization cannot deliver, and a second failure in which funding keeps flowing to activities that no longer create value.[^s2] The framework thus treats upward management of politicians and overseers, and outward management of partners, as part of the public manager's strategic work rather than as a distraction from it.[^s2]

## Measuring public value

Because government lacks a profit figure, Moore proposed a **public value account** as the public-sector counterpart of a bottom line, weighing collectively valued outcomes against the money and state authority used to produce them.[^s2] In a 2006 lecture he conceded that critics found the original book too abstract and reframed the challenge as *recognising* public value, both as a shared community judgment and as a technical measurement system comparable to financial accounting.[^s3] He asked why managers should measure at all, whether to measure inputs, processes, outputs or outcomes along the value chain, and who the customers whose satisfaction counts really are.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Supporters argue that public value fits a world of networked, multisector governance. Gerry Stoker, writing in 2006, contrasted it with traditional public administration and New Public Management (NPM) and argued that public value management, grounded in dialogue and exchange among partners, suits networks better than rules or market incentives.[^s4] Bryson, Crosby and Bloomberg (2014) placed it within a broader movement that moves beyond the efficiency focus of traditional administration and the efficiency-and-effectiveness focus of NPM, giving more weight to democratic values and to citizens, firms and nonprofits as co-producers of public outcomes.[^s6]

The sharpest critique comes from R. A. W. Rhodes and John Wanna (2009). They questioned whether the approach fits Westminster governments, with their hierarchical control, strong ministers, tight accountability and disciplined two-party politics.[^s5] They identified confusion over what kind of theory public value is and over who counts as a "public manager," and challenged several assumptions: a benign view of large organizations, the primacy of management, the transferability of private-sector experience, the downgrading of party politics, and the casting of officials as guardians of the public interest.[^s5] On this reading, encouraging unelected managers to define public value risks weakening democratic accountability.

## In practice

Moore illustrated the measurement problem with **COMPSTAT** at the New York City Police Department. In 1994 Commissioner William Bratton pledged a 10 percent cut in crime, made 72 precinct commanders accountable, and reviewed their crime statistics in regular meetings, after which reported crime fell sharply over two years.[^s3] Moore argued that the usual lessons drawn from COMPSTAT were too narrow, because crime counts alone do not capture all the public value a police force produces or the costs it imposes.[^s3] Governments have also adopted the framework directly: New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet held a 2017 roundtable with Moore to test how the triangle and a public value account could inform policy advice, including adding the authorizing environment to its test of desirable, deliverable and cost-effective options.[^s2]

## Connections

Public value is a branch of **public administration** and an adaptation of **strategic management** to government, which is why both are its broader nodes.[^s1][^s4] It **contrasts with New Public Management**, which treats citizens mainly as customers and leans on market-style incentives.[^s4][^s6] Its insistence on serving many constituencies parallels **stakeholder theory**, and the public value account plays the role a **balanced scorecard** plays in firms: a multidimensional substitute for a single financial measure.[^s2][^s3] In **public procurement**, the same logic underlies **best-value tradeoff** decisions, where agencies judge worth on more than price. **COMPSTAT** is the case Moore used to test how public value can be measured.[^s3]
