# DFARS: Defense Department Rules Layered on Top of the FAR

The Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS) is the body of procurement rules the US Department of Defense (DoD) applies on top of the government-wide Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR). It is codified as Chapter 2 of Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations, directly after the FAR's Chapter 1, and DoD describes it, together with its companion guidance, as providing uniform acquisition policies and procedures across the department.[^s1][^s2] It is one of dozens of agency supplements codified in Title 48, and the Congressional Research Service singles it out as the best-known example.[^s3]

## Origin

Before the FAR, civilian agencies bought under the Federal Procurement Regulations while DoD used its own Defense Acquisition Regulation, which had been called the Armed Services Procurement Regulation until 1978. The single FAR was published in September 1983 and took effect on October 1, 1984, and each agency's remaining unique rules moved into a supplement codified after it.[^s3] The FAR authorizes agency heads to issue regulations that implement or supplement it, but supplements may not conflict with the FAR unless a statute requires it or a deviation is authorized. The Congressional Research Service notes that supplements are often assumed to differ sharply from the FAR when they generally do not, except where an agency-specific statute applies.[^s3]

## How it works

DFARS Subpart 201.3 sets out what belongs in the supplement: legal requirements, DoD-wide policies, delegations of FAR authority, deviations from the FAR, and policies with a significant effect beyond DoD's internal operations or a significant cost or administrative impact on contractors. Material that fails that test goes into the non-regulatory Procedures, Guidance, and Information (PGI) companion.[^s1] DFARS numbering mirrors the FAR: implementing text keeps the corresponding FAR numbering (DFARS Subpart 201.3 parallels FAR Subpart 1.3), while purely supplemental text uses section numbers of 70 and up. Military department and component supplements must follow the same scheme, using 90 and up.[^s1]

Governance is centralized. Changes with a significant public effect need approval from the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment, who has delegated most of that authority to the Principal Director of Defense Pricing, Contracting, and Acquisition Policy (DPCAP). Requests run through the Director of the Defense Acquisition Regulations Council, new contractor certification requirements are restricted unless a statute requires them, and DPCAP publishes changes in the Federal Register with an effective date that governs which solicitations must carry new clauses.[^s1] DPCAP posts consolidated editions; as of November 2026 its page listed a DFARS edition dated November 10, 2025 and a PGI edition dated October 24, 2025.[^s2]

## In practice

For firms, the DFARS matters mainly through the defense-unique clauses in Part 252 that flow into contracts and down to subcontractors. A prominent recent example is the cybersecurity rule published in September 2025 (DFARS Case 2019-D041), effective November 10, 2025. It added clause 252.204-7021 and provision 252.204-7025, which require contractors handling federal contract information or controlled unclassified information to post a current Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) status, keep it for the life of the contract, flow it down to subcontractors, and affirm compliance annually, with a three-year phase-in and an exemption for awards solely for commercially available off-the-shelf items.[^s4] DoD estimated that from year four the rule would reach 337,968 unique entities, 68 percent of them small, and put its 10-year cost at about $344.9 million in present value at a 3 percent discount rate.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The main critique of the DFARS is accumulated complexity. The congressionally created Section 809 Panel reported in January 2018 that government clauses applicable to commercial-item buys under the FAR and DFARS together had grown from 57 in 1995 to 165, of which 122 came from statute, 20 from executive orders and 23 from agency policy, and it found dozens of inconsistent subcontract-related definitions across the two regulations.[^s6] The panel described flowing government-unique terms down to suppliers as costly and administratively complex, although its own count shows most of those clauses rest on statutes Congress passed rather than on DoD discretion.[^s6] In December 2025 DPCAP announced 31 Phase 1 class deviations to the FAR and DFARS under the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul, effective February 1, 2026. They keep statutory and necessary DoD provisions, remove, revise or move non-statutory material into PGI, and set up formal rulemaking during 2026.[^s5]

## Connections

The DFARS sits within **public procurement** and supplements the **Federal Acquisition Regulation**, whose structure and numbering it follows; it replaced the **Defense Acquisition Regulation** when the FAR took effect in 1984.[^s1][^s3] It is issued by the **Department of Defense** and includes the companion **PGI**.[^s1][^s2] Because it carries defense-specific statutory requirements and FAR deviations, DoD's own rules for practices such as the **best-value tradeoff**, **lowest price technically acceptable** source selection and **Truth in Negotiations Act** cost-or-pricing data sit in the DFARS alongside the FAR, and the RFO deviations rewrite those parts in parallel.[^s1][^s5] Its clauses carry **CMMC** cybersecurity requirements to contractors, and the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul** is now rewriting it alongside the FAR.[^s4][^s5]
