# Truth in Negotiations Act

The **Truth in Negotiations Act (TINA)** is the US federal law that requires a contractor negotiating a large government contract without real price competition to hand over its **cost or pricing data** and certify that the data are accurate, complete and current.[^s4] Enacted in 1962 as Public Law 87-653, it was designed to put the contracting officer on an equal factual footing with the seller, correcting the information gap that otherwise favors the contractor in sole-source deals.[^s5] For defense buys the requirement now sits at 10 U.S.C. § 3702, moved there in 2021 from the former 10 U.S.C. § 2306a, and the FAR implements it for both the defense and civilian statutes, 10 U.S.C. chapter 271 and 41 U.S.C. chapter 35.[^s4][^s1]

## How it works

A contracting officer must obtain certified cost or pricing data before awarding a negotiated contract or modification expected to exceed the threshold, unless an exception applies.[^s1] FAR 15.403-1 lists those exceptions: prices based on adequate price competition, prices set by law or regulation, commercial products or services (and modifications to them), and waivers. Certified data may never be demanded at or below the simplified acquisition threshold, although other data can still be requested to judge whether a price is fair and reasonable.[^s2] Competition is "adequate" when, among other conditions, two or more responsible offerors compete independently and price is a substantial factor in the award.[^s2] The contractor then signs a **Certificate of Current Cost or Pricing Data**, which speaks as of the date the parties agree on price.[^s1] Subcontractors above the threshold certify to the prime contractor, which passes the obligation down the supply chain.[^s4]

## Thresholds over time

The threshold is a time-sensitive figure. The FAR, as of FAC 2026-01 (effective March 13, 2026), lists $950,000 for prime contracts awarded before July 1, 2018 and $2.5 million for those awarded on or after that date.[^s1] The FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act (Pub. L. 119-60, 2025) then amended the defense statute so that contracts entered into after June 30, 2026 face a $10 million threshold, with the earlier $2 million statutory base still governing contracts signed on or before that date; the statute also adjusts its amounts for inflation every five years.[^s4] As of the March 2026 FAR text, the regulation had not yet been rewritten to show the new figure.[^s1]

## Defective pricing remedies

TINA's teeth are its price-reduction clauses. If certified data turn out to be inaccurate, incomplete or out of date, the government may recover the significant amount by which the price, including profit or fee, was inflated, plus interest from the date of overpayment.[^s3] Where a contractor knowingly submitted defective data, the contracting officer must also seek a penalty equal to the overpayment.[^s3] The FAR bars several defenses: that the contractor was a sole source or had superior bargaining power, that the contracting officer should have caught the error, or that the contractor never signed the certificate. Offsets for understated data are allowed only within limits.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

In economic terms, TINA attacks **adverse selection**: by forcing disclosure of costs, it limits the information rents a sole-source seller can extract.[^s5] A 2016 Naval Postgraduate School analysis argues its main flaw is that it ignores **moral hazard**. Under a firm-fixed-price contract, a firm that cuts costs sharply below its certified estimate risks a defective-pricing audit, which can dull the incentive to work efficiently or invite cost padding. The authors suggest waivers or multi-year fixed-price deals without TINA for some contracts, while conceding that waivers re-expose the government to information rents and that their case is theoretical rather than empirical.[^s5] Enforcement has also been uneven: a 1993 GAO review urged DoD to make better use of TINA's interest and penalty provisions, and later noted that defective pricing had declined significantly.[^s6] Opponents describe the regime as administratively burdensome,[^s5] and Congress has raised the certification threshold several times since 2018.[^s4][^s1]

## In practice

A defense prime negotiating a sole-source follow-on production lot above the threshold would typically submit a proposal backed by certified data, flow the requirement to large subcontractors, and face post-award audit of whether its estimates matched data available at price agreement.[^s1][^s3][^s4] A competitively awarded commercial IT buy, by contrast, falls under the competition or commercial exceptions.[^s2]

## Connections

TINA is part of **public procurement** and the FAR's **contract pricing** rules, and is **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation**, which implements it.[^s1] It **mitigates information asymmetry** and **adverse selection** in **sole-source contracting**, a classic **principal-agent** setting.[^s5] Its effect depends on **government contract types**, being most at odds with fixed-price incentives.[^s5] The **contracting officer** enforces it, and the **GAO** has reviewed its use.[^s3][^s6]
