# Contracting Officer: Warranted Authority to Bind the Government

A **contracting officer** (CO) is the US federal official authorized to enter into, administer and terminate contracts on the government's behalf and to make the determinations and findings those actions require. Contracting authority belongs to the head of each agency, who delegates it downward; under the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), only contracting officers may sign contracts for the government, and each may bind it only to the extent of the authority delegated to them.[^s1] That delegation is the **warrant**: a written appointment on Standard Form 1402, *Certificate of Appointment*, which states any limits on the officer's authority beyond those already set by law and regulation.[^s1]

## Origin and legal basis

The rule that the government is bound only by officials acting within their actual authority predates the FAR. In *Federal Crop Insurance Corp. v. Merrill* (1947), Idaho farmers insured spring wheat after a county committee acting as the federal insurer's agent told them, wrongly, that wheat reseeded on winter-wheat acreage was covered. When drought destroyed the crop, the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Frankfurter, refused payment for the reseeded acres. It held that anyone dealing with the government bears the risk of confirming that the official acts within his authority, even when the official himself misunderstands its limits, and that regulations published in the Federal Register give legal notice to all.[^s3] The modern FAR codifies the same principle in Subpart 1.6, and the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul model text carries it forward, renumbering the authority, appointment and ratification rules to sections 1.402 through 1.405.[^s1][^s2]

## How it works

**Selection and appointment.** Agency heads must run a procurement career-management program and a system for selecting, appointing and terminating contracting officers. Appointing officials weigh the complexity and dollar value of the work against the candidate's experience, training, education, business acumen, judgment, character and reputation.[^s1] Agencies tier warrants by dollar value. The Commerce Department's manual (revised December 2023), for instance, sets a Level I warrant at up to $250,000 inside an acquisition office, Level II at up to $1 million and Level III as unlimited, with one, two and four years of contracting experience respectively. Every level requires the single-tier Federal Acquisition Certification in Contracting (FAC-C Professional) that a January 2023 OFPP memorandum substituted for the old Levels I–III, plus 100 continuous-learning points every two years.[^s4] Warrants end by letter, on reassignment, separation or unsatisfactory performance, and never retroactively.[^s1][^s4]

**Responsibilities.** Before signing, a contracting officer must confirm that all legal, regulatory and procedural requirements, including clearances and approvals, are met and that funds are available. The officer must also ensure contract compliance, safeguard the government's interests and treat contractors impartially, fairly and equitably, while exercising wide latitude for business judgment and seeking audit, legal, engineering and other specialist advice as needed.[^s1][^s2]

**Representatives.** Day-to-day technical oversight is usually delegated in writing to a contracting officer's representative (COR). A COR is required on most contracts and orders other than firm-fixed-price ones, must be certified, cannot redelegate, may be personally liable for unauthorized acts and has no power to change price, quality, quantity, delivery or other terms.[^s1][^s2]

**Ratification.** When someone without authority commits the government, the agreement is an "unauthorized commitment." The head of the contracting activity (or a delegate no lower than the chief of the contracting office) may ratify it only if the government received a benefit, a proper contract would have been lawful, a contracting officer finds the price fair and reasonable and recommends payment, and funds were available at the time.[^s1] Commitments that fail these tests go to claims procedures instead.[^s1][^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The warrant system is a textbook answer to a principal-agent problem: it concentrates the power to obligate public funds in trained, accountable individuals and makes the limits of that power public. Its cost falls on contractors and front-line officials. As *Merrill* shows, an honest firm that relies on a program official's assurances can be left unpaid, and four justices dissented from that result.[^s3] The system is also only as strong as its record-keeping. A 2015 GSA Inspector General evaluation of unlimited-warrant holders found one officer whose continuous-learning hours had largely been completed by a spouse; while improperly warranted between 2011 and 2013, that officer awarded five actions worth more than $13 million. Another officer's warrant reinstatement stalled for nine months because of system delays.[^s5]

## Example

Suppose a program manager tells a vendor to start extra work "and we'll sort out the paperwork later." Because the program manager holds no warrant, the instruction is an unauthorized commitment. The vendor is paid only if the agency ratifies it under the FAR criteria, or through the claims process, the risk *Merrill* places on anyone dealing with the government.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

The contracting officer is the central actor of **public procurement** and is **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** (Subpart 1.6), now being restated in the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul**.[^s1][^s2] The role **contrasts with** the **contracting officer's representative**, who monitors performance but cannot change contract terms.[^s1] Competence is tied to the related **FAC-C** certification required for each warrant level.[^s4] The limits of the role are **exemplified by** ***Federal Crop Insurance Corp. v. Merrill***, and they are a governance answer to the **principal-agent problem**.[^s3] Contracting officers exercise the judgment in **best-value tradeoff** source selections and place orders under **IDIQ** vehicles.[^s1]
