# Best-Value Tradeoff

The **best-value tradeoff** is the source-selection method US federal agencies use when paying more for a better proposal may serve the government's interest. Under it, an agency may award a negotiated contract to an offeror that is neither the cheapest nor the highest technically rated, provided it weighs cost or price against the non-price factors announced in the solicitation.[^s1] The governing condition is that the perceived benefits of a higher-priced proposal must merit the additional cost, and the rationale for each tradeoff must be written into the contract file.[^s1] It is one end of what the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) calls the "best value continuum."

## Origin and legal basis

The FAR frames best value not as a single procedure but as a continuum of approaches an agency may use alone or in combination. Where requirements are clearly definable and performance risk is minimal, cost or price may dominate; where requirements are less definitive, need more development or carry more performance risk, technical capability and past performance may dominate.[^s1] The tradeoff process (FAR 15.101-1) sits at the judgment-heavy end of that continuum, while lowest price technically acceptable (LPTA, FAR 15.101-2) sits at the price-driven end and forbids tradeoffs altogether.[^s1] In the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul model text for Part 15, the same method is renumbered as the "tradeoff approach" at 15.103-1, alongside LPTA, a new "highest technically rated with a fair and reasonable price" approach and phased acquisition; the list is expressly non-exhaustive.[^s2] The Department of Defense adopted the overhauled Part 15, with its DFARS Part 215 supplement, through a class deviation effective March 16, 2026.[^s5]

## How it works

Before proposals arrive, the solicitation must state every evaluation factor and significant subfactor that will affect the award and their relative importance. It must also say whether the non-cost factors, taken together, are significantly more important than, approximately equal to, or significantly less important than cost or price.[^s1] Evaluators then rate each proposal's strengths, deficiencies, significant weaknesses and risks against those factors.[^s2]

The source selection authority makes the award decision. Under the overhaul text, that decision must rest on the authority's independent judgment and a comparative assessment of proposals against all the criteria. It must document the business judgments and tradeoffs, including the benefits tied to additional cost, although the tradeoffs need not be quantified.[^s2] The tradeoff runs in both directions: an agency can pay a premium for a superior proposal, or decline to pay one because the superiority is not worth the price.

## Evidence and critiques

A Government Accountability Office (GAO) review of 2,851 new, competitively awarded Defense Department contracts over $1 million in fiscal year 2013 found that tradeoff and LPTA together accounted for about 93 percent of them, and that tradeoff was the most common process for services.[^s3] Among contracts of $25 million or more, however, the tradeoff share fell from about 69 percent in FY2009 to about 58 percent in FY2013 as LPTA rose from about 26 to 36 percent; officials attributed the attention to LPTA partly to tighter budgets.[^s3] Across 16 case studies, officials chose tradeoff when requirements were uncertain, innovation was sought or non-cost factors were needed to tell vendors apart, and LPTA when requirements and vendors were well understood.[^s3]

Congress has since narrowed the use of LPTA. Section 880 of the FY2019 National Defense Authorization Act limits civilian agencies to using LPTA only when listed conditions are met, and directs them to avoid it as far as practicable for knowledge-based work such as IT, cybersecurity and audit services.[^s1] DoD's 2026 deviation goes further, prohibiting LPTA outright for categories such as auditing services and certain protective equipment.[^s5] The method's weakness is the flip side of its flexibility: it rests on subjective judgment, so the written record becomes decisive, and protests of tradeoff awards typically turn on whether the decision explains why a rival's advantages were or were not worth their price.[^s4]

## Example

In *DRS Technical Services* (B-412070, December 2015), the Defense Information Systems Agency awarded a communications contract for deployed missions in Southwest Asia to General Dynamics Information Technology at an evaluated price of about $132 million. DRS, rated higher on technical subfactors, had an evaluated price of about $152 million. DRS argued the agency had effectively run an LPTA competition despite making non-price factors significantly more important than price.[^s4] GAO denied the protest because the 77-page decision acknowledged DRS's strengths and explained why they did not justify a premium of more than 15 percent, or nearly $20 million. GAO stressed that a selection official has broad discretion so long as the record explains the judgment.[^s4]

## Connections

The best-value tradeoff is a core **public procurement** practice **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** (Part 15) and, for defense buys, the **DFARS**.[^s1][^s5] It is an **alternative to** **lowest price technically acceptable** source selection and to the **highest technically rated with a fair and reasonable price** approach introduced in the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul**.[^s2] Agencies also use it to compete orders under **IDIQ** vehicles, and the tradeoff judgment and its documentation are exercised through the **contracting officer** and source selection authority.[^s2][^s4]
