# Bid Protest: Challenging Federal Contract Awards at GAO

A **bid protest** is the US federal government's mechanism for letting a disappointed or would-be bidder challenge how a contract is being competed or awarded. The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) defines it as a written objection by an *interested party* (an actual or prospective offeror whose direct economic interest would be affected by the award or failure to award) to a solicitation, its cancellation, an award or proposed award, or a termination tied to award improprieties.[^s1] Protests may be heard in three venues: the procuring agency itself, the Government Accountability Office (GAO), or the U.S. Court of Federal Claims (COFC); federal district courts have no bid protest jurisdiction.[^s1]

## Origin

GAO has decided protests for nearly a century, and it is the only body with government-wide authority to hear them administratively.[^s3] Congress put that role on a statutory footing in the Competition in Contracting Act (CICA) of 1984, enacted within the Deficit Reduction Act of 1984, which authorizes interested parties to protest to GAO.[^s3] The COFC is the only judicial forum Congress has authorized for protests.[^s3] The policy rationale is accountability: by giving losing bidders a cheap way to expose evaluation errors, protests aim to deter favoritism and keep firms willing to compete for public work.[^s5]

## How it works

Timeliness rules are strict. A challenge to defects in a solicitation must be filed before bids open or proposals are due; most other protests must be filed within 10 calendar days after the basis was known or should have been known.[^s1] An agency-level protest is meant to be informal and inexpensive, and the agency should try to resolve it within 35 days.[^s1][^s3]

At GAO, the agency must file a report within 30 days, the protester comments within 10 days, and GAO issues its recommendation within 100 days of filing (65 days under an express option).[^s1] The most consequential feature is the **automatic stay**: if GAO notifies the agency within 10 days after award, or within 5 days after a required debriefing, the contracting officer must suspend or terminate performance.[^s1] The head of the contracting activity can override the stay only with a written finding of urgent and compelling circumstances or that continued performance serves the government's best interests.[^s1] GAO may also recommend that the agency reimburse the protester's costs, including reasonable attorney fees, though not profit.[^s1]

GAO's recommendations are not legally binding, but agencies almost always follow them. The COFC, by contrast, issues binding, appealable rulings, reviews agency action under an arbitrary-and-capricious standard, and grants no automatic stay, so protesters must seek an injunction.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Protests are rarer than their reputation suggests. In fiscal 2025 GAO received 1,688 cases, down 6% from fiscal 2024, and issued 380 merit decisions, sustaining 53 (a 14% sustain rate).[^s2] Its broader "effectiveness rate", the share of closed protests in which the protester got some relief through a sustain or voluntary agency corrective action, was 52% in both years.[^s2] The most common grounds for sustains in fiscal 2025 were unreasonable technical evaluation, unreasonable cost or price evaluation and unreasonable rejection of a proposal.[^s2]

A RAND study commissioned by Congress found that fewer than 0.3% of Department of Defense contracts were protested at GAO (as of its 2017 data), and that poor post-award debriefings were a leading driver of protests; it advised improving debriefings and cautioned against shortening GAO's timelines.[^s4] Critics counter that some protests are filed to hobble a rival or extend an incumbent's work, and that the mere threat pushes agencies to over-document and to favor price-driven or existing contract vehicles.[^s3] An economic model by Francois Melese argues that protest systems can inadvertently undermine both of their goals, curbing corruption and maximizing competition.[^s5] Finance researchers at Penn State report that firms winning a protest face a 68% lower chance of receiving contracts from the contested agency over the following four years, suggesting a hidden cost to protesting.[^s6]

## In practice

The best-known case is the Air Force KC-X aerial refueling tanker competition. After the Air Force selected Northrop Grumman on February 29, 2008, Boeing protested on March 11; on June 18, 2008, GAO sustained the protest on seven grounds, including evaluation outside the solicitation's stated criteria and misleading, unequal discussions.[^s7] GAO recommended reopening discussions, re-evaluating revised proposals and reimbursing Boeing's protest costs.[^s7] The case shows how a protest can unwind a major award, and why contractors and contracting officers treat the evaluation record and debriefing as protest exposure.

## Connections

Bid protests sit within **public procurement** and are **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** (Part 33) and the **Competition in Contracting Act**, and they **depend on** the **Government Accountability Office** as the main forum.[^s1][^s3] They discipline the **contracting officer**'s source-selection judgment, especially in a **best-value tradeoff**, where sustains most often turn on evaluation errors.[^s2] RAND found task-order protests under multiple-award vehicles such as **IDIQ** contracts slightly more effective than other protests, and recommended reducing small-business protests partly through better debriefings, linking the topic to **small business set-asides**.[^s4] The **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul** rewrites the FAR text that protest timelines reference. The **KC-X tanker competition** **exemplifies** a sustained protest.[^s7]
