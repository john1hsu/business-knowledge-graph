# Federal Acquisition Regulation

The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) is the rulebook for US federal procurement. FAR Part 1 describes it as the primary document of a "FAR System" that codifies uniform acquisition policies and procedures for all executive agencies, with agency regulations implementing or supplementing it rather than replacing it. It is issued as Chapter 1 of Title 48 of the Code of Federal Regulations and applies to all acquisitions as defined in FAR Part 2 unless expressly excluded.[^s1] Its scale matters for business strategy: GAO reported that the government committed about $793 billion on contracts in fiscal year 2025, roughly $17.8 billion more than in FY 2024 after adjusting for inflation (published May 2026).[^s6]

## Origin and authority

Before the FAR, civilian agencies generally followed the Federal Procurement Regulations and the Department of Defense followed the Defense Acquisition Regulation, while agencies such as NASA kept their own semi-autonomous rules. According to the Congressional Research Service, the unified FAR was published in September 1983 and took effect in 1984, building on the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) Act of 1974 and its 1979 amendments, which pushed toward a single procurement system.[^s2] Legally, the FAR rests on 41 U.S.C. chapter 13 and is prepared, issued and maintained jointly by the Secretary of Defense and the Administrators of GSA and NASA.[^s1] Amendments in 1988 created the FAR Council, chaired by the OFPP Administrator, with DoD, NASA and GSA as members; changes go through notice-and-comment rulemaking, with the Defense Acquisition Regulations Council and the Civilian Agency Acquisition Council steering the drafting teams.[^s2]

## How it works

The FAR is organised into 53 parts: Parts 1–51 set policies and procedures across the acquisition life cycle, from planning and competition to contract administration, while Parts 52 and 53 hold the standard solicitation provisions, contract clauses and forms that are incorporated into actual contracts.[^s2] Agencies may issue supplements, codified in later chapters of Title 48; the best known is the Defense FAR Supplement (DFARS) in chapter 2. Supplements may not unnecessarily restate or conflict with the FAR except where law requires or a deviation is approved.[^s1][^s2] Deviations come in two forms: individual deviations affecting one contract action and class deviations affecting many.[^s1]

Authority is personal and delegated. Only warranted contracting officers may sign contracts for the government, they can bind it only within written limits, and they must treat contractors impartially, fairly and equitably.[^s1] The FAR's guiding principles aim at delivering best-value products and services on time while maintaining public trust, and they state that a practice not addressed by the FAR, and not barred by law, executive order or regulation, may be presumed permissible, which is an invitation to innovate inside the rules.[^s1]

The FAR does not reach everything the government spends. It covers executive departments and wholly owned government corporations buying with appropriated funds, but exempts the Federal Aviation Administration and the US Mint, does not bind legislative or judicial branch agencies, and does not govern grants, cooperative agreements or "other transactions."[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The main critique is complexity. Executive Order 14275 (signed April 15, 2025) said the FAR had grown to more than 2,000 pages and was excessive and overcomplicated. It directed the OFPP Administrator and FAR Council to amend the FAR within 180 days so that it keeps only provisions required by statute or essential to simplicity, efficacy or economic and national security, and to consider making retained non-statutory provisions expire after four years unless renewed.[^s3] GSA described the resulting "Revolutionary FAR Overhaul" (RFO) as the largest change to federal purchasing rules in 40 years; in 2025 OFPP and the FAR Council began with model deviations, starting with Parts 1, 10 and 34, and GSA drew more than 5,500 participants to an industry webinar.[^s4] Formal rulemaking followed: a September 2026 proposed rule covering Parts 9, 27 and 47, one of twelve proposed rules in phase two, would reorganise text by acquisition phase, rewrite it in plain language, move how-to guidance into non-regulatory resources and drop 33 clauses from Part 52.[^s5] Defenders of detailed rules argue they protect competition and integrity; the overhaul itself states that fair competition and fraud safeguards are meant to be preserved.[^s4]

## In practice

For firms selling to government, the FAR shapes nearly every commercial decision: which contract type is used, how offers are evaluated, which clauses flow down to subcontractors and when a firm can be debarred or suspended. The September 2026 proposed rule, for example, includes tables showing which clauses apply to commercial products, commercial services and commercial-off-the-shelf items, and revises the debarment and suspension rules in Part 9.[^s5] Because the Department of Defense accounted for more than half of the $755.1 billion in FY 2024 contract obligations, defence contractors must also master DFARS on top of the base FAR.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

The FAR is the governing instance of **public procurement** in the United States and is maintained by the **FAR Council**.[^s2] It replaced the **Defense Acquisition Regulation** and parallel civilian rules, and it is being succeeded in content by the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul**.[^s2][^s4] Its parts define the **government contract types**, the **best-value tradeoff** source-selection process, **IDIQ** vehicles and **small-business set-asides** treated in sibling nodes, while the **contracting officer** is the role through which its authority is exercised and the **DFARS** is its largest agency supplement.[^s1][^s2]
