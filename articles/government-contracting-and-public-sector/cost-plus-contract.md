# Cost-Plus Contract

A **cost-plus contract** is a cost-reimbursement arrangement in which the buyer pays the seller's allowable incurred costs and adds a separately negotiated fee. Under the US Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), such contracts set an estimated total cost, used to obligate funds, and a ceiling that the contractor exceeds at its own risk unless the contracting officer approves more money.[^s1] The defining feature is that the government, not the contractor, carries most of the risk that the work costs more than expected; the fee structure determines how much profit motive is left to control that cost.[^s1][^s2]

## Variants and how they work

FAR subpart 16.3 recognises several fee designs.[^s1] In a **cost-plus-fixed-fee (CPFF)** contract the fee is set at award and does not move with actual cost, which FAR itself describes as giving the contractor only minimal incentive to control cost. A CPFF contract can take a *completion* form, tied to a defined end product, or a *term* form, which buys a level of effort over a fixed period; FAR prefers the completion form when the work can be defined.[^s1] In a **cost-plus-incentive-fee (CPIF)** contract the fee is adjusted by a formula comparing actual allowable costs to a target, while a **cost-plus-award-fee (CPAF)** contract pairs a base fee, possibly zero, with an award amount set by the government's judgmental evaluation of performance.[^s1] Cost-sharing and no-fee cost contracts sit at the edge of the family, for partners who expect offsetting benefits such as research results.[^s1]

Fees are capped. A CRS overview reports that a CPFF fee generally cannot exceed 10 percent of estimated cost, 6 percent for architect-engineering services and 15 percent for experimental, research or development work.[^s2] The **cost-plus-a-percentage-of-cost** variant, where profit rises automatically with spending, is banned outright: CRS traces the bans to a 1949 civilian statute and a 1956 defense codification, and notes that contracts entered into by mistake in that form are void.[^s2]

## When it may be used

Because the buyer underwrites cost risk, FAR restricts the type to work whose requirements or costs cannot be estimated well enough for a fixed price, such as research and early development.[^s1] Use requires a written acquisition plan approved above the contracting officer, a contractor accounting system adequate to segregate contract costs, and enough government staff to oversee performance; the type is prohibited for commercial products and services.[^s1] FAR also says CPFF normally should not continue into major-system development once risk reduction has shown the objectives to be achievable.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Economic theory explains both the appeal and the cost of the form. Bajari and Tadelis model a buyer who must pay to complete a design and trade stronger cost incentives against the transaction costs of renegotiating when plans change; they find cost-plus contracts are preferred as projects become more complex, offering microfoundations for transaction cost economics.[^s5] The flip side is weak cost discipline: a National Research Council review of the Department of Energy found that decades of cost-plus-award-fee management and operating contracts delivered speed and flexibility in the Cold War, but fostered a culture in which neither the agency nor contractors were adequately accountable for cost and schedule.[^s6]

Award fees, meant to restore incentives, have often failed to. GAO's December 2005 review of Defense Department award- and incentive-fee contracts (FY1999-2003) estimated about $8 billion in award fees paid regardless of outcomes, with a median of roughly 90 percent of available fee paid. CPAF was the most common type in its sample, and about 60 percent of those had a zero base fee.[^s3] Programs with large cost growth, including the F/A-22 and the Joint Strike Fighter, still earned 91 and 100 percent of available award fee.[^s3] GAO reports that DOD implemented six of its seven recommendations, but not the one to pay award fees only for above-satisfactory performance.[^s4]

## Example

DOE's nuclear complex grew out of the wartime Manhattan Engineer District, whose research and construction contracts became cost-reimbursed management and operating contracts; the model let Hanford and Savannah River facilities be built in under five years but was later blamed for weak financial control and cost growth.[^s6]

## Connections

The cost-plus contract is a **part of** the menu of **government contract types** and **contrasts with** the **fixed-price contract**, which places cost risk on the seller.[^s1][^s2] It is **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** and **depends on** rules for **allowable cost**, which define what can be reimbursed.[^s1] Because the seller's effort is hard to observe, it is a textbook setting for the **principal-agent problem** and tends to **cause** **moral hazard**, which incentive and award fees try to mitigate.[^s3][^s5] **Contract theory** and **transaction cost economics** supply the rationale for when it beats fixed pricing.[^s5] The **contracting officer** must justify and oversee it, cost data behind its estimates fall under the **Truth in Negotiations Act**, and DOE's **management and operating contracts** are a prominent example.[^s1][^s2][^s6]
