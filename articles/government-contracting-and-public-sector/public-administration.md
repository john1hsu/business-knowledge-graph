# Public Administration: Implementing Public Policy Through Government Agencies

**Public administration** is the implementation of government policy, and in modern states it also includes a share in shaping that policy.[^s1] It covers the planning, organizing, directing, coordinating and controlling of government operations at national, regional and local levels, and the body of officials who do this work is usually called the civil service, distinct from the military, the judiciary and the police.[^s1] As an academic discipline it asks how public bureaucracies should be organized, staffed, financed and held accountable, which makes it the public-sector counterpart of management as studied in business schools.

## Origins

Organized administration is ancient. Britannica traces separate administrative hierarchies for justice, finance and military affairs to Rome, and a centralized, examination-based civil service in China from the Han dynasty, refined under the Tang and Sung and lasting until 1912.[^s1] Prussia's 1794 General Code required merit-based appointment and university training in cameralistics, the study of public finance and administrative law, while Britain moved from patronage toward open competition after Sir Charles Trevelyan's 1854 report and the creation of a Civil Service Commission in 1855.[^s1]

As a self-conscious American field, public administration is usually dated to Woodrow Wilson's essay "The Study of Administration," published in *Political Science Quarterly* in 1887.[^s2] Wilson treated administration as the practical, business-like side of government, the execution of public law, and argued it should be studied so that government could do its work efficiently and at low cost.[^s2] He held that politics sets the tasks of administration but should not manipulate its offices, and that Americans could borrow techniques from Prussian and French administration without adopting their autocratic politics, comparing this to learning how a man sharpens a knife without borrowing his intent.[^s2]

## How it works

The field's classic model of the public organization is Max Weber's bureaucracy: hierarchical authority, job specialization, formal rules, written files, impersonal treatment of clients, and hiring and promotion on merit.[^s3] To this the U.S. tradition added the ideal of "neutral competence," in which officials apply expertise without partisan loyalties.[^s3] Senior civil servants advise the political executives who own policy decisions and then organize their implementation, and they are customarily shielded from public blame for that advice.[^s1]

In practice agencies also make policy. Congress writes broad statutes, and agencies turn them into detailed rules that are proposed in the *Federal Register* and opened for public comment; street-level bureaucrats such as police officers then apply and enforce that policy in practice.[^s3]

Staffing rules are central to the field. In the United States the Pendleton Act of 1883 replaced the spoils system with competitive examinations and protection from dismissal for political reasons, enforced by a Civil Service Commission that initially covered about 10 percent of the federal workforce.[^s4] The Hatch Act of 1939 restricted federal employees' campaigning, and the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 abolished the Commission and split its work between the Office of Personnel Management and the Merit Systems Protection Board.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Wilson's separation of politics from administration became the field's founding doctrine and its most contested one. Survey research on city managers by Tansu Demir (2009) supports a "complementarity" view instead: politics, policy, administration and management sit on a continuum, elected officials and administrators share responsibilities, and administrators carry substantial policymaking roles.[^s6] Critics of the dichotomy argue that it is descriptively inaccurate and carries hidden normative assumptions about who should decide.[^s6]

A second wave of critique targeted Weberian bureaucracy itself. Christopher Hood's 1991 paper named the reforms adopted from the early 1980s, mainly in English-speaking countries, the "New Public Management" (NPM).[^s5] He identified seven doctrines: hands-on professional management, explicit performance standards, output controls, disaggregation of units, greater competition, private-sector management styles, and parsimony in resource use, combining managerialism with economic rationalism.[^s5] Later assessments questioned NPM's central assumption that stronger vertical managerial accountability would improve performance.[^s5]

## In practice

The U.S. federal service shows how the field's principles become institutions. Applicants to the Senior Executive Service must demonstrate executive core qualifications such as leading change, results orientation, business acumen and coalition building, which mirror private-sector leadership competencies.[^s4] Agencies themselves remain subject to rulemaking procedures, legislative oversight and merit-system protections that have no direct equivalent in firms.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Public administration sits under **political science** and **management**, applying management ideas to government settings.[^s1][^s2] It was shaped by Wilson's **1887 essay** and by **Max Weber**'s model of bureaucracy, and its staffing institutions form the **civil service**.[^s2][^s3][^s4] **New Public Management** is the 1980s reform movement within the field, and **public value** is a later framework for public managers. **Public procurement** and the **Government Accountability Office** are specialized parts of administering and overseeing government. Delegation from legislatures to agencies is a classic **principal–agent problem**, and the efficiency focus of early administration paralleled **scientific management** in industry.[^s2][^s5]
