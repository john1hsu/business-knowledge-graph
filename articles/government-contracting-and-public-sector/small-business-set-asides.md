# Small Business Set-Asides

A **small business set-aside** is a decision by a US federal buyer to let only small businesses compete for a contract, or for a defined portion of one. It is the main working tool behind the Small Business Act's directive that small firms receive a "fair proportion" of federal contracts, and it rests on a test known as the **rule of two**: if the contracting officer reasonably expects offers from two or more responsible small businesses at a fair market price, the work must be reserved for them.[^s1][^s2] For a business student it is a clear case of a buyer deliberately shaping market structure, trading some breadth of competition for broader supplier participation and policy goals.

## How it works

The FAR sets out two tiers. For purchases above the micro-purchase threshold but not above the simplified acquisition threshold, the acquisition is set aside for small business by default unless the contracting officer finds no reasonable expectation of two or more competitive small-business offers; if only one acceptable small-business offer arrives, it should win, and if none does the set-aside is withdrawn and the work resolicited openly.[^s1] Above the simplified acquisition threshold, the contracting officer must set the work aside when there is a reasonable expectation of offers from at least two responsible small firms and an award at fair market prices; market research and past acquisition history inform that judgment but do not settle it alone.[^s1] As of FY2026 CRS gives these thresholds as $15,000 and $350,000 for most contracts.[^s3]

Set-asides come in several forms. A **total** set-aside reserves the whole requirement, while a **partial** set-aside reserves a severable portion when a total one is not justified; construction cannot be partially set aside.[^s3] Above the simplified acquisition threshold, contracting officers must first consider the socioeconomic programs, namely the 8(a) business development program, HUBZone firms, women-owned small businesses and service-disabled veteran-owned small businesses, before a general small-business set-aside.[^s2][^s3] Eligibility depends on Small Business Administration size standards, measured by employees or average annual revenue by industry; firms generally self-certify in SAM, and the contracting officer accepts that claim unless it is protested or questioned.[^s3]

## Origin and scale

The rule of two began as administrative practice rather than statute: CRS traces it to the Navy in 1964, with the FAR extending it government-wide in 1984.[^s2] Its reach is large. In FY2025 set-asides and competitions restricted to small businesses totaled just over $116 billion, about 14.3 percent of federal contract obligations, out of more than $812 billion in spending.[^s3] The government-wide goal for small-business prime contracting is 23 percent, raised from the 20 percent floor set in 1988.[^s3] A live policy question is whether to extend the rule of two to task and delivery orders under multiple-award contracts, which an October 2024 proposed rule and bills in Congress would do.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Economists frame the set-aside as a restriction on who may bid, and the evidence on its cost is mixed. Using US Forest Service timber sales, Athey, Coey and Levin estimate that set-asides substantially cut efficiency and revenue while raising small-firm participation, and that a bid subsidy for small firms would recover nearly all of the efficiency loss while boosting their profits.[^s5] Nakabayashi's study of Japanese public construction reaches a different conclusion for that market: removing set-asides would push roughly 40 percent of small and medium firms out, and the lost competition would raise procurement costs by more than the efficiency gain.[^s6] The lesson is that the cost of a set-aside depends on whether the protected firms would otherwise stay in the market. Critics in government focus on administrative burden; the Veterans Affairs department argued that applying a rule of two to routine schedule orders would slow ordinary purchases.[^s4]

## In practice

The leading case is *Kingdomware Technologies, Inc. v. United States* (2016). The VA's own statute contains a rule of two favoring veteran-owned small businesses, and the department had treated it as optional once its annual veteran-contracting goals were met. A unanimous Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Thomas, held that the statute's "shall" is mandatory for every covered contract, including orders under the Federal Supply Schedule.[^s4] The case shows how a set-aside rule written as a duty, rather than a goal, can redirect large volumes of routine buying.

## Connections

Set-asides are a practice within **public procurement**, **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** (FAR Part 19) and grounded in the **Small Business Act**, with eligibility administered by the **Small Business Administration**.[^s1][^s2][^s3] The **contracting officer** makes the rule-of-two determination, and the proposed extension to orders links the practice to **IDIQ** and other multiple-award vehicles.[^s1][^s2] A set-aside decides who may compete, while source-selection methods such as **lowest price technically acceptable** decide how the winner is picked; set-asides sit beside **sole-source** awards as a separate small-business preference; and **bidder subsidies** are the main economic **alternative**.[^s3][^s5] *Kingdomware* is the defining **example** of a mandatory rule of two.[^s4]
