# Public Procurement

Public procurement is how the state buys. The OECD describes it as identifying what is needed, finding the best supplier, and making sure delivery happens at the right place and time for the best price, all done fairly and openly.[^s2] It runs as a cycle: needs assessment, competition, award, payment and contract management, followed by monitoring or audit. The OECD's framework applies at every level of government and to state-owned enterprises, so procurement is treated as a core governance function rather than a back-office purchasing task.[^s2]

## Scale

Procurement is one of the largest channels of public money. Across OECD countries it averaged 12.7% of GDP in 2023, down from 13.0% in 2021 mainly because GDP grew faster than procurement spending; in OECD-EU countries the figure was 14.8%.[^s1] It also made up 29.9% of total government expenditure in 2023, about the same as in 2019.[^s1] Health was the largest single function in 2023, at 29.7% of procurement spending, ahead of economic affairs (16.7%), education (11.4%), social protection (10.1%) and defence (9.9%).[^s1] Most buying is local: sub-national governments accounted for 62.5% of procurement spending in 2023.[^s1] The WTO puts typical government procurement at about 15% of GDP in both developed and developing economies.[^s4]

## Principles and rules

The OECD Recommendation on Public Procurement, adopted in 2015, is the main international policy benchmark.[^s3] It replaced a 2008 recommendation focused on integrity and sets out twelve principles: transparency, integrity, access, balance, participation, efficiency, e-procurement, capacity, evaluation, risk management, accountability and integration.[^s2] In practice these mean competitive tendering as the default, tenders that firms of any size can reach, a professional procurement workforce, data-driven evaluation, and links to budgeting and public finance.[^s2] The "balance" principle names a central tension: governments increasingly use procurement for secondary goals such as green growth or SME development, and the OECD asks them to weigh these against the primary goal of value for money.[^s2] The OECD reviewed how countries applied the Recommendation over 2020 to 2024.[^s3]

Trade law adds a cross-border layer. The WTO's plurilateral Agreement on Government Procurement (GPA 2012) opens covered procurement markets reciprocally among its parties, binding only those that join.[^s4] As of June 2025 it had 22 parties covering 49 WTO members (the EU and its 27 states count as one party), with covered procurement estimated at USD 1.7 trillion a year.[^s4] That year Timor-Leste became the first least developed country to begin accession.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Procurement is exposed to waste and corruption. The OECD calls it especially vulnerable to mismanagement, fraud and corruption, notably in large infrastructure projects, and notes that saving 1% would free about EUR 43 billion a year across OECD countries.[^s2] Research suggests much of the loss comes from incompetence rather than graft. Bandiera, Prat and Valletti studied Italian public bodies buying standardised goods. They found that some bodies consistently paid more than others for the same items and that the gaps tracked governance structure. Passive waste, which benefits no one, made up an estimated 83% of total waste, far more than active waste that benefits the official.[^s5] Best, Hjort and Szakonyi analysed 16 million Russian public purchases and found that individual bureaucrats and their organisations explain 39% of the variation in prices paid for narrowly defined items.[^s6] They also showed that a common policy, bid preferences for domestic suppliers, improved outcomes only where buyers were ineffective. This supports the critique that one-size-fits-all procurement rules ignore differences in administrative capacity.[^s6]

## In practice

Countries put these principles into national systems. Many centralise purchasing of standardised goods through national procurement agencies and framework agreements, and the OECD encourages tools such as e-catalogues, e-auctions and integrated e-procurement platforms to cut transaction costs and raise transparency.[^s2][^s5] The OECD's evidence that sub-national governments do most of the buying explains why capacity-building and shared platforms matter as much as headline legislation.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Public procurement is the parent field for the US-specific nodes in this domain. The **Federal Acquisition Regulation** is one national rulebook for it, and practices such as the **best-value tradeoff**, **IDIQ** vehicles and **small-business set-asides** are particular US answers to the OECD's efficiency, access and balance principles.[^s2] It is **regulated by** the WTO **Agreement on Government Procurement** across borders and shaped by the **OECD Recommendation on Public Procurement**.[^s2][^s4] The economics of procurement waste is an applied case of the **principal-agent problem**: public buyers spend money that is not their own, and the evidence on passive versus active waste shows that weak agent effort can cost more than agent self-dealing.[^s5][^s6]
