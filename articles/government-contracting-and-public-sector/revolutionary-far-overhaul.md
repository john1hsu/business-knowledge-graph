# Revolutionary FAR Overhaul

The **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul (RFO)** is the US federal government's attempt to rewrite the whole Federal Acquisition Regulation rather than amend it piecemeal. Acquisition.gov describes it as the first-ever comprehensive overhaul of the FAR, led by the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) and the FAR Council, with three stated aims: return the rules to their statutory basis, rewrite them in plain language, and remove most non-statutory requirements so that buying is faster, more competitive and produces better results.[^s2] It is a case study in large-scale regulatory simplification: what to keep in binding rules, what to move into guidance, and how to manage a transition while agencies keep buying.

## Origin

The mandate is Executive Order 14275, *Restoring Common Sense to Federal Procurement*, signed April 15, 2025. It states that the FAR had grown past 2,000 pages and sets the policy that the FAR should keep only provisions required by statute or essential to sound procurement.[^s1] Section 4 gave the OFPP Administrator and the FAR Council 180 days to amend the FAR accordingly; Section 5 required each agency to name a senior acquisition official, told OMB to issue implementation guidance within 20 days, applied the "ten-for-one" deregulation rule of Executive Order 14192 to agency supplements, and allowed deviations and interim guidance until final rules appear.[^s1] Section 6 asked the Council to consider a four-year sunset for retained non-statutory provisions unless they are renewed.[^s1] GSA, OMB, NASA and DoD launched the RFO website on May 6, 2025, promising streamlined FAR text that agencies could use quickly, category "buying guides" starting with software as a service, and public feedback to inform later rulemaking.[^s3]

## How it works

The overhaul runs in two phases. In phase one (fiscal year 2025) the FAR Council issued *model class deviation* text for each FAR part, which agencies could adopt through their own deviations before any formal rule changed.[^s4][^s5] Acquisition.gov maintains a guide listing the overhauled parts and each agency's deviations, plus non-regulatory "practitioner albums" (deviation summaries and line-out comparisons) and buying guides such as the FAR Companion, a living best-practice resource.[^s2] Phase two is formal notice-and-comment rulemaking: the Council describes twelve proposed rules that together streamline the entire FAR.[^s4][^s5]

The first proposed rules show the method. FAR Case 2026-001, published June 23, 2026, rewrote Parts 1, 2, 4, 33, 39, 40 and 53: non-essential but useful material moved to non-regulatory guidance, mainly the FAR Companion; text was reorganized by acquisition phase in active voice; a regulatory sunset process was added; and Part 53's forms content moved into Part 1, leaving Part 53 reserved.[^s4] In Part 4 alone it proposed removing ten provisions and clauses, including the annual representations and certifications provision, and consolidating others.[^s4] A September 18, 2026 rule covering Parts 14, 28, 36 and 52 removed six construction-related provisions and clauses, while stating that it expected no measurable direct cost savings from the Part 36 changes; it also cited SAM data that roughly 45 percent of FY2025 contract dollars were awarded without competition or with only one offer.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Because final rules were still pending as of November 2026, there is little outcome evidence yet; the government's own proposed rules frame benefits mostly as clarity and reduced burden rather than quantified savings.[^s5] The main practical critique is transitional confusion. In the Army's MAPS competition, a professional-services vehicle valued at about $50 billion, the solicitation mixed RFO class-deviation clauses with legacy FAR clauses, and four bidders protested.[^s6] GAO denied all four protests on September 21, 2026, finding the challenged clauses either unchanged or substantively identical, but it acknowledged that the deviation process can leave offerors unsure which version applies and noted that formal rulemaking so far had not reached Parts 8, 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16, which govern most federal buying.[^s6] Critics of deep deregulation also worry that rules removed as "non-statutory" were protecting competition or integrity; the FAR Council responds that it kept non-statutory requirements essential to sound procurement, such as taxpayer safeguards.[^s4] The launch announcement also listed removing DEI-related requirements among its goals, giving the effort a political dimension beyond efficiency.[^s3]

## In practice

For contracting officers and contractors, the RFO changes where answers live: binding rules shrink, while how-to material moves into guides such as the FAR Companion that are not mandatory.[^s2][^s4] During the transition a solicitation may cite deviation text for some parts and legacy FAR for others, so bidders need to check which version each clause comes from, as the MAPS protests showed.[^s6]

## Connections

The RFO is **preceded by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** it rewrites and sits within **public procurement** and the wider practice of **regulatory reform**.[^s1][^s2] It was **created by** the **FAR Council** and **OFPP** and **introduced in** **Executive Order 14275**.[^s1][^s2] Source-selection methods such as the **best-value tradeoff** (FAR Part 15) are so far changed only through deviations, not yet through formal rulemaking; agency supplements such as the **DFARS** must be streamlined alongside the FAR; the **contracting officer** is the practitioner who applies deviation text and the new guides; and the **MAPS** competition **exemplifies** its transitional clause problems, including protests over **small-business** effects.[^s1][^s2][^s6]
