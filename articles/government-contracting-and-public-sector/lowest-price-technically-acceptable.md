# Lowest Price Technically Acceptable

**Lowest price technically acceptable (LPTA)** is a way for a US federal agency to choose a winner in a negotiated competition. The agency publishes minimum requirements, checks each proposal only for whether it meets them, and awards to the acceptable proposal with the lowest evaluated price. Under FAR 15.101-2 it is the right tool when the agency expects best value to come from buying the cheapest offer that clears the bar, rather than from paying more for a better one.[^s1] It sits at the price-driven end of the FAR's best-value continuum, opposite the judgment-heavy tradeoff process, and is the textbook case of how a buyer's evaluation rule shapes what sellers offer.

## How it works

The solicitation must name the factors and significant subfactors that define acceptability and say plainly that award goes to the lowest evaluated price among proposals that meet them.[^s1] Non-price factors are scored pass/fail and never ranked, and the FAR states flatly that tradeoffs are not permitted, so a proposal that far exceeds the minimum earns no credit for doing so.[^s1] Past performance may be dropped as a factor if the contracting officer documents why; if kept, it is judged only as acceptable or not. When a small business is found unacceptable on past performance, the question goes to the Small Business Administration for a Certificate of Competency decision instead of ending the offer outright.[^s1] Discussions with offerors are still allowed.[^s1]

## Statutory limits

Congress has steadily narrowed when LPTA may be used. For civilian agencies, Section 880 of the FY2019 National Defense Authorization Act, now in FAR 15.101-2(c), allows LPTA only when six conditions all hold: requirements can be stated as performance objectives and standards; exceeding them adds little or no value; comparing technical approaches needs little subjective judgment; the agency is highly confident no proposal would show extra value; the lowest price reflects total cost, including operation and support; and the file documents the choice.[^s1] Civilian buyers must also avoid LPTA as far as practicable for knowledge-based work such as IT, cybersecurity, audit and health-care services, for personal protective equipment, and for some overseas training and logistics.[^s1]

The Defense Department follows its own, stricter rule. A DFARS final rule effective October 1, 2019, implementing Sections 813 and 822 of the FY2017 and FY2018 NDAAs, set eight criteria. Beyond the civilian list, it asks whether a different method would yield innovation or future technological advantage, and whether purchased goods are mainly expendable, nontechnical or short-lived.[^s2] It also bans LPTA outright for combat-critical protective equipment and aviation critical safety items, for engineering and manufacturing development of major defense programs, and for audit services.[^s2] DoD said the rule was not meant to stop LPTA use, and noted it could not fully gauge the effect on small firms because federal procurement data do not record which source-selection method was used.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Use is substantial but uneven. GAO estimated that about 26 percent of DoD contracts and orders of $5 million or more competitively awarded in FY2017 used LPTA, buying items from fuel and equipment to IT and construction services.[^s3] In a narrower review of the first half of FY2017, only 9 of 133 sampled IT and support-services contracts of $10 million or more used LPTA, and officials said they chose it for well-defined, recurring work where paying more would bring no benefit.[^s4] GAO also found that contracting officials generally weighed five of DoD's eight criteria but usually skipped three: a written justification, the expendable-goods test and full life-cycle cost. GAO recommended clearer guidance, which DoD issued in November 2021.[^s3]

The main critique is that a pass/fail bar removes the incentive to offer anything above it. A 2018 Naval Postgraduate School symposium paper surveys concerns that LPTA discourages innovation, can push higher-quality suppliers out of the defense market and was misapplied to complex, high-risk buys. It argues that budget-driven policy nudged contracting officers toward it.[^s5] The same paper shows how weak the evidence base is: because the main federal procurement database does not track source-selection method, outcomes of LPTA and tradeoff awards cannot easily be compared.[^s5] Defenders, including the contracting officials GAO interviewed, reply that for well-defined commodity work the government gains nothing by paying more than the lowest acceptable price.[^s4]

## In practice

GAO's examples show the intended fit: a Navy contract for commercially available monthly telephone maintenance and an Air Force contract for mail sorting and delivery, both simple, repeatable services with clear standards.[^s4] Under the Revolutionary FAR Overhaul, the model Part 15 text renumbers the method as the LPTA "approach" at 15.103-2 and keeps the Section 880 conditions and DoD carve-out in place.[^s6]

## Connections

LPTA is a **source-selection** method within **public procurement**, **regulated by** the **Federal Acquisition Regulation** and, for defense buys, the **DFARS**.[^s1][^s2] It is an **alternative to** the **best-value tradeoff** and to the overhaul's new **highest technically rated with a fair and reasonable price** approach.[^s1][^s6] Its total-cost condition ties it to **total cost of ownership**; the **contracting officer** must justify its use; its Certificate of Competency rule links it to **small-business** policy; the **Revolutionary FAR Overhaul** carries it forward; and it has been examined in repeated **GAO** reviews.[^s1][^s3][^s6]
