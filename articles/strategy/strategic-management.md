# Strategic Management

Strategic management refers both to a managerial activity and to the academic field that studies it. As an activity, OpenStax's *Principles of Management* defines it as the set of things managers do to position their firm to compete well in its marketplace, organized into five components: developing a vision and mission, analysing the firm and its environment, setting objectives, creating, choosing and implementing strategies, and measuring and evaluating performance.[^s1] As a field, it grew out of the business policy course taught at Harvard and was institutionalized as an empirical research discipline in the late 1970s.[^s3]

## Origin

Harvard taught business policy from 1912 as a capstone course on the problems of the firm as a whole that demanded a response from top management.[^s3] In the 1950s Kenneth Andrews, Edmund Learned and C. Roland Christensen rebuilt the course around the idea of strategy as its organizing principle, leading to the textbook *Business Policy: Text and Cases* (1965) and Andrews's *The Concept of Corporate Strategy* (1971).[^s3] In parallel, business historian Alfred D. Chandler Jr.'s *Strategy and Structure* (MIT Press, 1962) traced how environmental pressures pushed large American firms into new strategies, and how those strategies failed unless the organization was restructured; in four successful corporations of the 1920s the change meant adopting the multidivisional form.[^s4] Chandler's book is credited with shaping later strategy thinking even though its influence on the field was initially limited.[^s4]

The field took its present name when Dan Schendel and Charles Hofer organized a 1977 conference in Pittsburgh that called for an academic discipline grounded in empirical research; their edited volume *Strategic Management: A New View of Business Policy and Planning* followed in 1979.[^s3] Schendel became founding editor of the *Strategic Management Journal* in 1980 and founded the Strategic Management Society in 1981.[^s3]

## How it works

The OpenStax process model starts with **vision and mission**: the vision states broadly why the firm exists and what it hopes to achieve, while the mission says how it will pursue that vision, including which products it offers and which customers it serves.[^s1] **Strategic analysis** then supplies the information needed to craft strategy; a good strategy exploits the firm's resources and capabilities to set it apart from rivals in its external environment.[^s1] **Objectives** translate the mission into big-picture goals.[^s1]

Strategy is then formulated at several levels. Business-level strategy decides how a single business competes, through cost leadership, differentiation or focus; corporate-level strategy decides which industries a multi-business firm should be in, using tools such as the BCG matrix and acquisitions; international strategy decides which countries to operate in; and grand strategy sets the overall posture of growth, stability or defence.[^s1] **Implementation** runs through strategic, tactical and operational plans owned respectively by the CEO, unit managers and frontline managers, and **evaluation** at the end of each planning cycle feeds revised objectives into the next one.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The most influential critique concerns whether strategy is really made through this sequence. Henry Mintzberg and James Waters argued in 1985 that deliberate (intended and planned) and emergent (patterns that form without, or despite, intentions) strategies are two ends of a continuum, and they catalogued intermediate types such as umbrella, process, entrepreneurial and imposed strategies.[^s5] On this view, the formal process model describes only one way strategies are formed, and managers must also learn from patterns that emerge in action.[^s5]

Empirical support for formal planning is real but modest. A meta-analysis presented at the 2006 Academy of Management meeting pooled 39 studies covering 5,223 firms and found a positive overall association between strategic planning and firm performance (standardized mean difference 0.257).[^s6] The authors also showed that the result is sensitive to publication bias: after adjusting for likely missing studies, the estimate fell to 0.109 with a confidence interval spanning zero.[^s6] Results also varied with research design choices such as the performance measure, industry and how planning was defined.[^s6]

## In practice

OpenStax illustrates the cycle with Boxed, an online seller of bulk goods founded by Chieh Huang after he lost easy access to warehouse clubs. Environmental analysis revealed the opportunity, but planning drove growth: the company raised capital for distribution centres, developed private-label products and broadened supplier relationships, growing revenue from about $8 million to more than $100 million over three years (as reported in the 2019 edition).[^s2] Huang's stated approach, solving a real problem and then watching for external changes that act as tailwinds, mixes deliberate analysis with opportunism of the kind Mintzberg and Waters describe.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

Strategic management is a branch of broader **management theory**, preceded institutionally by the **business policy** tradition and given its modern academic form by **Dan Schendel**.[^s3] It was **influenced by Alfred D. Chandler Jr.**'s account of how a new strategy requires a matching structure.[^s4] The business-level choices it teaches (cost leadership, differentiation, focus) and its emphasis on analysing the external environment tie it to **Porter's five forces** and to the **industrial organization** economics behind such industry analysis.[^s1] Its parts include formal **strategic planning**, with supporting techniques such as **scenario planning** and the **BCG growth-share matrix**, and the **emergent strategy** that Mintzberg and Waters set against it.[^s1][^s5] Because the field treats a firm's resources and capabilities as the basis for standing apart from rivals, it is the parent of theories such as **dynamic capabilities**.[^s1]
