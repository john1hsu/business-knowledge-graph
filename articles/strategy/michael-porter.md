# Michael E. Porter

Michael E. Porter is a Harvard economist who, more than anyone else, turned business strategy into an analytical field built on industrial-organization economics. He holds a Harvard University Professorship, the highest honor Harvard confers on a faculty member, and leads the Institute for Strategy & Competitiveness that Harvard Business School and the university set up around his work.[^s1] His institute credits him with nineteen books and more than 130 articles and describes his work on industry structure, the value chain and positioning as foundational to the modern strategy field.[^s1]

## Career

Born in 1947, Porter trained first in aerospace engineering at Princeton, then took an MBA at Harvard Business School and a PhD in business economics from Harvard's economics department.[^s1][^s2] He joined the HBS faculty as an assistant professor in 1973, became associate professor in 1977 and full professor in 1982.[^s2] His honors include the Adam Smith Award of the National Association of Business Economists, the Academy of Management's top award for scholarly contribution, seven McKinsey Awards for the best *Harvard Business Review* article of the year, and 23 honorary doctorates (as listed by his institute in 2026).[^s1] He co-founded the strategy consultancy Monitor Group, which filed for bankruptcy in November 2012.[^s6]

## Core ideas on competition

*Competitive Strategy* (1980) and *Competitive Advantage* (1985) gave managers tools to analyze industries and to build and sustain superior performance.[^s2] His first HBR article, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy" (1979), restated and extended in 2008, argued that five forces (rivalry, buyer power, supplier power, and the threats of entrants and substitutes) determine how an industry's value is divided and thus its long-run profitability.[^s3] Strategy, in this view, means either defending against the forces or finding a position where they are weakest, and firms can sometimes reshape the forces themselves.[^s3] *Competitive Advantage* added the value chain, which breaks a firm into discrete activities to locate the sources of cost and differentiation.[^s1]

His 1996 HBR essay "What Is Strategy?" distinguished operational effectiveness, adopting best practices such as benchmarking and outsourcing, from strategy proper.[^s4] He called the fashionable belief that any position can be copied and every advantage is temporary a set of "dangerous half-truths," arguing instead for distinctive positions sustained by trade-offs and by activities that fit together.[^s4]

## Clusters, nations and society

*The Competitive Advantage of Nations* (1990) moved the analysis from firms to countries.[^s1][^s2] In "Clusters and the New Economics of Competition" (1998), Porter argued that location still matters in a global economy: geographic concentrations of linked companies, suppliers and institutions raise productivity, steer innovation and spur new business formation, advantages that distant rivals struggle to match.[^s5] His institute's Cluster Mapping Project measures this economic geography, and the so-called Porter Hypothesis of the early 1990s claimed that strict environmental standards can raise both profitability and competitiveness.[^s1]

Later work applied the same lens to social problems. With Elizabeth Teisberg he wrote *Redefining Health Care* (2006), launching value-based health care, measured as patient outcomes per dollar spent; with Mark Kramer he introduced creating shared value in 2011; and he helped design the Social Progress Index, first released in 2014.[^s1] He also co-founded nonprofits including the Initiative for a Competitive Inner City, FSG and the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

When Monitor collapsed, a 2012 *Forbes* column asked why its consultants could not save it with the five forces and accused Porter of a conceptual error: treating strategy as the pursuit of excess profit rather than a better product.[^s6] Kellogg strategy faculty mostly rejected the attack as a straw man. David Dranove noted the framework was never meant to be prescriptive; Meghan Busse likened it to a pilot's checklist that diagnoses threats but cannot by itself create advantage. David Besanko said Monitor's failure had nothing to do with the five forces.[^s6] Several also conceded that the framework is overused and only a starting point, since sustainable advantage requires well-fitted, hard-to-imitate activities.[^s6] A related critique, which Porter himself shares, is that industry structure is not fixed: firms can shape the forces as well as respond to them.[^s3]
