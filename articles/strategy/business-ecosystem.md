# Business Ecosystem: Co-Evolving Communities of Firms Around a Shared Value Proposition

A **business ecosystem** is a community of distinct but interdependent organizations that together deliver something none of them could deliver alone. The idea shifts the unit of strategic analysis from a single firm competing inside a single industry to a wider web of suppliers, complementors, partners and customers that cooperate and compete at the same time, often across industry lines.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

James F. Moore introduced the term in "Predators and Prey: A New Ecology of Competition" in *Harvard Business Review* (1993). Borrowing from biology, he argued that a company should see itself as part of an ecosystem spanning several industries, in which firms co-evolve around an innovation to meet customer needs and develop new products. Moore's central claim was that rivalry increasingly takes place between whole ecosystems rather than between individual firms, citing contests such as IBM's and Apple's personal-computer ecosystems and Wal-Mart's versus Kmart's in discount retailing. Managers, he said, should watch for new ecosystems forming and understand the stages through which ecosystems evolve.[^s1]

Marco Iansiti and Roy Levien extended the metaphor in 2004 with the idea of the **keystone**: a firm that stabilizes an ecosystem by providing common assets others build on, such as Wal-Mart's procurement system or Microsoft's Windows. A keystone first creates value through a platform and then shares it with members; one that hoards value may profit briefly but risks being abandoned. They proposed judging ecosystem health by productivity, robustness and niche creation, and pointed to WorldCom's failure as an example of a keystone's collapse damaging a whole supplier community.[^s4]

## How it works: two views

Ron Adner (2017) distinguishes two lenses. **Ecosystem-as-affiliation**, the Moore and Iansiti–Levien tradition, starts from actors and their network or platform ties and asks about access, openness and centrality. **Ecosystem-as-structure** starts from a focal value proposition and works backward to the activities, actors, positions and links needed to make it real. In the structural view, the value proposition, not the firm or the sector, draws the ecosystem's boundary, and the concept is only needed when the required relationships cannot be decomposed into separate bilateral deals.[^s2]

The key strategic variable is **alignment**: how far partners agree on their positions and on the flows among them. Participation is not alignment. Adner defines ecosystem strategy as the way a focal firm goes about aligning its partners, and he highlights two risks, *co-innovation risk* (whether partners can build what is needed) and *adoption-chain risk* (whether intermediaries will choose to adopt). His example is Michelin's PAX run-flat tire, which depended on independent repair garages changing their equipment and practices.[^s2]

Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer (2018) offer a theory of when ecosystems arise. They argue that modularity lets interdependent firms coordinate without full hierarchical control, and that ecosystems manage multilateral dependencies built on *non-generic complementarities*, both unique and supermodular. They also create standard roles under shared rules, so participants need not negotiate a bespoke contract with each partner. This sets ecosystems apart from markets, alliances and hierarchically managed supply chains.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical work shows that where a challenge sits in an ecosystem matters. Studying every firm that introduced a semiconductor lithography tool across nine technology generations from 1962 to 2005, Adner and Rahul Kapoor found that hard upstream component challenges amplified early movers' advantages, while hard downstream complementor challenges eliminated them, because slow adoption gave rivals time to catch up.[^s5]

The main critique concerns vagueness. Adner notes that because "ecosystem" is used so widely, it is often unclear how it differs from platforms, networks, business models or supply chains. Labels such as "the healthcare ecosystem" can create an illusion of focus that hides partners' inconsistent views, and the affiliation lens yields mostly general governance advice. He argues the concept adds value only when bounded by a specific value proposition.[^s2]

## In practice

eBay in the early 2000s illustrates keystone behavior: it gave sellers tools such as Turbo Lister, enforced standards through ratings and PowerSeller status, and kept commissions at or below about 7 percent, well under the 30–70 percent margins typical of retail, at a time when it reported more than 70 million buyers and sellers.[^s4] By contrast, many B2B marketplaces of the dot-com boom failed because their operating costs rose with usage, so they could not share value with participants.[^s4]

## Connections

Business ecosystems are a lens within **strategic management** that extends analysis beyond the firm's own industry.[^s1] They overlap with but are not the same as the **platform business model** and **two-sided markets**: a platform is often the keystone's shared asset, and **network effects** can help an ecosystem grow, but Adner treats platforms as one structure among several.[^s2][^s4] The emphasis on partners' contributions links to **complementary assets** and to **first-mover advantage**, whose value depends on where ecosystem bottlenecks lie.[^s5] **Dynamic capabilities** describe how a firm reconfigures itself as its ecosystem shifts. Jacobides and colleagues set ecosystems against **supply chain management**, in which a lead firm coordinates suppliers through hierarchy and contracts.[^s3] **Walmart** appears in both Moore's and Iansiti–Levien's accounts as an exemplar of ecosystem leadership.[^s1][^s4]
