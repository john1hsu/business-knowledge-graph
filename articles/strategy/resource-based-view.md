# Resource-Based View

The **resource-based view (RBV)** of the firm explains differences in firm performance by looking inside the firm: at the bundle of resources and capabilities it controls, rather than at the structure of the industry it competes in. Its central claim is that firms holding *strategic* resources, ones that rivals cannot easily obtain or copy, earn a competitive advantage that can persist over time.[^s2][^s6] The perspective is often called resource-based theory, and it became one of the dominant frameworks for explaining organizational performance from the 1990s onward.[^s5]

## Origin

Birger Wernerfelt gave the view its name in a 1984 *Strategic Management Journal* article that proposed analysing firms from the resource side rather than from the product side. He adapted two familiar product-market tools to resources: a "resource position barrier", modelled on entry barriers, and a resource-product matrix, modelled on growth-share portfolio matrices, and used them to show strategic options that a product lens hides.[^s1]

Jay Barney's 1991 *Journal of Management* article turned the idea into a testable theory of sustained advantage. Barney started from two assumptions: strategic resources are distributed unevenly across firms (heterogeneity), and those differences are stable over time because resources do not move freely between firms (immobility). He then proposed four attributes that determine whether a resource can underpin sustained competitive advantage: it must be valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and without strategically equivalent substitutes.[^s2]

## How it works

Textbook treatments separate **resources**, what a firm owns or controls, from **capabilities**, what the firm can do with them. Resources can be tangible, such as property, equipment and cash, or intangible, such as employee knowledge, reputation, brand and culture; intangible resources are more likely to be strategic because they are harder to buy or copy.[^s6] OpenStax describes resources as things a firm has to work with and capabilities as things it can do, such as delivering strong customer service or developing new products.[^s7]

In practice the theory is usually applied through the **VRIO** test, a later reformulation of Barney's criteria that replaces non-substitutability with organization. Each question narrows the outcome: a resource that is not valuable yields a competitive disadvantage; valuable but common yields parity; valuable and rare but easy to copy yields only a temporary advantage; and only a resource that is valuable, rare, hard to imitate and backed by an organization able to capture its value yields sustained advantage.[^s6] Commodity assets such as cash or vehicles rarely qualify because competitors can acquire them readily, although a bundle of individually copyable elements can be hard to copy as a whole.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The empirical record is broadly supportive. A 2008 meta-analysis by Crook, Ketchen, Combs and Todd pooled 125 studies covering more than 29,000 organizations and estimated a conservative correlation of 0.22 between strategic resources and performance. The link rose to 0.29 when the resources met the theory's criteria and performance was measured in ways not distorted by others appropriating the value created.[^s5]

The most cited critique came from R. L. Priem and J. E. Butler in the *Academy of Management Review* in 2001. They argued that the elemental RBV was a perspective rather than a full theory, that it assumed stable product markets and sidestepped how resource value is determined, that its definitions were too imprecise to yield managerial prescriptions, and that its static analysis left the causal path from resources to advantage a black box.[^s3] Barney replied in the same issue that the direct criticisms of his 1991 argument were unfounded, while accepting that they flagged real methodological requirements and naming further open questions for a fuller theory.[^s4] Their proposed agenda was to formalize the view, answer the causal how-questions, add a time dimension and integrate demand-side heterogeneity.[^s3]

## In practice

Analysts use the RBV for internal analysis, alongside external tools. In Kennedy's strategic management text, Southwest Airlines' culture passes every VRIO test and supports a sustained advantage because its history makes it hard to replicate, while the airline's on-time arrival capability is valuable and rare but imitable, so it offers only a temporary edge.[^s6] OpenStax applies the same test to Starbucks: its brand and customer service qualify as sources of advantage, but its large store network does not, because rivals such as McDonald's and Subway also operate thousands of locations.[^s7]

## Connections

- **Strategic management** and **competitive advantage** are the broader fields: the RBV is one of the two main answers to why some firms outperform others.[^s5]
- It **contrasts with Porter's five forces** and the **industrial organization** tradition, which locate profitability in industry structure; Wernerfelt explicitly reworked entry barriers into resource position barriers, and its portfolio tool echoes growth-share matrices built on the **experience curve**.[^s1]
- **Dynamic capabilities** extends the view to changing environments by focusing on how firms keep building new capabilities, the temporal gap that critics identified.[^s6][^s3]
- The **VRIO framework** is the RBV's main applied tool; **Southwest Airlines** is a standard teaching example.[^s6]
- It was **created by Birger Wernerfelt** and **Jay Barney**, and **introduced in** Wernerfelt's 1984 article.[^s1][^s2]
