# First-Mover Advantage

**First-mover advantage** is the idea that the firm that pioneers a new product market can build an edge over later entrants that lets it earn above-normal returns. The term was popularised by Marvin Lieberman and David Montgomery of Stanford, whose 1988 *Strategic Management Journal* article reviewed the theory and evidence on the mechanisms that can give pioneers either advantages or disadvantages, and drew out implications for managers.[^s1][^s2] Through the 1970s and early 1980s the benefits of pioneering were widely taken for granted by business schools, consultants and founders; the 1988 paper was as much a critique of that belief as a statement of it.[^s5]

## How it works

Theory identifies several sources of pioneer advantage. On the producer side, an early entrant can move down the learning or experience curve first and lower costs faster than imitators; it can hold a temporary monopoly in its niche; and it can preempt scarce assets such as raw materials, shelf space, distribution channels and attractive product positions.[^s4] On the buyer side, customers who adopt the first product may face switching costs that make them hard for later entrants to win, and a successful pioneer can shape how buyers judge a category's attributes and become its reference standard.[^s3][^s4] Scale economies and technological leadership can reinforce these effects.[^s3]

Against these sit **first-mover disadvantages**. Followers can learn from the pioneer's development, testing and pricing mistakes, cut their own risk and cost by reverse engineering, and enter once demand and technology are clearer. Rapid imitation narrows the window in which the pioneer earns its lead; in one study of new-product launches, the stock-market gains of first movers shrank once rivals imitated.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Early empirical support came largely from the PIMS database and similar surveys, which suggested pioneers held lasting market-share leads. Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis (1993) challenged this with a historical study of roughly 500 brands in 50 categories. They argued the earlier data suffered from survivor bias (failed pioneers were missing), relied on self-reported entry order, and asked whether a firm was "one of the pioneers" rather than the first.[^s3] In their data nearly half of pioneers (47%) failed, pioneers averaged about 10% market share against roughly 30% in earlier studies, and pioneers still led in only 4 of 36 categories. "Early leaders" that entered on average about 13 years after the pioneer did markedly better.[^s3]

Later work, summarised in a 2026 UCLA Anderson Review retrospective on Lieberman's research, sharpened the doubts: results that rely on market share rather than profit tend to flatter pioneers; Boulding and Christen (2003) found that being first lowered long-run profits on average; and Suarez and Lanzolla argued pioneers fare best when markets and technologies evolve slowly.[^s5] Lieberman and Montgomery's own 1998 and 2013 revisits stressed sample-selection bias, inconsistent definitions of "market" and "first mover," and disagreement over how long to wait before judging success.[^s5] The 1988 paper itself had been cited more than 6,500 times as of June 2026.[^s5] The field has since shifted from asking whether pioneering pays to asking when entry timing pays, with digital platforms a current focus.[^s5]

## In practice

Many firms celebrated as pioneers were not first. MITS sold a personal computer in 1975, years before IBM's 1981 entry; Chux disposable diapers (1950) preceded Pampers (1961); Ampex led video recorders before RCA and Matsushita; and Trommer's Red Letter light beer appeared about 14 years before Miller Lite.[^s3] Lieberman's own reading of the evidence is that first movers, on balance, do not do well: established companies often gain more as fast followers, while small start-ups may have to pioneer a market or niche to get a foothold, and luck plays a larger part than researchers would like.[^s5] For managers, the practical test is whether a specific mechanism (preemptable assets, steep learning, real switching costs) will actually protect an early lead before rivals can copy it.

## Connections

First-mover advantage is a candidate source of **competitive advantage** and is studied within **industrial organization** economics.[^s1] Its mechanisms overlap with **barriers to entry**: preempted assets, learning-based cost leads (the **experience curve**) and buyer **switching costs** are exactly what deter later rivals.[^s3][^s4] **Economies of scale** and, in platform markets, **network effects** can amplify an early lead, which is why platforms are a live area of entry-timing research.[^s3][^s5] The concept contrasts with a **fast-follower strategy**, in which a firm deliberately enters after the pioneer to exploit resolved uncertainty and the pioneer's mistakes.[^s4][^s5]
