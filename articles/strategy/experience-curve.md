# Experience Curve

The **experience curve** is the observation that the real cost of producing a product tends to fall by a predictable proportion each time the cumulative volume a firm (or industry) has produced doubles. The Boston Consulting Group (BCG), which popularized the idea, put the typical decline at 20 to 30 percent in real terms per doubling of accumulated "experience".[^s1] In strategy the curve became the classic argument for chasing market share: the firm that produces the most accumulates experience fastest and should therefore end up with the lowest costs.[^s1]

## Origin

The experience curve generalizes the older aircraft-industry **learning curve**. T. P. Wright's 1936 paper "Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes" in the *Journal of Aeronautical Science* launched the discussion of a cumulative-average formulation, in which the average cost of all units built so far falls by a constant percentage whenever total quantity doubles. The U.S. Government Accountability Office's cost-estimating guide still presents this form, written as a power function of cumulative units; with a 90 percent slope, if the first 10 units average $100, the first 20 average $90 and the first 40 average $81.[^s2]

BCG's contribution, under founder Bruce Henderson, was to extend the idea from direct labour to a product's total cost and to turn it into a strategy tool. According to BCG's own retrospective, the concept emerged from a 1966 cost study the firm carried out for a large semiconductor maker, and Henderson set out its strategic implications in BCG *Perspectives* essays in 1968 and 1974.[^s1] Abernathy and Wayne's 1974 *Harvard Business Review* article drew the same distinction: the learning curve tracks falling production expense as output grows, while the experience curve tracks the total costs of a product line declining over long periods as volume expands.[^s3]

## How it works

The usual statement is a constant "slope": an 80 percent curve means each doubling of cumulative output cuts unit cost to 80 percent of its prior level. Because the driver is cumulative output rather than calendar time, a firm that grows faster than rivals moves down the curve faster.[^s1][^s2] BCG attributes the decline to repetition and incremental improvement, both deliberate and tacit, in making existing products.[^s1] Its retrospective gives examples from technology markets: hard-disk-drive costs fell about 50 percent per doubling between 1980 and 2002, with the average cost per gigabyte dropping from $80,000 in 1984 to $6 in 2001, and laser diode costs fell 40 to 45 percent per doubling.[^s1]

The strategic logic follows directly. If costs depend on accumulated volume, the share leader holds a self-perpetuating cost edge, so BCG advised clients through the 1970s to invest and price aggressively to win share early, accepting lower margins now for lower costs later.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The underlying regularity has strong empirical support. Marvin Lieberman's 1984 study of 37 chemical products found a strong, consistent learning effect driven by cumulative industry output and cumulative investment rather than the passage of time; conventional scale economies were significant but small by comparison, and R&D spending and capital intensity explained differences in slopes.[^s4] Nagy, Farmer, Bui and Trancik (2013) tested six proposed laws of technological progress against cost and production data for 62 technologies and found that Wright's law, cost as a power law of cumulative production, forecast best, with Moore's law a close second; because production usually grows exponentially, the two are hard to tell apart.[^s6]

The strategic prescriptions have fared worse. Day and Montgomery (1983) argued in the *Journal of Marketing* that early applications rested on simplistic market-share rules that led to disillusionment, and that the curve misleads unless measurement and interpretation problems are addressed; they saw it as useful mainly where scale, technology and learning effects strongly shape a market.[^s5] Lieberman's evidence that learning tracks *industry* output also implies that knowledge can spill over to rivals, weakening the idea of a proprietary cost lead.[^s4] Abernathy and Wayne warned that relentless cost reduction along the curve undermines a firm's ability to innovate and to respond to competitors' innovations.[^s3] BCG itself concluded in 2013 that in volatile industries with frequent product launches, experience in fulfilling demand must be paired with experience in shaping it, citing firms such as Netflix and Facebook that the classic curve cannot explain.[^s1]

## Real-world example

Hard-disk drives show how steep the curve can be in a fast-growing technology market. BCG reports that drive costs fell roughly by half with each doubling of cumulative output from 1980 to 2002, taking average cost per gigabyte from $80,000 in 1984 to $6 in 2001.[^s1] Abernathy and Wayne's caution applies to any such market: managers cannot fully capture a steep curve's savings while also pursuing rapid product innovation, so riding the curve is a strategic commitment rather than a free option.[^s3]

## Connections

The experience curve is a source of **competitive advantage** and a determinant of a firm's **cost structure**.[^s1] It is distinct from **economies of scale**: scale lowers cost through the size of current output, whereas experience lowers it through cumulative output, and Lieberman's data separate the two effects.[^s4] Learning-cost leads are one mechanism behind **first-mover advantage** and can act as a **barrier to entry** when the leader's experience cannot easily be copied.[^s1][^s4] The concept was created at BCG by **Bruce Henderson** and builds on Wright's **learning curve**.[^s1][^s2] The **hard-disk drive industry** exemplifies its steep cost declines, and BCG's call to balance demand-fulfilling and demand-shaping experience links it to **organizational ambidexterity**.[^s1][^s3]
