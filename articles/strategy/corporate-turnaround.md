# Turnaround Strategy: Retrenchment and Recovery in Declining Firms

A corporate turnaround is the reversal of a firm's slide into distress. The definition most researchers still use goes back to Dan Schendel and colleagues in 1976, who described a turnaround as a period of decline that is followed by recovery.[^s1] Turnaround strategy is the managerial side of that pattern: the choices about what to cut, what to fix, whom to replace and where to grow so that a failing company becomes viable again.[^s1]

## Origin and the two-stage model

The dominant framework comes from D. Keith Robbins and John A. Pearce II, whose 1992 *Strategic Management Journal* article "Turnaround: Retrenchment and recovery" studied a single industry, U.S. textiles, to examine how retrenchment fits into the wider turnaround process.[^s2][^s3] They offered an operational definition of a turnaround situation, separated internal from external causes of decline, measured how severe the decline was, and distinguished retrenchment used on its own from retrenchment used as the opening phase of a fuller strategy.[^s2] They also classified the recovery that follows as either efficiency-oriented or entrepreneurial.[^s2] Pearce and Robbins later condensed Donald Bibeault's five-step account into four steps: turnaround situation, retrenchment response, recovery response and turnaround success.[^s1]

## How it works

In the **retrenchment** phase the firm tries to stop the bleeding by cutting costs and shedding assets; because any recovery needs cash, retrenchment is often treated as a precondition for what follows.[^s1] The **recovery** phase turns to growth: new products, markets or acquisitions intended to restore profitability on a sustainable footing.[^s1] Charles Hofer's 1980 work added a widely taught distinction between operating turnarounds, which do things right, and strategic turnarounds, which do the right things, and suggested a rule of thumb: firms close to break-even should mainly retrench, while firms far below it need portfolio restructuring and revenue-raising moves.[^s1]

Lars Schweizer and Andreas Nienhaus, reviewing more than 250 publications, argue that the operating-versus-strategic split is too coarse and sort turnaround actions into four content groups: portfolio, financial, operational and management replacement.[^s1] Replacing the CEO is justified by agency arguments and by the idea that threatened incumbents grow rigid, yet the evidence is mixed: replacement tends to help when it comes early, helps less after years of distress, and can be undermined if the ousted chief stays on the board.[^s1] Stakeholders shape the outcome too, since creditors gain power in distress and can steer asset sales and debt renegotiation.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Whether retrenchment actually causes recovery is the field's best-known dispute. Vincent Barker and Mark Mone re-examined Robbins and Pearce's 32 textile firms and concluded there was little evidence that retrenchment is integral to turnaround; deep cuts might simply be a consequence of steep decline.[^s3] Pearce and Robbins replied that the critics misrepresented their study and relied only on secondary data with unusual definitions, and maintained that retrenchment is the foundation of a two-stage process.[^s4] Later findings on specific levers remain contradictory: layoffs are linked to savings and survival in some studies but to lost creativity, commitment and reputation in others, and results on leverage, capital-spending cuts and dividend cuts are inconclusive.[^s1] Critics also fault the literature's heavy reliance on accounting measures and its lack of agreement on when a turnaround starts or counts as a success.[^s1] Success is uncommon: one estimate cited by Schweizer and Nienhaus puts successful turnarounds below 10%, and Edith Hotchkiss found in 1995 that more than 40% of Chapter 11 firms still posted operating losses in the three years after the process.[^s1] Cheryl Trahms, Hermann Ndofor and David Sirmon's 2013 review in the *Journal of Management* called the field fragmented and argued that managerial cognition, strategic leadership and stakeholder management, plus complex resource-based actions, go beyond the two-stage model.[^s5]

## Example: IBM, 1993-2002

When Louis Gerstner became IBM's CEO in 1993, the company had posted an $8 billion loss, its share price had fallen from $43 in 1987 to about $12, and more than 90% of its profit depended on a shrinking mainframe business.[^s6] He cut mainframe prices, halted plans already under way to split IBM into separate units, tied executive pay to total corporate results, and pushed the firm toward integrated solutions and services; IBM earned about $8 billion in 2001.[^s6] Knowledge at Wharton notes that critics regard the recovery as somewhat overstated, pointing to a pension-plan change and a large share buyback.[^s6]

## Connections

Turnaround strategy sits under **strategic management** and **change management**, since it combines a strategic repositioning with a forced organizational change.[^s1] It is the response that **mitigates corporate decline**, while **strategic renewal** describes the broader, often pre-emptive refreshing of a firm's capabilities.[^s1][^s5] It was **introduced in** Robbins and Pearce's 1992 article, is **exemplified by** the **IBM turnaround**, and draws on **threat rigidity** and **agency costs** to explain why leaders are replaced.[^s2][^s6][^s1] **Downsizing** and **financial distress** are related topics because layoffs and creditor bargaining are core turnaround levers.[^s1]
