# Consumer Switching Costs

**Switching costs** are the costs a buyer bears when it changes from its current supplier to a competitor. Farrell and Klemperer define a switching cost as an investment specific to the current seller that must be duplicated when the customer buys from someone new, covering things like equipment, relationship set-up, learning and psychological attachment.[^s2] Michael Porter's industry-structure note describes them more simply as fixed costs buyers face when changing suppliers, such as rewriting product specifications, retraining staff or building new procedures and systems.[^s3] Because a customer who is already "locked in" will tolerate a somewhat higher price before leaving, switching costs turn today's market share into tomorrow's profit.[^s1]

## Origin

The economics of switching costs is a branch of industrial organization. Paul Klemperer's 1995 survey in the *Review of Economic Studies* synthesized the field, arguing that a firm's current share is an important determinant of its future profitability and that firms therefore trade off low prices to win customers against high prices to exploit locked-in ones. The survey extended the idea to entry deterrence, price wars, product choice, macroeconomic pricing over the business cycle and international trade, and used switching costs to help explain why multi-product firms exist.[^s1] Farrell and Klemperer later treated switching costs and network effects together as two forms of "lock-in" in a survey first drafted in 1997 and later published in the *Handbook of Industrial Organization*.[^s2]

## Types

Farrell and Klemperer distinguish learning costs, paid once, from transaction costs, which recur each time a customer switches back. Contractual or pecuniary switching costs, such as frequent-flyer programs and loyalty rebates, are created by firms and are not real social costs. Incompatible follow-on goods such as spare parts and repairs create aftermarket lock-in, and search and shopping costs behave similarly.[^s2] Marketing research takes a customer-perception view: Burnham, Frels and Mahajan (2003) grouped switching costs, as Caruana summarizes them, into informational costs (economic risk, evaluation, learning, set-up), contractual costs (lost benefits, financial loss) and relational costs (lost personal and brand relationships), eight facets in all.[^s4]

## How it works

The signature pricing pattern is "bargain-then-ripoff." Firms price low, even below cost, to attract unattached buyers, then charge more once those buyers are locked in. In the simplest model, competition for new customers passes the later rents back through the early discounts, so lifetime prices track lifetime costs. Real markets break that result: firms that cannot tell old from new customers settle on intermediate prices, large incumbents behave as less aggressive "fat cats," and rents can be dissipated on wasteful marketing.[^s2] In Porter's five forces, switching costs raise the barrier facing entrants, strengthen suppliers whose customers have invested in their equipment (Bloomberg terminals and computer-aided design software are his examples), and weaken buyers who cannot easily play vendors against each other.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical work cited by Farrell and Klemperer finds switching costs are large in practice: average implicit costs of about $3.43 for households switching breakfast-cereal brands, higher than any brand's price, and about 4.12% of the loan for Norwegian bank borrowers, with smaller customers facing more.[^s2] Survey evidence from corporate mobile-phone customers links relational switching costs most strongly to loyalty.[^s4] The authors' overall reading of the theory is that switching costs usually raise prices, but they stress caveats: effects on collusion are ambiguous, markets can look more competitive than they are because introductory prices are low, and most empirical studies are static.[^s2] The marketing evidence also suggests that not all switching costs are equal: relational switching costs were the type most strongly linked to loyalty in Caruana's sample.[^s4]

## In practice

Regulators reduce switching costs when lock-in limits competition. In the United States, wireless local number portability let subscribers keep their numbers when changing carriers in the same area, starting in November 2003 in the 100 largest metropolitan areas and May 2004 elsewhere.[^s6] Farrell and Klemperer cite evidence that portability for U.S. toll-free numbers led the largest firm to cut prices.[^s2] In cloud computing, the EU Data Act (applying since 12 September 2025) allows providers to recover switching and data-egress costs only during a transition that ends on 12 January 2027, after which such charges must be removed entirely.[^s5] Firms, meanwhile, deliberately raise switching costs; Porter cites Intel's "Intel Inside" campaign, which made it harder for PC makers to move to rival chip suppliers.[^s3]

## Connections

Switching costs are one of the classic sources of barriers to entry and form part of the entrant, buyer and supplier forces in Porter's five forces.[^s3] They are a core topic of industrial organization, and economists treat them alongside network effects as the two main sources of lock-in.[^s2] Because current share drives future profit, they underpin first-mover advantage,[^s1] and aftermarket lock-in through incompatible follow-on goods is the logic behind the razor-and-blades model.[^s2] Because switching costs bolster customer loyalty, they also feed customer lifetime value.[^s4] The EU Data Act is a current example of regulation aimed directly at them.[^s5]
