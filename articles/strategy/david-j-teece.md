# David J. Teece

David J. Teece is an economist and strategy scholar at the Haas School of Business, University of California, Berkeley, where he has held the Thomas W. Tusher Chair in Global Business and directed the Tusher Initiative for the Management of Intellectual Capital.[^s1] His two signature contributions are the "profiting from innovation" (PFI) framework, which asks who captures the value an innovation creates, and the dynamic capabilities view of strategy, which asks how firms keep creating value when markets and technologies shift.[^s2] He has written more than 200 books and articles and co-edited the journal *Industrial and Corporate Change*.[^s1]

## Career

Teece studied at the University of Canterbury in New Zealand (BA 1970, MComm 1971) and earned his economics PhD at the University of Pennsylvania in 1975.[^s4] He was an associate professor at Stanford's Graduate School of Business from 1978 to 1982 before moving to Berkeley, where he directed the Center for Research in Management (1983–1994) and then the Institute of Management, Innovation and Organization (1994–2008).[^s4] Alongside academic work he built economics consultancies: he co-founded LECG in 1988 and Berkeley Research Group in 2010, and has testified on antitrust and intellectual-property questions before federal and state courts, Congress and the Federal Trade Commission.[^s4]

## Profiting from innovation

Teece's 1986 *Research Policy* article (vol. 15, no. 6) argued that innovators frequently fail to capture the returns from their inventions, while imitators, customers and the owners of complementary assets such as manufacturing, distribution and service capacity reap the gains.[^s2] The innovator's share depends on the appropriability regime (how easily the know-how can be copied and how well intellectual property protects it), on whether a dominant design has emerged, and on who controls the scarce complementary assets; where protection is weak, an innovator may need to own or contract for those assets early.[^s2] Berkeley's research page describes the paper as the journal's most cited, and *Research Policy* marked its twentieth anniversary with a special issue in 2006.[^s2]

In a 2018 *Research Policy* paper Teece extended PFI to digital and wireless industries, arguing that patents are not self-enforcing, that licensing on its own rarely suffices, and that technological innovation therefore usually needs a matching business-model innovation.[^s5] He illustrates the point with contrasting value-capture models: Apple earns mainly from devices and app revenue, Google from advertising, and ARM from royalties on chip designs.[^s5]

## Dynamic capabilities

With Gary Pisano and Amy Shuen, Teece published "Dynamic capabilities and strategic management" in the *Strategic Management Journal* in 1997.[^s3] The paper holds that advantage in fast-moving markets rests on a firm's distinctive organizational and managerial processes, shaped by its asset position and its historical path, and that its durability depends on how hard those capabilities are to imitate.[^s3] It concluded that sensing new opportunities and organizing to pursue them matters more than maneuvers aimed at blocking rivals, a direct challenge to positioning-based strategy.[^s3] Teece's 2007 follow-up, "Explicating Dynamic Capabilities," decomposed the idea into microfoundations for sensing, seizing and reconfiguring.[^s2] ScienceWatch ranked the 1997 paper the most cited article in economics and business for 1995–2005.[^s2]

## Theory of the firm and antitrust

Earlier work on economies of scope (1980) and the multiproduct firm (1982) explained why firms diversify and internalize know-how, extending transaction cost economics with evolutionary ideas about path dependence and heterogeneous firm endowments.[^s1][^s2] In competition policy he has favoured dynamic analysis, skepticism of per se rules and caution about agency intervention, arguing that monopoly power can be fragile in innovative markets.[^s2]

## Influence and critiques

A 2020 *PLOS Biology* citation analysis ranked Teece the most cited scholar in business and management, and he entered the Thinkers50 Hall of Fame the same year.[^s4] Yet the empirical case for his best-known idea remains contested: a review of 92 quantitative studies found that the link between dynamic capabilities and firm performance is still unclear, partly because studies define and measure the capabilities in divergent ways, and that the most promising evidence is indirect, running through change and intermediate outcomes.[^s6]

## Connections

Teece sits under `kt:strategic-management` as the originator of `kt:dynamic-capabilities`, which builds on the `kt:resource-based-view` while stressing change rather than static resource endowments.[^s3] His PFI framework introduced the ideas of appropriability regimes and `kt:complementary-assets`, central to technology strategy and to how platform firms capture value.[^s2][^s5] His theory-of-the-firm work draws on `kt:transaction-cost-economics`, and his antitrust advocacy places him in `kt:industrial-organization` and debates over `kt:antitrust-law`.[^s1][^s2] He is often read alongside `kt:clayton-christensen`: both study why incumbents and pioneers win or lose from technological change.[^s2]
