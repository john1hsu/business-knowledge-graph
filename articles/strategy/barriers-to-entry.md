# Barriers to Entry

**Barriers to entry** are the conditions that keep potential competitors out of a market or make entering it unprofitable. OpenStax describes them as legal, technological or market forces that discourage or prevent rivals, ranging from trivial obstacles such as the cost of renting retail space to near-absolute ones such as owning every available radio frequency. Where barriers are high, above-normal profits fail to draw in new firms, so market power can persist.[^s2] For strategists the concept explains why some industries stay profitable for decades while others see returns competed away.[^s3]

## Origin and definitions

The concept is closely tied to Joe Bain's work in the 1950s. As summarized by the OECD, Bain (1956) treated a barrier as anything that lets incumbents persistently earn above-normal profits without attracting entry, with "persistently" meaning a period long enough for existing capital to be replaced. George Stigler (1968) proposed a narrower test: a barrier is a cost that an entrant must bear but that incumbents did not. Under Stigler's view, scale economies are not barriers if every firm can access the same technology. Later writers such as von Weizsäcker narrowed it further to cost asymmetries that also reduce social welfare, and Demsetz used taxi medallions to show a case that both classic definitions miss.[^s1] The OECD's 2005 roundtable concluded there is still no agreed definition and that most competition agencies manage without a fixed one.[^s1]

## Types of entry barriers

Michael Porter's industry-structure framework lists seven sources of barriers: supply-side economies of scale; demand-side benefits of scale (network effects); customer switching costs; capital requirements, especially unrecoverable spending on advertising or R&D; incumbency advantages independent of size, such as proprietary technology, prime locations or accumulated experience; unequal access to distribution channels; and restrictive government policy such as licensing.[^s3] Entrants also weigh expected retaliation, which is more likely when incumbents have fought past entrants, hold excess cash or capacity, or face slow industry growth.[^s3]

Economists group these into *structural* barriers, which come from cost and demand conditions, and *strategic* barriers, which incumbents deliberately create or reinforce, for example through limit pricing or exclusive dealing.[^s1] OpenStax adds control of a scarce physical resource (Alcoa's hold on bauxite in the 1930s), legal monopolies (the U.S. Postal Service's exclusive right to deliver first-class mail), intellectual property such as 20-year U.S. patents, and intimidation through predatory pricing or heavy brand advertising.[^s2]

## Mechanism: why sunk costs matter

The OECD's background note stresses sunk costs. An incumbent that has already sunk its investment can ignore it when pricing after entry, while an entrant still has to commit that money; the entrant therefore generally needs to be considerably more efficient than the incumbent to enter profitably. Under contestability logic, scale and scope economies only deter entry when combined with sunk costs, although in practice sunk costs usually generate those economies.[^s1] Where entry and exit are costless and immediate, a perfectly contestable market performs competitively even with one firm, but such conditions rarely exist.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The definitional debate has been sharp. McAfee, Mialon and Williams (2004) compared competing economic definitions and proposed a classification to resolve the confusion, applying it to scale economies and sunk costs.[^s4] Dennis Carlton argued in the same year that the standard definitions lack a time dimension and need refinement before they are useful in antitrust or regulatory cases.[^s5] Policymakers responded by asking not whether a barrier exists in the abstract but whether entry would be likely, timely and sufficient; many agencies treat two years as a benchmark for timely entry.[^s1] Porter warns managers against related errors: overstating capital requirements when capital markets will fund attractive opportunities, assuming visible barriers are permanent, and judging barriers in the abstract rather than against the capabilities of likely entrants.[^s3]

## In practice

U.S. merger review applies the concept directly. Under the DOJ and FTC 2023 Merger Guidelines (released December 2023), merging firms that argue new entry will offset lost competition must show it would be timely, likely and sufficient in magnitude, character and scope. A history of failed or absent entry suggests entry will be slow or difficult, and entry that requires participation at several market levels at scale is treated as especially hard.[^s6] In strategy work, Porter cites European low-cost airlines, which bypassed travel agents that favored established carriers by selling tickets online, as a case of entrants getting around a distribution barrier.[^s3]

## Connections

Barriers to entry are a feature of market structure and a core subject of industrial organization, where Bain's work made them central.[^s1] They form the "threat of new entrants" force in Porter's five forces.[^s3] Several sources of barriers have their own nodes: economies of scale on the supply side, network effects on the demand side, and switching costs on the customer side.[^s3] The concept contrasts with contestable markets, the theory that markets with costless entry and exit behave competitively regardless of concentration.[^s1]
