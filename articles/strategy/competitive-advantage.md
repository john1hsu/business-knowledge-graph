# Competitive Advantage: Why Some Firms Persistently Outperform Rivals

**Competitive advantage** is the central outcome that business strategy tries to produce. In textbook terms, a firm holds one when it attracts more customers, earns more profit or returns more value to shareholders than its rivals, and it gets there either by adding more value to its offerings or by driving its costs down more effectively than competitors do.[^s1] Michael Porter gave the idea its now-standard economic form: a company outperforms rivals only if it establishes a difference it can preserve, delivering greater value to customers at comparable cost, comparable value at lower cost, or both.[^s2]

## Origin and core logic

Porter's work in the 1980s made competitive advantage the organizing concept of the strategy field, alongside his Five Forces account of industry structure and his three generic strategies of cost leadership, differentiation and focus.[^s1] In his 1996 Harvard Business Review article he traced both cost and price back to the many discrete activities a firm performs, so advantage is ultimately built from activity choices rather than from slogans or aspirations.[^s2] A cost leader keeps every stage of its value chain lean so it can profit at a lower price; a differentiator invests in quality, features, service or brand so customers will pay a premium that more than covers its higher costs.[^s1]

Porter also drew a sharp line between **operational effectiveness**, performing similar activities better than rivals, and **strategic positioning**, performing different activities or similar ones in different ways.[^s2] Best practices such as benchmarking and quality programs raise productivity, but rivals copy them quickly, so they rarely yield lasting advantage; durable advantage comes from trade-offs (choosing what not to do) and from fit, where activities reinforce one another so that a rival must replicate a whole system rather than one piece.[^s2]

## Sustainability: the resource view

Jay Barney's 1991 paper shifted attention from industry position to the firm's internal resources. It assumes that strategic resources are unevenly distributed across firms and that these differences can persist, and it proposes that a resource supports **sustained** advantage when it is valuable, rare, hard to imitate and lacks strategically equivalent substitutes.[^s3] That test, later packaged as the VRIO framework, explains why an advantage built on a widely available asset fades while one rooted in culture, history or tacit know-how can last.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical work supports the premise that firm-level differences matter. Richard Rumelt's 1991 variance decomposition of returns across FTC Line of Business units found large, stable business-unit effects, small but stable industry effects and negligible corporate-parent effects, implying that most economic rents arise from business-specific sources rather than industry membership.[^s4] That finding challenged a purely industry-structure view and gave weight to the resource-based explanation of advantage.[^s4]

The main critique concerns durability. Using two large longitudinal samples, Robert Wiggins and Timothy Ruefli found that competitive advantage had become significantly harder to sustain over time, across a broad range of industries rather than only high technology or manufacturing.[^s5] They concluded that lasting superior performance increasingly depends on stringing together a series of temporary advantages rather than holding one for decades, a view associated with the hypercompetition thesis.[^s5] Porter's insistence on a single, stable position thus sits in tension with evidence that advantages erode faster than strategy textbooks once assumed.[^s2][^s5]

## In practice

Walmart illustrates a cost-based advantage: plain stores, aggressive supplier pricing and tight control of labor and other operating costs let it profit at prices rivals struggle to match.[^s1] Starbucks shows the differentiation route, charging premium prices for atmosphere, service, quality and brand.[^s1] Southwest Airlines is Porter's classic case of fit: by forgoing meals, assigned seats and baggage transfers it built a fast, low-cost, point-to-point system, and Continental Lite's attempt to match it while keeping a full-service model lost hundreds of millions of dollars.[^s2]

## Connections

Competitive advantage is the core outcome studied within **strategic management**, its broader field.[^s1] The **resource-based view** explains where sustained advantage comes from inside the firm, while **Porter's Five Forces** explains how industry structure caps the profits any advantage can earn, and the **generic competitive strategies** describe the basic routes to it.[^s1][^s3] The concept was shaped by **Michael Porter** and is defined in contrast to **operational effectiveness**, which improves performance but rarely differentiates; **Southwest Airlines** exemplifies an advantage built on a tightly fitted activity system.[^s2] Related articles such as first-mover advantage and the experience curve describe specific mechanisms through which an advantage can arise.
