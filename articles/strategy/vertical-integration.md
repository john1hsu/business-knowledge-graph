# Vertical Integration

**Vertical integration** is common ownership of successive stages of an industry chain. A firm that integrates **backward** takes over the production of its own inputs; one that integrates **forward** takes over the distribution or sale of its output.[^s3] Economists treat "make or buy" as a simplification: full integration and spot-market purchasing are polar cases, with hybrid forms such as long-term contracts, franchises, requirements contracts, joint ventures and dual sourcing in between.[^s4] For strategists the question is therefore not only whether to integrate, but how far, at which stages and in what form.[^s2]

## Origin and theory

Under textbook assumptions of costless market exchange, vertical integration is hard to explain, and early antitrust thinking saw it mainly as a source of anticompetitive harm, justified at most by technological links between stages.[^s1] Oliver Williamson's 1971 paper in the *American Economic Review* reframed it: firms often integrate to escape "transactional failures" in intermediate-product markets, substituting internal organization for market exchange.[^s1] Inside the firm, adversarial haggling is dampened, performance can be audited more closely, and minor disputes can be settled by managerial fiat; these advantages matter most when contracts are incomplete, products are complex and changing, and suppliers must make long-lived investments.[^s1] Williamson also flagged the other side of the ledger: administrative coordination costs grow as the organization expands, and integration is an extreme response compared with long-term contracts or joint ventures.[^s1]

Joskow's survey groups the later theories into two families. Neoclassical theories stress double marginalization (two independent monopolies each adding a markup), price discrimination, retailer free-riding on pre-sale service, and **vertical foreclosure**, where integration is used strategically to raise rivals' costs.[^s4] Transaction-cost and property-rights theories stress relationship-specific assets (site, physical, human, dedicated and brand assets) that lock parties together and expose them to hold-up.[^s4] He also notes integration's costs: bureaucracies adapt poorly, internal incentives are weaker, and the firm loses the information carried by market prices.[^s4]

## As a strategy

Kathryn Harrigan's 1984 *Academy of Management Review* framework moved the debate into strategic management, describing vertical integration along several dimensions and proposing hypotheses about the forces that should shape hybrid make-or-buy strategies in linked industries.[^s2] John Stuckey and David White's 1993 *Sloan Management Review* article distilled four reasons to integrate: the adjacent market is too risky or unreliable; firms in adjacent stages hold more market power; integration would create or exploit market power, for instance through entry barriers or price discrimination; or the market is young (requiring forward integration to build demand) or declining (as independent partners exit).[^s3] They warned that integration carries heavy setup costs, is expensive to reverse, and that management's record on these decisions is poor, so quasi-integration such as long-term contracts, joint ventures, alliances, licensing and franchising is often the better choice.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Empirical support for the transaction-cost view is strong. Lafontaine and Slade's 2007 *Journal of Economic Literature* review found that asset specificity, complexity and uncertainty make backward integration more likely, judging the weight of the evidence "overwhelming".[^s5] On consequences, they concluded that integration firms choose voluntarily is generally efficient and benefits consumers, that evidence of foreclosure is weak, and that government-mandated separation, such as US gasoline divorcement laws and UK beer-tie rules, typically raised prices or cut service.[^s5] Joskow likewise found minimal support for anticompetitive foreclosure except where a firm controls a bottleneck facility.[^s4] The caveats: Lafontaine and Slade warn that publication bias may make the findings look more uniform than they are, and agency theory's prediction about risk and integration remains a puzzle.[^s5]

## In practice

Stuckey and White point to steel, where blast furnaces and steelmaking vessels sit a few hundred yards apart and are almost always commonly owned, because tightly bound buyer-seller pairs would face constant negotiation and exploitation risk; Weirton Steel's 1991 sale of hot metal to Wheeling-Pittsburgh was a rare exception.[^s3] A modern case of backward integration into design is Apple's June 2020 announcement that it would move the Mac from Intel processors to its own system-on-chip designs, citing performance per watt and a common architecture across its products, built on more than a decade of in-house chip design for iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch.[^s6]

## Connections

Vertical integration is a **corporate-strategy** choice about the scope of the firm and a long-standing topic in **strategic management**.[^s2][^s3] Its main theoretical basis is **transaction cost economics**, and its competitive effects are studied in **industrial organization** and policed under **antitrust law** as vertical foreclosure.[^s1][^s4][^s5] It **contrasts with** horizontal integration, which combines firms at the same stage, and is the **alternative to** outsourcing and the hybrid contracts that sit between make and buy.[^s3][^s4] **Apple** exemplifies backward integration into chip design.[^s6]
