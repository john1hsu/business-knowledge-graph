# Strategic Renewal

Strategic renewal is the refreshment or replacement of the organizational attributes that have the potential to substantially affect a firm's long-term prospects. Rajshree Agarwal and Constance Helfat, writing in *Organization Science* in 2009, argued that renewal is vital to long-run success yet had drawn less research attention than strategic change in general; they defined the concept, showed that it matters for individual firms, whole industries and economies, and used IBM's repeated reinventions as an illustration.[^s1] A later review by Andreas Schmitt, Sebastian Raisch and Henk Volberda framed renewal as the process through which organizations alter their path dependence by transforming their strategic intent and capabilities.[^s2]

## What distinguishes it

Schmitt and colleagues identify three defining features: renewal transforms the core capabilities tied to competitive advantage, it involves the organization across levels rather than one unit, and it is essential for breaking path dependence and securing long-term survival.[^s2] That distinguishes it from competitive strategy (positioning within a given business), corporate venturing (creating new businesses) and the broader category of strategic change, of which renewal is a subset.[^s2] Agarwal and Helfat stress that renewal can follow content-based paths (what the firm changes, such as businesses or capabilities) and process-based paths (how change is initiated and carried out), and they look for common themes across both.[^s1]

## How it works

An early formal model by Thomas, Huff and Huff (1992) in the *Strategic Management Journal* treats renewal as a tension between two forces. Inertia is commitment to the current strategy; stress is accumulated dissatisfaction that signals a need to change. The model asks how the interplay of the two moves a firm between routine renewal, which maintains its current strategy, and a more radical reconceptualization that substantially changes what it does. The authors captured this in a four-phase framework and simulated the resulting renewal paths.[^s3]

The review by Schmitt, Raisch and Volberda organizes the wider literature, built from 95 core publications published between 1983 and 2015, around three tensions:[^s2]

- **Antecedents — learning vs. resources.** Renewal is driven either by organizational learning (balancing exploration and exploitation) or by dynamic capabilities that reconfigure the resource base.[^s2]
- **Process — induced vs. autonomous.** Renewal is launched either top-down by senior executives or bottom-up by middle managers and employees who experiment outside the official strategy.[^s2]
- **Outcomes — co-alignment vs. co-creation.** Firms either realign reactively with a changed environment or proactively shape how their industry evolves.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The same review criticizes the field as fragmented, with inconsistent definitions and blurred boundaries, and notes that findings from different studies are hard to compare, aggregate and replicate.[^s2] It argues that "either/or" framings exaggerate the differences between perspectives and proposes a "both/and" paradox lens, since renewal often requires top-down and bottom-up initiative, or learning and resource reconfiguration, at the same time.[^s2] It also observes that much empirical work is static even though renewal is a process, and that assumptions such as renewal depending on organizational slack may be too simple.[^s2]

## In practice: Intel's exit from memory

Intel is the classic case. Robert Burgelman's Stanford case describes the choice its top management faced at the end of 1984 over whether to leave the DRAM business that Intel had pioneered. By then memory chips contributed little to its product mix and microprocessors had become the core, yet executives still regarded DRAMs as strategically important for market and technology reasons.[^s4] The case uses the decision to show how organizational beliefs can lag behind shifts in industry structure, how an internal selection environment moves resources toward winning businesses before formal strategy catches up, and how renewal depends on top management's capacity for "strategic recognition" of what the organization has already begun to become.[^s4] That pattern of autonomous initiative later ratified from the top is the induced-versus-autonomous tension in action.[^s2]

## Connections

Strategic renewal is a branch of **strategic management** concerned with sustaining a firm across environmental shifts rather than within one competitive position.[^s2] It draws on **dynamic capabilities** and **organizational learning**, the two antecedent views the literature contrasts, and on **organizational ambidexterity**, the ability to explore and exploit at once.[^s2] It is the constructive counterpart to **corporate decline**, while a **corporate turnaround** is a crisis-driven special case. **Intel**, the **IBM turnaround**, **Apple** and **Netflix's business model shift** illustrate it, with IBM being Agarwal and Helfat's own example of a firm that renewed itself repeatedly.[^s1] Robert Burgelman's research on internal strategy making at Intel shaped the autonomous-process view.[^s4]
