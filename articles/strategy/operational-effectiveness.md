# Operational Effectiveness: Performing Similar Activities Better Than Rivals

**Operational effectiveness (OE)** is Michael Porter's name for doing the same or similar activities better than competitors do. It covers efficiency but is broader: cutting defects, using inputs more productively and developing products faster all count.[^s1] Porter introduced the term in his 1996 *Harvard Business Review* article "What Is Strategy?", written at a time when managers were leaning heavily on total quality management, benchmarking and reengineering. His central claim was that these tools delivered real operational gains yet seldom produced lasting profitability, and had gradually crowded strategy itself out of management attention.[^s2]

## The productivity frontier

Porter framed OE with the idea of a **productivity frontier**: the set of all best practices available at a given moment, defining the most value a company can deliver at a given cost using the best current technologies, skills and management methods.[^s1] Improving OE moves a firm toward that frontier. The frontier itself keeps shifting outward as new technologies and management approaches appear, so staying near it is a continuous effort rather than a one-time project.[^s1]

Porter treated OE as necessary but not sufficient for superior performance.[^s2] A company that falls far behind the frontier will lose to rivals that deliver more value at the same cost. But a company cannot outperform for long by OE alone, because the techniques that produce it are generic and easy to imitate.[^s1][^s2]

## Why OE is not strategy

Porter offered several reasons why competing on OE rarely yields durable advantage.[^s1]

- **Rapid diffusion.** Best practices spread quickly through consultants, benchmarking and supplier networks, and the most generic solutions spread fastest.[^s1]
- **Competitive convergence.** When every firm benchmarks the same leaders and outsources to the same third parties, rivals come to look alike, and customers choose mainly on price.[^s1]
- **Zero-sum outcomes.** Gains raise the bar for the whole industry without giving any one player a relative edge; Porter cited US commercial printing, where R.R. Donnelley's margin fell from above 7% in the 1980s to under 4.6% by 1995 as firms raced to adopt the same equipment.[^s1]
- **The Japanese case.** Japanese manufacturers led the OE revolution of the 1980s, offering lower cost and higher quality at once, yet many later earned persistently low profits once rivals closed the gap.[^s1]

The alternative is **strategic positioning**: performing different activities from rivals, or similar activities in different ways. Positioning requires trade-offs (choosing what not to do) and fit, in which activities reinforce one another so that a rival must copy a whole system rather than a single practice.[^s1] Harvard's Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness frames the same contrast as competing to be the best, a zero-sum race no one wins, versus competing to be unique, which can leave room for several winners.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Later research qualifies Porter's assumption that operational practices are easy to copy. Robert Gibbons and Rebecca Henderson document economically significant, persistent performance gaps among seemingly similar enterprises, and argue that many important management practices rest on relational contracts, informal shared understandings that are slow to build, hard to communicate and costly to change.[^s4] On that view, superior operations can themselves be hard to imitate, and firms such as Toyota and Southwest Airlines used such investments to enter industries dominated by incumbents with harder assets.[^s4]

Large-scale survey evidence points the same way. Nicholas Bloom, Raffaella Sadun and John Van Reenen scored management practices at more than 11,000 manufacturing firms in 34 countries and found that roughly 77% of the variation lay within the same country and industry, not between them.[^s5] Better-managed firms showed higher productivity, profitability, growth and survival, and in their 2016 paper (revised 2017) management practices accounted for about 30% of the average total factor productivity gap between other countries and the US.[^s5] If best practice diffused as quickly as the OE argument implies, such wide and lasting dispersion would be hard to explain.[^s4][^s5]

## In practice

Managers use the distinction as a diagnostic. Initiatives such as lean programs, cost-reduction drives or quality improvement belong on the operational agenda and should run continuously, while strategy calls for a separate discussion of which customers, needs and activities the firm will and will not serve.[^s1] Porter's cautionary example is Continental Lite, which tried to match Southwest's low-cost point-to-point service while remaining a full-service airline; it lost hundreds of millions of dollars, because adopting a rival's practices without its trade-offs produced neither low cost nor a distinctive position.[^s1]

## Connections

OE is a concept within **strategic management**, its broader field, and was defined by **Michael Porter** in **"What Is Strategy?" (1996)**.[^s1][^s2] It **contrasts with strategic positioning**, the activity choices that create a unique position, and it is the operational half of the explanation of **competitive advantage**, which on Porter's account requires both.[^s1][^s3] **Benchmarking**, **total quality management** and **business process reengineering** are the management tools Porter singled out as drivers of OE that rivals can readily imitate.[^s2]
