# Dynamic Capabilities

**Dynamic capabilities** are a firm's higher-order abilities to integrate, build and reconfigure its resources and competences so that it can cope with, and help shape, a changing environment. They sit above *ordinary* (operational) capabilities, which run today's income-generating activities, and govern how those ordinary capabilities change over time.[^s5] The theory tries to explain why some firms keep earning superior returns in markets where technology and demand shift quickly, while others see their advantage erode.[^s1]

## Origin

The framework was introduced by David Teece and Gary Pisano in 1994 and set out fully by Teece, Pisano and Amy Shuen in "Dynamic capabilities and strategic management", *Strategic Management Journal* 18(7), 1997.[^s5][^s1] The 1997 paper argued that competitive advantage in fast-moving technology markets rests on distinctive internal processes, shaped by the firm's asset positions (especially knowledge and complementary assets that are hard to trade) and by the evolutionary paths it has followed. Path dependence matters more when returns increase with adoption, and the durability of advantage depends on how stable demand is and how easily rivals can replicate or imitate the firm's capabilities.[^s1] The work grew out of the resource-based view, which critics saw as too static: holding valuable, rare and hard-to-imitate resources is not enough if those resources must be renewed as markets turn.[^s5]

## How it works

Teece's 2007 restatement in the *Strategic Management Journal* split dynamic capabilities into three clusters. *Sensing* (and shaping) means scanning, learning about and interpreting technological, market and competitor developments to spot opportunities and threats. *Seizing* means committing investment to new products, processes and business models, including choices about product architecture and the firm's boundaries. *Managing threats and reconfiguration* means continually recombining, realigning and redeploying assets and routines so the asset base stays relevant.[^s2] Teece calls the underlying skills, processes, structures, decision rules and disciplines the *microfoundations* and stresses that they are hard to build and deploy.[^s2] He likens top management's role to an orchestra conductor whose instruments are constantly being created, renewed or retired, and argues that units must be decentralized enough to move fast while staying coordinated.[^s2]

The theory also separates what is truly dynamic from what is merely good practice. Teece argues that ad hoc problem solving is not necessarily a dynamic capability, and that adopting widely known best practices usually is not one, because competitors can copy it.[^s2] A *California Management Review* summary draws the same line between industry-wide functional capabilities, such as knowing how to build an auto assembly plant, and firm-specific "signature processes" tied to a company's business model.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The most influential challenge came from Kathleen Eisenhardt and Jeffrey Martin (2000). They treated dynamic capabilities as specific, identifiable processes such as product development, strategic decision making and alliancing, and found that these share common features across firms, so they are more substitutable, and closer to best practice, than Teece's account implies. In moderately dynamic markets such capabilities look like stable routines; in high-velocity markets they become simple, experiential and unpredictable, which led the authors to argue that resource-based logic reaches its limits there.[^s3] That firm-specific versus best-practice disagreement remains one of the theory's internal tensions.[^s5]

Other critics point to definitional vagueness, inconsistent measurement that blurs dynamic and ordinary capabilities, and a tendency toward tautology, in which strong performance is credited to dynamic capabilities after the fact without identifying them independently.[^s5] Empirical support is mixed. A systematic review of 92 quantitative studies by Baía and Ferreira (published online 2019; issue dated 2024) found that the link to performance remains unclear and debated, that conceptualizations range from very specific capabilities to generic bundles, and that performance measures vary widely. The authors concluded that an indirect model, in which dynamic capabilities drive change and intermediate outcomes that then affect performance, is the most promising.[^s4]

## In practice

The *California Management Review* uses Apple's iPod as an illustration. Apple sensed latent demand because existing MP3 players were bulky and unattractive; it seized the opportunity with a smaller, better-designed device and a broader shift from computers into consumer electronics; and it kept transforming the line through later models such as the Nano and Touch, along with iTunes.[^s6] In management practice the framework is used to examine how executives allocate attention, how supply chains build resilience and how firms pursue business model innovation, open innovation and digital transformation.[^s5]

## Connections

Dynamic capabilities are a theory within **strategic management** and an extension of the **resource-based view**, which they were designed to make less static.[^s5] **David J. Teece** is the principal creator, and the 1997 *Strategic Management Journal* article is the founding text.[^s1] The framework contrasts with **Porter's five forces**: Teece argues that the five forces treat industry structure as given, whereas innovation reshapes it, so he replaces the industry with the wider **business ecosystem** of complementors, suppliers, regulators and standard setters.[^s2] It overlaps with **organizational ambidexterity** and is offered as a remedy for the inertia described by **disruptive innovation** and **the innovator's dilemma**, since sensing and reconfiguring are exactly what incumbents fail to do.[^s2][^s5] Within this graph, the **IBM turnaround** node is linked as an applied case of the framework (see that article for its sources).
