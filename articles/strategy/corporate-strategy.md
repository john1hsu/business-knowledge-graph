# Corporate Strategy

**Corporate strategy** (or corporate-level strategy) answers the question of *where* a multibusiness firm should compete: which industries to enter, which businesses to keep or sell, and how the corporate centre should relate to the units it owns. Business-level strategy, by contrast, asks how a single unit wins inside its own industry. Michael Porter framed the distinction sharply in 1987: competition happens at the business-unit level, so a diversified company only justifies itself if owning a unit makes that unit, or the corporation, better able to compete.[^s1] Andrew Campbell, Michael Goold and Marcus Alexander put the same point in terms of the corporate parent, which must create value from joint ownership for the multibusiness form to make economic sense.[^s2]

## Origin and core ideas

Porter's *Harvard Business Review* article "From Competitive Advantage to Corporate Strategy" drew on the diversification records of 33 large US companies between 1950 and 1986 and concluded that most diversification, whether through acquisition, joint venture or start-up, failed to deliver the expected advantage or profits.[^s1] He described four concepts of corporate strategy in common use. Portfolio management, buying autonomous units and supplying capital and managers, he judged obsolete in the US because capital markets already allocate funds efficiently. Restructuring was a stopgap that tended to leave an unwieldy conglomerate. Transferring skills and sharing activities across related units offered the best odds, because they exploit real interrelationships between businesses.[^s1]

Textbook treatments turn this into three tests that any diversification move should pass: the target industry must be attractive enough to offer strong profits; the firm must be able to recoup the cost of entering; and either the new unit or the existing firm must end up better off, meaning one side gains a competitive advantage from the combination.[^s4] The same texts distinguish **related diversification**, entering an industry that shares important similarities with existing businesses and often leverages a core competence, from **unrelated diversification**, entering an industry with no such links. Their verdict is that related moves are generally wiser and that most unrelated moves end badly because of high direct and indirect costs.[^s4]

## Parenting advantage

In 1995 Campbell, Goold and Alexander argued in *California Management Review* that many corporate parents destroy rather than create value, because value creation depends on conditions that are often absent. Successful diversified firms, in their account, have a **parenting advantage**: they create more value from owning their businesses than any rival parent could, and that test should guide which businesses to hold and which corporate skills to build.[^s2] A 2024 *CMR Insights* piece by Herman Vantrappen and Frederic Wirtz extends the idea to organisation design. It criticises the usual linear scale of parenting archetypes, from financial holding company to operational centre, for assuming the parent behaves the same across all influence areas and all businesses, and proposes setting loose or tight coupling separately for each type of business and each way the parent exerts influence.[^s3] They define parenting advantage as the value the parent adds to its portfolio businesses and note that a unit to which it adds none may be better off elsewhere or standing alone.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The empirical record has long looked unfavourable to broad diversification. Graham, Lemmon and Wolf summarise Berger and Ofek's 1995 finding that conglomerates traded at roughly a 15% discount to the imputed value of their segments, with a larger loss for unrelated diversification.[^s5] Their own study challenges the causal reading. In 286 acquisitions from 1980 to 1995, acquirers' excess value fell about 6% around the deal, but the acquired units already traded at about a 9% discount as stand-alones, and adding those already-discounted units explains half or more of the decline. Firms that added segments by internal growth showed no significant value loss.[^s5] The authors still allow that the unexplained remainder could reflect inefficient operation, so the debate is about how much value diversification destroys, not whether the discount exists.[^s5] A second critique, from Porter himself, is that portfolio logic ignores the fact that shareholders can diversify risk on their own, so risk spreading alone fails the better-off test.[^s1]

## In practice

The open textbook cites Coca-Cola's 1982 purchase of Columbia Pictures for $750 million and its sale to Sony seven years later for $3.4 billion as an example of unrelated diversification, and contrasts it with Honda, which carried its small-engine skills from motorcycles into cars, lawn mowers, boat motors and the HondaJet.[^s4] Vantrappen and Wirtz describe Dover, a diversified manufacturer with revenue of over $8 billion (as of 2024), whose parent runs a digital labs centre, global finance shared services and supply-chain programmes that its operating companies draw on, an example of a parent adding value selectively.[^s3]

## Connections

Corporate strategy is a branch of **strategic management** and **contrasts with business-level strategy**, which concerns how one unit achieves **competitive advantage**.[^s1][^s4] Its main instruments are **diversification**, **vertical integration** and **mergers and acquisitions**, the routes by which firms change their scope.[^s1][^s4] Portfolio tools such as the **BCG growth-share matrix** embody the portfolio-management concept Porter criticised.[^s1] The **parenting advantage** test is a component of the field,[^s2][^s3] the **resource-based view** underlies the related-diversification logic of leveraging core competences,[^s4] the field was shaped by **Michael Porter**,[^s1] and its value effects are measured empirically through the **diversification discount**.[^s5]
