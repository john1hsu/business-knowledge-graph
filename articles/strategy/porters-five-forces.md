# Porter's Five Forces

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing the structure of an industry in order to explain why some industries are persistently more profitable than others. Its central claim is that an industry's long-run profitability, and the way the economic value it creates is divided, is set by five forces: rivalry among existing competitors, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, the threat of new entrants and the threat of substitute products or services.[^s1] Strategy, in this view, means either building defences against the forces or finding a position in the industry where they are weakest.[^s1]

## Origin

Harvard Business School professor Michael E. Porter first set out the framework in the 1979 Harvard Business Review article "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", and revisited and extended it in a 2008 HBR article that added guidance on applying the analysis.[^s1] He developed it at book length in *Competitive Strategy: Techniques for Analyzing Industries and Competitors* (1980), whose first part pairs the five forces with three generic strategies for coping with them: overall cost leadership, differentiation and focus.[^s3] Also in 1980, Porter presented the forces to investors in the Financial Analysts Journal, arguing that how hard competition presses returns down toward the competitive level is not luck but a product of each industry's economic and technical characteristics.[^s2]

## How it works

Each force captures a different way that value can leak out of an industry.[^s4] Intense **rivalry** among incumbents competes profits away through price cuts, incentives and costly marketing; it tends to be weak where firms are few or occupy distinct positions. The **threat of new entrants** depends on barriers such as capital requirements, brand loyalty and regulation; OpenStax notes that airline entry is rare because aircraft, landing rights and expertise are costly to obtain.[^s4] **Substitutes** come from other industries that meet the same customer need, as grocery stores do for fast-food chains.[^s4] **Supplier power** rises when inputs are specialised or scarce and falls for commodity inputs such as wheat or aluminium, while **buyer power** falls when products are unique or switching costs are high, as with the ecosystem that keeps iPhone users loyal.[^s4]

The analysis is typically done in two steps: first assess the industry's forces and their underlying drivers, then judge how well a particular company's strategy positions it against them.[^s2] Porter distinguishes a defensive use, building capabilities that best withstand the existing forces, from an offensive one that shifts the balance of forces or exploits structural change before rivals do.[^s2] In his 2008 restatement he cautions that fast industry growth does not guarantee profitability, that removing competitors through mergers can lower an industry's profit potential, and that government policy and complementary products matter through their effect on the five forces rather than as forces of their own.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The framework's empirical weight rests on how much of a firm's profitability is explained by the industry it is in. Richard Rumelt's 1991 decomposition of returns among US business units found stable industry effects to be small and business-unit effects very large, concluding that the main sources of economic rent are specific to the business rather than to industry membership.[^s5] Porter, with Anita McGahan, replied in 1997 using a broader sample of US public companies: year, industry, corporate-parent and business-specific effects accounted for about 2%, 19%, 4% and 32% of variance in profitability respectively, with industry mattering less in manufacturing and more in services, lodging and entertainment, trade and transportation.[^s6] The debate is usually read as showing that industry structure matters, but that firm-level differences matter more, which is the opening exploited by resource-based critics.[^s5][^s6]

Other common criticisms are practical rather than statistical. Rating each force as strong or weak calls for analyst judgement, and the tool looks at a firm's immediate competitive environment rather than at its internal resources.[^s4]

## In practice

Analysts use the forces to explain persistent profit gaps between industries and to forecast earning power. Porter's 1980 illustration contrasted industries with intense forces, such as tyres, paper and steel, where no firm earns spectacular returns, with milder ones such as oil-field equipment and services or cosmetics and toiletries, where high returns were common at the time.[^s2] Managers then use the same diagnosis to choose where to compete, for example by seeking positions shielded by entry barriers or by raising buyers' switching costs.[^s1][^s4]

## Connections

The framework is a core tool of industry analysis within strategic management, applying industrial organization's question of how industry economics shape returns to the choices of individual firms; its link to the structure-conduct-performance tradition is recorded as an influence.[^s2][^s3] Barriers to entry and switching costs are drivers of individual forces (the entry threat and buyer power), and Porter's generic competitive strategies were proposed as the ways a firm copes with the forces it faces.[^s3][^s4] The industry-versus-firm effects debate set it against the resource-based view, which locates advantage inside the firm, and dynamic capabilities later extended that internal focus.[^s5][^s6]
