# Strategic Planning

Strategic planning is the recurring organizational process through which leaders decide where an enterprise should go over the next several years and how it will get there. Wolf and Floyd, reviewing three decades of research, define it as a periodic and more or less formalized process that structures how strategy is formulated, implemented and controlled over a set horizon, while coordinating both deliberate and emergent decisions.[^s5] It is one component of the broader discipline of strategic management, distinguished by its emphasis on a scheduled cycle, written plans and explicit targets.

## How it works

Textbook treatments describe a sequence of steps. OpenStax lays out five: a vision statement describing where the business is headed; a mission statement explaining why it exists, whom it serves and how it differs; a gap analysis that asks where the organization stands now, where it wants to be and what blocks the way; a set of goals (broad, typically three to five years) and objectives (specific, usually a year or less) written to SMART criteria; and continuous monitoring, often through a dashboard of key performance indicators.[^s1] The resulting plan is cascaded through three levels: corporate strategy set by top management for the whole portfolio, business-level strategy for each unit, and functional strategies for areas such as marketing, operations and human resources.[^s1]

## Origins and decline

Formal strategic planning took off in the mid-1960s, helped by Igor Ansoff's 1965 book *Corporate Strategy*, and for a time large planning departments were a fixture of big corporations.[^s2] Its reputation then slid. Mintzberg notes that General Electric's celebrated planning system was dismantled in the early 1980s after Jack Welch became chief executive.[^s2] A 1980s GE planner recalled that the old planning department had acted as a kind of corporate policeman, a sign that the system had become a control mechanism.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The most influential critique came from Henry Mintzberg in 1994. He argued that planning rests on three fallacies: predetermination (that the environment can be forecast or held stable), detachment (that strategists can be separated from operations and rely on hard data) and formalization (that strategy creation can be broken into routine steps).[^s2] Together these form a grand fallacy: because analysis is not synthesis, strategic planning never really produced strategies, and Mintzberg proposed it be renamed "strategic programming."[^s2] In his view strategies come from leaders' vision or from learning; planning then codifies them, elaborates them into programmes and converts them into budgets and objectives, which works best in stable, capital-intensive settings such as airlines or mass production.[^s3]

Empirical work is more favourable but mixed. Miller and Cardinal's 1994 meta-analysis of 26 studies in the *Academy of Management Journal* found that planning has a positive effect on firm performance and attributed earlier inconsistent findings mostly to methodological differences rather than to substantive contingency factors, which had little impact.[^s4] Wolf and Floyd note that the average effect was modest and correlations varied widely, and that published research in top journals fell sharply after 1994 as scholars turned to how planning is actually practised, the participation of middle managers and planning's role in coordination and communication.[^s5]

## In practice

Robert Grant's case studies of eight of the world's largest oil companies, published in 2003, showed that planning survived turbulence by changing form. Planning systems at the oil majors shifted toward "planned emergence": headquarters set demanding performance targets and clear guidelines while business units formulated strategy in a decentralized way. The systems aided adaptation but showed limited innovation and analytical depth.[^s6] This matches Mintzberg's own recommended roles: plans serve as communication media for insiders and outsiders and as control devices, while planners act as finders of emergent strategies, analysts and catalysts for strategic thinking.[^s3] Royal Dutch/Shell's former planning head Arie de Geus argued that the real purpose of planning is to change managers' mental models, not to produce a document.[^s3]

## Connections

Strategic planning sits under strategic management, which treats it as one part of the discipline alongside emergent strategy.[^s5] It contrasts with emergent strategy, the learning-based view Mintzberg championed, and Grant's planned emergence suggests the two can be reconciled.[^s6] Ansoff's 1965 work shaped its early form.[^s2] Scenario planning is a related technique that responds to the forecasting problem behind Mintzberg's fallacy of predetermination.[^s2] Hoshin kanri, the balanced scorecard and strategy execution address the conversion of plans into targets and controls, the programming role Mintzberg assigned to planning.[^s3] Stakeholder analysis feeds the assessment stage of a plan, and General Electric and Shell illustrate planning's rise, retreat and reinvention.[^s2]
