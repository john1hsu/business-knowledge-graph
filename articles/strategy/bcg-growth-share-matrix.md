# BCG Growth-Share Matrix

The **BCG growth-share matrix** is a portfolio-planning framework that places each business or product line of a diversified company on two axes: its market share relative to competitors and the growth rate of its market. Splitting each axis into high and low yields four cells (stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs), each with a default prescription for how much cash to put in or take out.[^s2] Bruce Henderson, founder of the Boston Consulting Group, set out the logic in a short 1970 essay, "The Product Portfolio".[^s1]

## Origin

Henderson's argument was about cash, not accounting profit. Fast-growing products absorb cash to fund the assets that growth requires, while slow-growing products should throw off cash, so a company needs both at once and must move money between them.[^s1] Market share mattered because, through the experience curve, the share leader accumulates volume faster and so enjoys lower costs, higher margins and more cash generation.[^s1] His blunt test was that every product must eventually generate cash or it is worthless.[^s1] The tool emerged from consulting practice rather than academic theory, and Seymour Tilles's 1966 writing on portfolio thinking is also cited as an influence.[^s3]

## How it works

- **Stars** (high share, high growth) often look profitable but may not fund themselves; if they keep leadership they become large cash generators once growth slows.[^s1]
- **Cash cows** (high share, low growth) produce more cash than they need, and Henderson argued the surplus should be redeployed elsewhere rather than reinvested in the same product.[^s1]
- **Question marks** (low share, high growth), also called problem children, need far more cash than they earn and must either be pushed to leadership or abandoned.[^s1][^s2]
- **Dogs** (Henderson's "pets"; low share, low growth) tie up funds for little return and are candidates for divestment, freeing capital for stars and question marks.[^s1][^s2]

A balanced portfolio, in this view, uses cows to fund question marks with a chance of becoming stars, which in turn become the next cows.[^s1] OpenStax presents the matrix as a way for companies with several strategic business units to allocate limited marketing and R&D funds across them.[^s2]

## Adoption

The matrix spread with the conglomerate wave of the 1960s and 1970s. Studies summarised by Madsen report that more than 100 large U.S. companies used it by 1972 and roughly half of the Fortune 500 used it at some point between 1972 and 1982; rivals answered with their own grids, such as the GE/McKinsey nine-cell matrix.[^s3] Its standing fell in the 1980s as conglomerates lost legitimacy and attention shifted to execution, yet surveys in 2007 and 2014 still found roughly a quarter to under a third of organisations using portfolio matrices.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Critics call the matrix reductionist, because one variable stands in for the market and one for the firm's capabilities; mechanistic, because it yields four generic prescriptions; and hard to apply, because what counts as "high" share or growth, and the market itself, is open to judgment.[^s3] Hambrick and MacMillan, analysing PIMS data in 1982, questioned the reflex to divest dogs and argued that how mature, low-growth businesses can thrive deserved far more study.[^s5]

The sharpest evidence came from a laboratory experiment. Armstrong and Brodie (1994) gave 1,015 subjects in six countries a choice between a profitable investment in a "dog" and a money-losing one in a "star". Among those shown the BCG matrix, 63.5% picked the unprofitable star, versus 44.7% of controls and 37.1% of those shown net present value; the authors advised against matrix methods altogether.[^s4]

## In practice

BCG's own 2014 reassessment conceded that relative share no longer reliably predicts sustained performance. It found that businesses moved between quadrants much faster: average time in a quadrant fell from about four years in 1992 to under two years in 2012.[^s6] It recommended shorter review cycles, more and cheaper experiments among question marks, and a new measure of competitiveness, citing Google's spread from mature advertising products to nascent bets as an example as of 2014.[^s6]

## Connections

The matrix rests on the **experience curve**, also popularised by BCG, and on **economies of scale**, which together justify treating share as a proxy for cost position and cash flow.[^s1] It was created by **Bruce Henderson** and introduced in his 1970 essay **"The Product Portfolio"**.[^s1] It is a tool of **portfolio planning** and **corporate strategy** for multi-business firms organised into **strategic business units**, and it rose and fell with conglomerate-style **diversification strategy**.[^s2][^s3] The **GE/McKinsey matrix** is its main alternative grid, while **net present value** is the profit-based approach Armstrong and Brodie found led to better choices.[^s3][^s4] BCG's later call to balance new question marks against milking cows echoes **organizational ambidexterity** and its exploration-exploitation trade-off, with **Google** as the illustration.[^s6]
