# Scenario Planning

**Scenario planning** is a disciplined way of imagining several possible futures and using them to shape present-day strategy. Instead of betting on one forecast, a team builds a handful of coherent, plausible narratives about how markets, technology, regulation and geopolitics might evolve, then asks how its strategy would fare in each. Paul Schoemaker framed it as a remedy for two chronic failures of managerial judgment: overconfidence and tunnel vision, both of which lead people to underestimate how far the future can depart from the status quo.[^s2] Scenarios are judged by plausibility and relevance rather than probability; they are not predictions.[^s3]

## Origin

The practice took shape inside Royal Dutch/Shell. In 1965 Shell set up a long-term studies activity under Ted Newland that ran alongside its model-based planning system; Pierre Wack, a former magazine editor, joined to make the work persuasive to senior executives, and the group produced its first formal scenario round in November 1971.[^s3] The 1971–72 scenarios explored the possibility that oil-producing countries would gain power and restrict output, and the 1973 round treated "business as usual" as implausible; when the oil embargo and energy crisis arrived months later, Shell's managing directors had already considered a comparable world.[^s3] Wack later set out the method in a 1985 Harvard Business Review article, arguing that scenario work also helped Shell prepare to sell surplus oil before prices fell in 1981.[^s1]

## How it works

Wack's central argument was that forecasts work in stable periods but fail precisely when a major shift occurs, because they assume tomorrow will resemble today; the answer is not better forecasters but accepting uncertainty as a structural feature of the environment.[^s1] He distinguished several building blocks:[^s1]

- **Predetermined elements** are developments already in motion whose consequences can be traced, such as demographic or supply trends.[^s1]
- **Critical uncertainties** are genuinely open questions; treating them as settled, or hiding them, produces brittle plans.[^s1]
- **First-generation scenarios** merely quantify obvious uncertainties (for example, high and low oil prices) and rarely change decisions.[^s1]
- **Decision scenarios** explain the forces driving events and are designed to reshape how managers see their world.[^s1]

The real target is the decision maker's mental model. Wack described a gap between a manager's private "microcosm" and the real "macrocosm"; scenarios succeed only when they bridge that gap and lead executives to revise their assumptions.[^s1] Schoemaker's version of the process starts by identifying the basic trends and key uncertainties relevant to a decision, then combines them into a range of distinct scenarios that stretch the set of futures managers take seriously.[^s2]

Shell's own practice evolved several design rules: scenarios should address the real dilemmas executives face (gathered through in-depth interviews) while pushing them out of their comfort zone; they should be memorable but disposable; and quantitative models should support the narratives rather than replace them.[^s3] By 1989 Shell had cut its sets from six or seven scenarios to two, and Wack argued scenario work should run on its own rhythm rather than inside a ritualized annual planning cycle.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Despite roughly half a century of use, empirical tests are thin. Phadnis, Caplice, Sheffi and Singh found only three earlier experiments, all using students, with conflicting results, and noted that one field study linking scenario planning to firm performance could not rule out reverse causality.[^s4] Their own three field experiments with transportation-infrastructure experts (published in the *Strategic Management Journal* in 2014) found that using multiple scenarios did not systematically change experts' overall confidence; judgments did shift toward how an investment performed in the scenario considered, the most confident experts changed their minds least, and after multiple scenarios experts favored more flexible investment options.[^s4] The study had no control group, so it shows effects on judgment rather than on outcomes.[^s4]

Practitioner critiques come from Shell's own history. Wilkinson and Kupers report that top management largely ignored challenging scenarios in the 1980s, that heavy reliance on a 2007 energy model missed the 2008 financial crisis, the U.S. shale-gas boom and Germany's post-Fukushima energy shift, and that scenarios risk being either too familiar to provoke or too challenging to be heard.[^s3] They also found no solid evidence that Shell anticipated developments better than its rivals; global scenarios added value only indirectly.[^s3] Schoemaker cautions that anchoring on current trends and hindsight bias can make even scenario builders underestimate the range of possible change.[^s2]

## In practice

Shell remains the canonical case: its scenario team described itself as closer to personal trainers for executives than to forecasters, and the scenarios became a shared language for debating strategy and for engaging governments, national oil companies and NGOs.[^s3] Schoemaker points to firms such as General Motors in the 1970s and IBM and Digital Equipment in the 1980s as examples of companies that might have spotted discontinuities (OPEC, globalization, the personal computer) had they systematically explored alternative futures.[^s2]

## Connections

Scenario planning sits under **strategic management** and **strategic planning** as an alternative to single-point **forecasting**, with which it is often contrasted.[^s1] It was created by Pierre Wack and colleagues and is exemplified by **Royal Dutch Shell**.[^s3] Because it is meant to counter **overconfidence bias** and narrow framing, it relates to **groupthink** and to structured expert methods such as the **Delphi method**.[^s2] Its tendency to push decision makers toward flexible options links it to **real options** thinking, and researchers often promote it as a cognitive aid and a form of **dynamic capabilities**.[^s4] It complements, rather than replaces, execution-focused planning systems such as **Hoshin Kanri**, mirroring Wack's view that scenario work should run outside the annual planning ritual.[^s3]
