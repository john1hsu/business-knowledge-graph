# Business Format Franchising

**Business format franchising** is the form of franchising in which a franchisor licenses a complete way of running a business, not merely a brand or a product line. The franchisee gets the trademark plus the operating system behind it, and in return pays a fee to join and continuing royalties on sales.[^s2] It is usually contrasted with *product or trade-name franchising*, where the franchisee mainly distributes the franchisor's goods or uses its name, as in soft-drink bottling, car dealerships and gasoline stations.[^s2] Business-format chains have supplied all the growth in U.S. franchised outlets since at least the 1970s. In 2007 the Census counted roughly 387,000 business-format establishments with 6.4 million employees, against about 66,000 traditional ones with 1.5 million.[^s4]

## How it works

The franchisor supplies a "turn-key" operation (format, training, standards, brand marketing) and is paid through a one-time upfront franchise fee plus a royalty that is a share of the outlet's revenue.[^s4] The franchisee is a legally independent owner who typically funds the site and equipment, runs the outlet day to day and keeps the residual profit.[^s4] Most chains also own some outlets directly, which is called dual distribution. In one large panel of U.S. chains, the average chain had about 36 franchised outlets and 3 company-owned ones.[^s4]

In the United States the model is defined and regulated by the FTC Franchise Rule. A relationship counts as a franchise if three things hold: the franchisor provides a trademark, it exercises significant control over or gives significant assistance with the franchisee's methods, and the franchisee pays at least $500 within the first six months.[^s1] Franchisors must hand prospects a Franchise Disclosure Document with 23 items at least 14 calendar days before any contract is signed or money is paid. Any earnings claim must be substantiated and disclosed in Item 19.[^s1] What the parties call the arrangement doesn't decide whether the Rule applies; only those three elements do, so the Rule covers business-format and product franchises alike.[^s1]

## Why firms franchise: theory and evidence

The leading explanation is agency theory. A chain with many scattered outlets finds it costly to supervise salaried managers, while an owner-operator who keeps the profit has a strong reason to work hard. Lafontaine's 1992 study of 548 U.S. franchisors found that the data fit a *two-sided* moral hazard model best. In that model, the efforts of both the franchisee and the franchisor affect outlet results, so neither side can be left without an incentive.[^s3] The same models explained *how much* firms franchise better than the specific royalty and fee levels they set. Contrary to some theory, royalty rates and upfront fees were not negatively related.[^s3] Later work by Fan, Kühn and Lafontaine argues that franchisee collateral matters because it sharpens incentives. Using their estimates, they find that a 30% fall in collateralizable housing wealth would delay chains' entry into franchising and cut outlet growth by about 10%.[^s4]

## Critiques and limits

Franchising is often sold as a safer path to small-business ownership, but the evidence is mixed. Bates's Census study of firms started between 1984 and 1987 found that franchise startups were bigger and better capitalized than independent startups, yet the independents were more profitable and more likely to survive.[^s6] Disclosure rules exist because buyers know much less than sellers. That is why the FTC requires historical earnings claims to state which outlets, which period, and how many outlets actually reached the claimed results.[^s1]

## In practice

The model is far broader than fast food. The 2017 Economic Census counted 498,234 franchise establishments across nearly 300 industries, about 11.4% of businesses in the covered industries. They employed 9.6 million people and made $1.7 trillion in sales. Limited-service restaurants were the largest category, with 144,941 franchise establishments.[^s5] McDonald's is the textbook case. At the end of 2025, about 95% of its 45,356 restaurants were franchised, and franchised restaurants produced roughly $16.5 billion of its $26.9 billion in total revenue.[^s7] Under its conventional franchise, McDonald's usually controls the real estate while the franchisee pays for equipment and décor and owes rent plus sales-based royalties, typically under a 20-year agreement.[^s7]
