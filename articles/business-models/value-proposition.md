# Value Proposition

A **value proposition** (or **customer value proposition**, CVP) states what a firm will do for a specific group of customers and why that offer beats their alternatives. In its original formulation it is a clear, simple statement of the tangible and intangible benefits a company will provide, together with the approximate price it will charge each target segment; value is the benefit received minus the price paid, which economists call consumer surplus.[^s1] Introductory marketing texts describe it more loosely as a short statement of the measurable benefits customers can expect, functioning both as a promise and as a competitive differentiator.[^s5]

## Origin

The term was coined by Michael Lanning and Edward Michaels in a June 1988 McKinsey staff paper, "A business is a value delivery system," later adapted for the *McKinsey Quarterly*.[^s1] Stephen Vargo notes that Lanning dated the underlying ideas to about 1983 and formalized them further in his 1998 book *Delivering Profitable Value*; other marketing writers of the 1990s used the term, often without citation.[^s4] Lanning and Michaels' central argument was that strategy should start from the superior value a firm chooses to deliver to customers, not from rivalry alone.[^s1]

## How it works

Lanning and Michaels proposed building a proposition from concrete, observable product features customers want, grouping customers into segments by the benefits they seek and the prices they will pay, and choosing the segments where the firm can deliver superior value. Value maps, which plot benefit against price per segment, show where a new offering can stand out.[^s1] They then recast the business system from the production sequence "make, then sell" into three customer-facing stages: choose the value, provide the value and communicate the value. Every function should be able to show which stage its work supports, so that manufacturing, advertising and service all reinforce the same promise.[^s1]

In the Business Model Canvas, the value proposition is the block that explains why customers pick one offering over another; it is defined per customer segment and draws on channels, key activities, key resources and revenue streams.[^s6] OpenStax's illustration is Apple, whose proposition of a seamless music experience was delivered through iTunes.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Despite heavy use, the concept is thinly theorized. A 2017 review in the *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* found the CVP literature fragmented and lacking a strong theoretical foundation, with relatively little published research compared with the broader study of customer value; the authors proposed a model of its antecedents, consequences and moderators and a research agenda.[^s2] In business-to-business markets, Anderson, Narus and Van Rossum found no agreement among managers on what a value proposition is, and observed that most propositions assert savings and benefits without evidence. Listing every conceivable benefit invites "benefit assertion" about features customers do not value, so they argue suppliers must demonstrate and document superior value.[^s3]

A more fundamental critique comes from service-dominant logic. Vargo argues that treating the value proposition as a promise a firm makes and owns misreads its origins: Lanning defined it experientially, and if value is co-created in use, firms can only participate in proposing value, which multiple actors and institutions interpret in context.[^s4]

## In practice

Lanning and Michaels' examples include Frank Perdue's chicken, marketed on tenderness and golden color as superior to a commodity product, and Canon's AE-1 camera; both show a benefit chosen for a segment and then provided and communicated consistently.[^s1] They stress execution: a well-delivered ordinary proposition can beat a novel one poorly executed, and consistent delivery is harder for rivals to copy.[^s1] Consumer-facing statements are often compressed to a single line, such as meal-kit firm HelloFresh's promise to take the stress out of mealtime.[^s5]

## Connections

The value proposition is the customer-facing core of a **business model** (`kt:business-model`) and one of the nine blocks of the **Business Model Canvas** (`kt:business-model-canvas`), tied there to customer segments and revenue streams.[^s6] Because a proposition is defined per chosen segment, it is the operational output of **segmentation, targeting and positioning** (`kt:segmentation-targeting-positioning`).[^s1] It overlaps with **jobs to be done** (`kt:jobs-to-be-done`), which frames the benefit as the progress a customer seeks, and with **competitive advantage** (`kt:competitive-advantage`), since superior delivered value is what earns profit share.[^s1] Its originators were **Michael Lanning** (`kt:michael-lanning`) and **Edward Michaels** (`kt:edward-michaels`), and **service-dominant logic** (`kt:service-dominant-logic`) adopted and reinterpreted the term.[^s4]
