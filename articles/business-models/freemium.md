# Freemium

**Freemium** is a business model in which a company offers a basic product free of charge and charges for a premium tier with more features, capacity or convenience. The word blends "free" and "premium"; it was coined in 2006 by Jarid Lukin and venture capitalist Fred Wilson, who credited Lukin with first proposing it.[^s1] The model relies on a small minority of paying users to fund the product for everyone else.[^s1] Well-known users of the approach include Spotify, Dropbox, LinkedIn, Skype and Hootsuite.[^s2]

## How it works

The free tier acts as a customer-acquisition channel: it lowers the barrier to trial, builds a large user base, and lets people learn the product's value before paying. Revenue then depends on converting a fraction of free users to the premium tier.[^s2] Marketing scholars P.K. Kannan, Xian Gu and Hongshuang Li note that firms can separate the tiers by features, usage limits, service speed or user type, and that a free trial is best understood as a time-limited special case of freemium.[^s2]

Freemium suits products whose cost to serve one more user is very low, such as software and digital content. Firms with meaningful per-user costs must also earn money from free users, for example through advertising or in-app purchases.[^s2] The same logic of subsidizing one group to monetize another links freemium to the pricing of platforms.

## Evidence and critiques

Conversion is the model's central risk. In a 2023 review, Kannan, Gu and Li put typical free-to-premium conversion at 2–5% for most firms and warned that the model fails when conversion is too low.[^s2] Drawing on Vineet Kumar's 2014 *Harvard Business Review* analysis, they treat the conversion rate as a diagnostic: a low rate can mean the premium tier adds too little or the free tier gives away too much, while a very high rate can mean the free offer is not attracting enough users.[^s2]

Field and empirical research suggests how firms can improve conversion. In a randomized experiment on the National Academies Press website in 2016, which offered free PDFs alongside paid paperbacks, Gu, Kannan and Liye Ma found that adding a premium hardback raised paperback revenue by 8.9% and adding a premium e-book raised it by 21.5%, attributing the gains to compromise and attraction effects.[^s3] They cautioned that a cheaper premium option can also cannibalize the existing paid product.[^s3] A University of Maryland summary of the same work adds that too many paid options can stall buyers entirely.[^s6] In a free-to-play video game, Julian Runge, Harikesh Nair and Jonathan Levav found that randomized in-game price promotions increased conversion and revenue without evidence of buyers waiting for sales.[^s5] Anja Lambrecht and Kanishka Misra showed that content providers mixing free and paid content can benefit from offering more free content when demand is high, a policy they call countercyclical offering.[^s4]

Critics point out that a free tier that is too limited can alienate users, and that dependence on a small share of payers leaves a narrow revenue base.[^s1] Regulators have also increased scrutiny of in-app purchasing and privacy practices common in freemium apps.[^s1]

## In practice

Spotify illustrates the classic design: an ad-supported free tier and a paid tier, priced at $9.99 a month as of 2018, that removes ads and adds offline listening.[^s6] Many streaming services, by contrast, dropped free trials once the category became familiar; Kannan and colleagues argue that free offers matter most when customers are uncertain about a new product.[^s2]

## Connections

Freemium is a form of **pricing strategy** and a recognizable **business model**.[^s2] It relates to the **two-sided market**, since free users can be monetized through advertisers or other paying groups, and to **customer lifetime value**, because research finds that customers acquired through free offers can be worth much less than those from other channels.[^s2] The term was created by **Fred Wilson** (with Jarid Lukin).[^s1] The **free trial** is an alternative, time-limited way to let customers sample a product.[^s2] Freemium contrasts with the **razor-and-blades model**, where the cheap base item is a physical good tied to paid consumables rather than a free version of the same product. **Spotify** exemplifies it.[^s2][^s6]
