# Business Model Canvas

The **Business Model Canvas** is a single-page template for describing, designing and challenging a business model. Its authors, Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, define a business model as the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value, and the canvas splits that rationale into nine building blocks laid out on one sheet so a team can sketch and debate them together.[^s2] Strategyzer's overview of the tool dates the canvas to 2005 and presents it as a working instrument for corporate innovators and entrepreneurs.[^s5]

## Origin

The canvas grew out of academic work at the University of Lausanne. Osterwalder's 2004 doctoral dissertation, *The Business Model Ontology: A Proposition in a Design Science Approach*, built a formal meta-model of business models, and a 2005 tutorial by Osterwalder, Pigneur and Christopher Tucci condensed it into nine building blocks grouped under four pillars: product, customer interface, infrastructure management and financial aspects.[^s1] The authors framed the blocks as an ontology, an explicit shared vocabulary, because the field lacked one: a survey on an information-systems mailing list yielded 54 different definitions from 62 respondents, and the term's appearances in journal full texts rose from 7 articles in 1990 to 667 in 2003.[^s1] They deliberately left competition and implementation outside the blocks, treating them as the environment around a model rather than parts of it.[^s1]

The popular canvas format and its current block names arrived with the book *Business Model Generation* (Wiley, 2010), which the authors report was co-created with 470 practitioners from 45 countries.[^s2]

## How it works

The nine blocks of the 2010 canvas are:[^s2][^s5]

- **Customer Segments**: the distinct groups of people or organizations the business aims to serve.[^s2]
- **Value Propositions**: the bundle of products and services that creates value for a given segment.[^s2]
- **Channels**: how the offer reaches customers through communication, distribution and sales.[^s2]
- **Customer Relationships**: the kind of relationship maintained with each segment.[^s2]
- **Revenue Streams**: the cash generated from each segment.[^s2]
- **Key Resources**: the assets needed to make the model work.[^s2]
- **Key Activities**: the most important things the firm must do.[^s2]
- **Key Partnerships**: the suppliers and partners that supply outsourced activities and resources.[^s2]
- **Cost Structure**: the costs incurred to operate the model.[^s2]

The book numbers the blocks one to nine and groups them into four areas that echo the 2005 pillars: customers, offer, infrastructure and financial viability.[^s1][^s2] Strategyzer groups the blocks into questions of desirability, feasibility and viability, and adds a surrounding "adaptability" view of industry forces, market forces, key trends and macroeconomic forces.[^s5] The Customer Segments block also covers multi-sided models: a credit card company needs both cardholders and merchants, and a free newspaper needs readers to attract advertisers who in turn fund it, so each interdependent group is mapped as its own segment.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The canvas spread quickly through entrepreneurship teaching. Steve Blank's 2013 *Harvard Business Review* article made it the first step of the lean startup method: instead of writing a business plan, founders record their untested hypotheses in the nine blocks and then validate each one with customers.[^s3] Blank reported that lean methods were then taught at more than 25 universities, and that the US National Science Foundation had used them since 2011 in its Innovation Corps program for research scientists.[^s3]

Its creators and their collaborators have also catalogued its limits. Boris Fritscher and Pigneur note that most use stays basic, analysing one business model at a single point in time, a weakness they trace to the static ontology the canvas inherits.[^s4] Because that ontology covers the business itself rather than its environment, competitors and external forces are not part of the nine blocks; the 2005 tutorial treated them as context on purpose.[^s1][^s4] Fritscher and Pigneur also observed that users rarely recorded explicit links between elements and found it cumbersome to maintain several versions of a model, and they propose coherence rules, cause-and-effect "mechanics" and layered versioning to make the tool dynamic.[^s4] More broadly, a filled-in canvas is a description, not proof that the model is defensible or profitable, which is why Blank treats its contents as hypotheses.[^s3]

## Example

*Business Model Generation* uses Apple's iPod and iTunes store as a showcase. The book credits the pairing of the iPod player with the iTunes online store, rather than the device alone, with creating an innovative business model that made Apple the dominant force in online music.[^s2] Mapped on a canvas, the case shows how a value proposition can rest on a combination of offerings and the activities and resources behind them, not on a single product. Its catalogue of examples also includes Xerox's 1959 photocopier leasing with per-copy payment and Zipcar's membership-based hourly car rental.[^s2]

## Connections

The canvas is a tool for representing the broader concept of a **business model**, and it was created by **Alexander Osterwalder** and **Yves Pigneur**, building on Osterwalder's ontology and popularized in ***Business Model Generation*** (2010).[^s1][^s2] Its central block is the **value proposition**, and its multi-sided variant maps the logic of the **platform business model**.[^s2] It **influenced** the **lean startup** method, which adopted it as the founders' hypothesis map, and it is widely used as a working surface for **business model innovation**, a use its creators have tried to strengthen with dynamic extensions.[^s3][^s4] **Apple**'s iPod and iTunes model is the book's emblematic example.[^s2]
