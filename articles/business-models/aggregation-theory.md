# Aggregation Theory

**Aggregation theory** explains why a handful of internet companies came to dominate industries as different as news, retail, video and hotels. Its claim is that once the internet made distribution nearly free and cut transaction costs toward zero, the most valuable position in a market moved from controlling scarce supply to controlling demand: the firm that offers users the best experience gathers the most users, and suppliers then have to come to it on its terms.[^s1] Ben Thompson, who writes the subscription newsletter Stratechery, set out the idea in a July 2015 essay and refined it in later pieces.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin and the value-chain argument

Thompson's starting point is a simple value chain of suppliers, distributors and consumers. In his account, profits go either to a firm that holds a horizontal monopoly at one stage or to one that integrates two stages into a vertical solution. Before the internet, distributors usually integrated backward into supply, because consumers vastly outnumbered suppliers and reaching them was expensive; newspapers, book publishers, broadcasters, taxi medallion holders and hotel chains all fit this pattern.[^s1] He frames the shift with Clayton Christensen's conservation of attractive profits, the idea that when one stage of a chain becomes modular and commoditized, profits migrate to an adjacent stage that stays integrated.[^s1]

The internet broke the old model in two ways: distribution of digital goods became essentially free, and transaction costs fell so far that a distributor could deal directly with huge numbers of end users. Competition then turned on user experience rather than exclusive supply deals. Aggregators integrate the user relationship with modularized supply, and a virtuous cycle follows: a better experience attracts users, users attract suppliers, and more supply improves the experience again.[^s1]

## Defining an aggregator

In 2017 Thompson narrowed the definition to three traits: a direct relationship with users, effectively zero marginal cost of serving another user, and a demand-driven multi-sided network in which customer acquisition gets cheaper as the network grows.[^s2] He also graded aggregators by what supply costs them. Level 1 aggregators such as Netflix must buy their supply; Level 2 aggregators such as Uber and Airbnb do not own supply but pay to onboard and vet suppliers; Level 3 aggregators such as Google search and social networks get supply at no cost. "Super-aggregators" such as Google and Facebook run three-sided markets of users, suppliers and advertisers with zero marginal cost on every side.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Aggregation theory is a practitioner framework rather than an empirically tested model, and most of its support is illustrative case reasoning about Google, Facebook, Amazon, Netflix, Uber and Airbnb.[^s1][^s2] Thompson has also shown its limits. In 2022 he argued that Netflix's exclusive original content does not have the commoditized, scalable supply that aggregation needs: it still has to bid against rivals for programming, and its spending had not produced the hoped-for gains in subscribers. Spotify, whose music catalogue is much the same on every service, fits the model better, though label royalties still cap its gross margins.[^s5]

The theory has been taken up in competition law. Legal scholar Thibault Schrepel argues that "platforms" (bases on which others build, such as Android or Apple's developer tools) should be separated from "aggregators" (which collect and sort existing information, products and services for users, such as Google Search or Amazon's store).[^s4] He contends the two belong in different relevant markets, can exclude rivals in different ways, and expose users to different harms; aggregators are more prone to exploitative conduct because suppliers depend on reaching their users.[^s4] A peer-reviewed version of this argument appeared in the *Journal of European Competition Law & Practice* in 2021.[^s3]

## In practice

Strategists use the framework to diagnose whether an industry is open to aggregation: whether its core function can be digitized, whether supply can be commoditized, and who owns the customer relationship. Google is the canonical case. It turned individual web pages into searchable modules, kept the user relationship, and paired search results with user data to sell targeted advertising, while publishers became interchangeable suppliers.[^s1]

## Connections

Aggregation theory is a specific account of the **platform business model** and **two-sided markets**: it stresses demand-side control and commoditized supply rather than platforms as foundations for third parties, a distinction that Schrepel's antitrust work formalizes.[^s4] It relies on **network effects** and falling acquisition costs to explain winner-take-all outcomes,[^s2] and it extends the **long tail** logic that near-zero distribution cost lets one intermediary aggregate dispersed supply.[^s1] Its reading of incumbent decline draws on Christensen's work behind **disruptive innovation**, and its policy implications run through **antitrust law**.[^s1][^s3]
