# Razor and Blades

The **razor-and-blades model** (also called bait and hook) is a business model in which a firm prices a durable base product low, sometimes below cost, and recovers its margin on consumables or complements that buyers purchase again and again: blades for a razor, cartridges for a printer, games for a console.[^s1] Economists describe the structure as a two-part tariff, where the base product acts like an entry fee and the consumable like a per-use charge, which lets the seller earn more from heavy users than from light ones.[^s2]

## Origin

The model is conventionally credited to King C. Gillette, whose safety razor made thin, disposable blades practical. His patents were issued on 15 November 1904 with a 17-year term that ran to November 1921.[^s1] The historical record, however, does not fit the legend. In the University of Chicago Law Review in 2011, Randal Picker showed that during the patent years Gillette did the opposite of giving razors away: from mid-1904 it sold a razor with 12 blades for $5, roughly a third of an average weekly industrial wage in 1900, and charged $1 for each extra dozen blades, while rivals sold handles for about $1.[^s1] Only when the patents expired in 1921 did Gillette launch the low-priced "Brownie" razor at a $1 list price alongside a new $5 model; its razor-set sales roughly doubled from about 2.09 million in 1920 to 4.25 million in 1921.[^s1]

## How it works

The logic rests on three conditions. First, the base product and the consumable must be complements, so that each sale of the base creates a stream of future consumable purchases.[^s1] Second, the firm must capture that stream, typically through patents, proprietary designs or contractual and technical "locks" that stop rivals from selling compatible consumables.[^s1] Third, buyers must differ in how intensively they use the product: a low entry price draws in light users, while consumable margins extract more from heavy users, which makes the model a form of price discrimination.[^s2] Marketing research treats razors and blades as the archetypal case of such "tied goods", and models demand for them as dynamic because consumers stockpile the consumable and choose which retail format to buy it from.[^s3][^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

The model is better known than it is supported. Picker argues that two myths surround it: that Gillette invented and practiced it, and that it requires a lock between base and consumable. Gillette's profits rose after 1921 even though no lock existed and Sears was selling compatible third-party blades from 1925, which sits awkwardly with standard theory.[^s1] He also notes that a free razor creates little lock-in by itself, since a rival can give its razor away too; genuine switching costs arise only when the base product strands an investment, as when a console owner's game library does not carry over to a rival system.[^s1]

Richard Schmalensee's 2015 analysis in the International Journal of Industrial Organization reaches a related conclusion on pricing. Building on Walter Oi's 1971 result that a monopolist should price the consumable above cost, he shows that pricing the razor below cost is never optimal in a standard continuum model and is optimal only in a narrow range of cases with two buyer types. He concludes below-cost razors are unlikely to be optimal in practice and that such pricing, when used for price discrimination, should not automatically be treated as predatory.[^s2]

## In practice

Inkjet and laser printing is the most-cited modern example. HP Inc.'s Printing segment had net revenue of $18,029 million in fiscal 2023, of which Supplies contributed $11,452 million, and the segment earned 18.9% of net revenue as operating profit.[^s5] The same filing shows the model's vulnerabilities: supplies revenue fell 2.6% that year, which HP attributed mainly to a shrinking installed base and lower usage, and the company identifies non-original supplies, including imitation, refill and remanufactured cartridges, as competition. In response it is steering demand toward HP+ and "profit upfront" printers that earn more on the hardware itself.[^s5]

## Connections

The model is a type of **business model** and a **pricing strategy**, and its economics are studied in **industrial organization** as a two-part tariff and a vehicle for **price discrimination**.[^s2] Its durability depends on **switching costs** and on the firm's control of compatible complements.[^s1] It is related to the **two-sided market**, where one side is subsidized to profit from another, but here the subsidy and the profit come from the same customer.[^s2] It contrasts with **freemium**, which gives away a version of the same product rather than a base that drives consumable purchases, and it is exemplified, if imperfectly, by **Gillette**.[^s1]
