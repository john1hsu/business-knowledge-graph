# Long Tail: Aggregating Demand for Niche Products

The **long tail** describes what happens to a market's demand curve when the cost of stocking and distributing one more product falls close to zero. Physical retailers can only carry items that sell enough copies to cover shelf space, so they concentrate on the "head" of popular hits. Online catalogs can carry millions of titles, and the many low-selling items in the "tail" can together rival or exceed the hits.[^s1] As a business model, the long tail means earning money by aggregating thin, geographically scattered demand for niche products.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Chris Anderson, then editor of *Wired*, introduced the idea in the magazine's October 2004 issue. He later expanded it into a book.[^s1][^s6] Anderson drew on data from digital retailers. More than half of Amazon's book sales came from titles outside its top 130,000, roughly the stock of an average Barnes & Noble. A fifth of Netflix rentals fell outside its top 3,000 titles, while a Blockbuster store stocked fewer than 3,000 DVDs. Every track in Rhapsody's top 400,000 was streamed at least once a month (all figures as of 2004).[^s1] Speaking in 2005, he argued that online a hit and a miss stand on "equal economic footing," because an obscure sale carries the same margin as a popular one.[^s5]

## How it works

Anderson summed up the model in three rules. Make everything available, because aggregating dispersed audiences makes niches pay. Cut prices, since digital delivery costs less than physical delivery. And help customers find things, using hits as entry points and recommendations to lead buyers into the tail.[^s1] He named three kinds of business that profit from the shift: aggregators that assemble huge catalogs, niche suppliers, and filters such as reviews and collaborative recommendation.[^s5]

Research separates two drivers. On the supply side, digital catalogs, centralized warehouses and print-on-demand lower the cost of offering obscure items. On the demand side, search, sampling, reviews and recommender systems lower consumers' cost of finding them.[^s4] Brynjolfsson, Hu and Simester tested the demand side using a multichannel retailer that sold the same products at the same prices online and through its catalog. The online channel still had a markedly less concentrated sales distribution, and customers who used search and recommendation tools bought a higher share of niche products. That points to lower search costs, not just wider selection, as part of the cause.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest economic case concerns consumer welfare. In a 2003 *Management Science* paper, Brynjolfsson, Hu and Smith estimated that Amazon's book selection was more than 23 times that of a typical Barnes & Noble superstore. They estimated that greater variety at online booksellers raised consumer welfare by $731 million to $1.03 billion in 2000, which is 7 to 10 times the gain from lower prices and more competition.[^s2] Follow-up work in *MIT Sloan Management Review* put Amazon's sales of books not typically stocked in physical stores at 30% to 40% of the total.[^s4]

The main critique is that the head still dominates. In a 2008 *Harvard Business Review* article, Harvard Business School's Anita Elberse analyzed Rhapsody data covering more than 32 million plays over three months. The top 10% of titles drew 78% of plays and the top 1% drew 32%.[^s6] She found that heavy users explored the tail while light users stayed with popular items. Consumers of all kinds rated obscure titles less enjoyable than hits, so she advised firms not to abandon blockbuster strategies.[^s6] The MIT Sloan research likewise warns that weaker hit economics could reduce the funding available to develop new talent.[^s4]

## In practice

Amazon and Netflix's DVD-by-mail service are the canonical examples. Netflix said 60% of its rentals came from recommendations (as of 2004), which shows how filtering converts a vast catalog into demand.[^s1] Managers apply the idea by investing in discovery tools and by using cheap production and distribution to test niche offerings at low risk. They should also expect that a broad catalog still needs popular items to draw customers in.[^s4][^s6]

## Connections

The long tail is a kind of **business model**, and its most successful practitioners run catalogs or **platforms** that aggregate many suppliers. Anderson named aggregators as one of the three kinds of long-tail business, which links the idea to **aggregation theory**.[^s5] It **contrasts with** the **80/20 rule**. Brynjolfsson and colleagues framed their findings as a move away from Pareto-style concentration, observing that online sales are less concentrated than catalog sales.[^s3][^s4] It **depends on** **recommender systems** and search to lower consumers' search costs.[^s3] It is **exemplified by** **Amazon** and by **Netflix's** DVD rental model, and it was **created by** **Chris Anderson** in his 2004 *Wired* article.[^s1]
