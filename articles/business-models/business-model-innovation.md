# Business Model Innovation

**Business model innovation (BMI)** is the deliberate redesign of how a firm creates, delivers and captures value, rather than of what it sells. Nicolai Foss and Tina Saebi define it as designed, novel and nontrivial changes to the key elements of a business model and/or the architecture that links those elements; the novelty and nontriviality conditions are there to exclude simple imitation and minor tweaks.[^s1] The idea matters because the same technology can earn very different returns under different business models, so a firm that cannot change its model may fail to profit from its own inventions.[^s2]

## Origin and development

Interest in BMI grew in the 2000s alongside the wider business-model literature. Henry Chesbrough's 2010 article in *Long Range Planning* framed the core problem: companies invest heavily in new ideas and technologies yet often lack the capacity to innovate the business models that carry them to market.[^s2] In the same year Raphael Amit and Christoph Zott, writing at IESE, described a business model as an activity system: a bundle of activities, who performs them (the focal firm or its partners), and how they are linked.[^s4] Mark Johnson, Clayton Christensen and Henning Kagermann's 2008 *Harvard Business Review* article gave managers a four-part template (customer value proposition, profit formula, key resources and key processes).[^s3] By 2015 the field had produced roughly 150 peer-reviewed articles, which Foss and Saebi reviewed systematically.[^s1]

## How it works

Amit and Zott identify three design elements that can be innovated alone or together: **content** (which activities are performed), **structure** (how they are linked and sequenced) and **governance** (who performs each one). They separate the business model from the revenue model, which determines how much of the value created the firm keeps, and argue that whole activity systems are harder for rivals to copy than individual products or processes.[^s4] Foss and Saebi add a two-by-two typology. Change can be **modular** (one component) or **architectural** (the links among components), and it can be new to the firm or new to the industry. The result is four types: evolutionary, adaptive, focused and complex BMI.[^s1]

Johnson and colleagues list circumstances that often justify a new model. These include reaching customers shut out of a market by cost or complexity, commercializing a new technology, introducing a jobs-to-be-done focus where none exists, fending off low-end disruptors and responding when core products commoditize.[^s3]

## Barriers

Chesbrough argues that BMI is hard mainly because the new model conflicts with assets and configurations that support the current one, and because managers have trouble even perceiving those conflicts. His remedies are experimentation, effectuation-style action under uncertainty and deliberate leadership of organizational change.[^s2] Johnson and colleagues report a parallel underinvestment: an American Management Association study they cite found that no more than 10% of global companies' innovation investment went to developing new business models.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence that BMI pays off is suggestive rather than settled. CEO surveys by IBM (2006) and the Economist Intelligence Unit ranked business model innovation as a key source of value creation, even ahead of new products and services. Foss and Saebi treat these surveys as adding legitimacy rather than rigorous proof.[^s1] Some studies link novel business models to better performance in entrepreneurial firms, while others find no significant difference in financial results across BMI types. Few explain how the effect works, partly because outcomes lag the change by years.[^s1] The review's main critique is that the literature lacks theoretical grounding and construct clarity, and that its empirical work has not been cumulative.[^s1]

## Example

Johnson, Christensen and Kagermann cite Apple's iPod and iTunes. Earlier digital music players had capable technology, but Apple paired hardware, software and a store that sold songs cheaply in order to drive sales of high-margin devices. The authors report that the combination produced nearly $10 billion in revenue within three years, close to half of Apple's revenue at the time of writing in 2008.[^s3] Their second case, the power-tool maker Hilti, moved from selling tools to leasing a managed fleet for a monthly fee covering maintenance, repair and replacement. That change forced the firm to build service capabilities alongside manufacturing and distribution.[^s3]

## Connections

BMI is a kind of change to a **business model** and a branch of **innovation management** that targets the logic of the firm rather than its offerings.[^s1] The **Business Model Canvas** is a common tool for mapping the elements a BMI effort alters. **Servitization** is one recurring pattern, as Hilti's fleet model shows.[^s3] Chesbrough's prescription ties BMI to **effectuation** and experimentation, and **Henry Chesbrough** is a leading influence on the field.[^s2] The **jobs-to-be-done** lens supplies the customer value proposition at the centre of the Johnson–Christensen–Kagermann template.[^s3] **Apple** is the standard illustration.[^s3]
