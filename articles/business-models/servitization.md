# Servitization: From Selling Products to Selling Outcomes

**Servitization** is the movement of manufacturing firms from selling products toward selling combinations of products and services, and in its most developed form toward selling the use, availability or result that the product delivers. Sandra Vandermerwe and Juan Rada coined the term in a 1988 European Management Journal article, observing that firms in almost every industry were offering fuller "bundles" of goods, services, support, self-service and knowledge, and that services were coming to dominate those bundles.[^s1] A 2009 literature review by T. S. Baines and colleagues defined servitization as innovating an organization's capabilities and processes so that it can shift from selling products to selling integrated product-service systems.[^s2]

## Origin

Vandermerwe and Rada presented servitization as a customer-driven trend that firms were adopting to strengthen their competitive position, and argued that its cumulative effect was changing the basis of competition across industries. Their main message to senior managers was that services had to be integrated into corporate strategy rather than treated as an afterthought to the product.[^s1] Baines and colleagues found roughly 60 papers on servitization between 1988 and 2008, plus about 90 more in closely related fields, with authorship concentrated in the United States, the United Kingdom and Switzerland.[^s2]

## How it works

The literature describes a spectrum. At one end, services such as maintenance, spare parts and training support the sale of a product that the customer owns. In the middle, use-oriented offerings such as leasing or pooling let the provider keep ownership. At the far end, result-oriented offerings sell a guaranteed outcome, so the provider takes on risks the customer used to carry.[^s4][^s5] Andy Neely's analysis of 10,028 firms in 25 countries extended the classic product-, use- and result-oriented categories with integration-oriented and service-oriented types and identified 12 distinct servitization strategies in practice.[^s4]

Baines and colleagues group the motives into three drivers. Financially, services promise higher margins and steadier income, and in sectors with large installed bases service revenue can exceed new-product sales by one or two orders of magnitude. Strategically, services differentiate an offering and are harder to copy, which helps a firm escape commoditization. In marketing terms, services support product sales, build loyalty and reveal customer needs.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The financial case is weaker than the rhetoric. Heiko Gebauer, Elgar Fleisch and Thomas Friedli, working with more than 30 equipment manufacturers, described a **service paradox**: firms invested heavily in expanding services, raising both offerings and costs, but did not earn correspondingly higher returns.[^s3] Neely's 2008 study found that servitized manufacturers generated higher revenues than pure manufacturers but lower profits as a share of sales, that servitization appeared to pay off in smaller firms but not in larger ones, and that a higher proportion of servitized firms in his sample had gone bankrupt.[^s4] Baines and colleagues trace part of the problem to organization: service management principles often clash with manufacturing practice, and the transition needs a change of culture and mindset that meets internal resistance.[^s2]

Product-service systems are also promoted as a route to sustainability, on the theory that a provider paid for results will use fewer materials. Arnold Tukker's study of eight PSS types for the EU SusProNet network concluded that most types produce at best marginal environmental gains; only the functional-result type has large potential, and it first requires solving liability and risk-premium problems.[^s5]

## In practice

The canonical example is aero-engine maintenance, which Baines and colleagues cite as Rolls-Royce's "power by the hour", alongside Xerox's move from photocopiers to document management.[^s2] Under Rolls-Royce's long-term service agreements, airlines pay for each engine flying hour, and the company maintains the engine to an agreed standard, bearing the cost of shop visits and keeping the gain if engines stay on wing longer. As of early 2024 it had 248 large-engine agreements covering about 4,000 engines, and more than 90% of Trent engines were on such contracts.[^s6] The model also shows the accounting complexity of servitization: under IFRS 15, revenue is recognized as costs are incurred, so cash received and reported revenue diverge from year to year, and changes in cost estimates trigger catch-up adjustments.[^s6]

## Connections

Servitization is a form of **business model innovation** applied to the **business model** of a manufacturer, because it changes how value is delivered and captured, not only what is sold.[^s2] Its endpoint is the **product-service system**, the closely related and largely Scandinavian research field that is converging with servitization research.[^s2] Its pay-per-use and availability contracts resemble the recurring revenue of the **subscription business model**, and delivering the services draws on **services marketing**.[^s4] The concept was created by **Sandra Vandermerwe** with Juan Rada in their 1988 article, and **Rolls-Royce** is the case most often used to illustrate it.[^s1][^s6]
