# Direct Selling

**Direct selling** is a business model in which a manufacturer or brand reaches consumers through independent salespeople who sell in person, typically in homes, workplaces or other places that are not a store, rather than through wholesalers and retail outlets.[^s1] In their foundational review, marketing scholars Robert A. Peterson and Thomas R. Wotruba framed it as face-to-face selling away from a fixed retail location and stressed that it is often confused with **direct marketing** (catalogs, telemarketing, direct-response ads), which involves no personal salesperson.[^s1] They also noted that it was sometimes equated with pyramid schemes, which they treated as a degenerate form of the practice rather than its definition.[^s1]

## How it works

The company supplies product, training and a compensation plan; the sellers are independent contractors rather than employees, so the firm carries little fixed sales cost and pays mainly through commissions and discounts.[^s1] In **single-level** direct selling, a representative earns only on personal sales. In **multi-level marketing (MLM)**, also called network marketing, sellers additionally earn overrides on purchases by the "downline" of people they recruit, and advancement can depend on recruiting.[^s7]

Herbalife's filings show the mechanics of a large multi-level plan. As of 31 December 2024 it reported 6.2 million members, including 3.0 million preferred members and 2.1 million distributors in markets that split the two, and it sold in 95 markets.[^s5] Distributors buy at up to a 50% discount to suggested retail price and resell at a profit, and can also earn royalty overrides of up to 15% and production bonuses of up to 7% on their organization's sales.[^s5]

## Scale

The World Federation of Direct Selling Associations, an industry body, estimates global direct-selling retail sales at $163.9 billion in 2024, roughly flat on 2023, generated by 104.3 million independent representatives, 72.1% of them women.[^s6] Its largest markets in 2024 were the United States, Germany, China and Korea, and wellness, cosmetics and personal care, and household goods are the leading categories.[^s6] These are industry-reported figures, and the WFDSA describes them as estimates.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

The central regulatory question is whether a plan rewards **sales to real customers** or **recruitment**. In a January 2017 letter to the Direct Selling Association, FTC Chairwoman Edith Ramirez said lawful plans tie compensation to verifiable, profitable retail sales to customers outside the network, should not pay for recruiting, and should treat participants' "personal consumption" with skepticism, since targets that can be met by participants buying product themselves let a plan run on its own sellers.[^s2] She also warned against unrealistic "lifestyle" earnings claims.[^s2] FTC staff turned these principles into formal business guidance in January 2018, noting that legality depends on a fact-specific investigation of each plan.[^s3]

Enforcement shows what goes wrong. The FTC alleged in 2019 that AdvoCare, a seller of energy drinks and supplements, paid distributors mainly for recruiting others who spent heavily to join; its complaint said 72% of distributors earned nothing or lost money and another 18% earned between a penny and $250 a year.[^s4] Herbalife's 2016 FTC consent order now requires U.S. distributor rewards to be based on preferred-member purchases, documented profitable retail sales and capped personal consumption, with no volume credit until a product is sold to a customer at a profit.[^s5][^s2] Critics go further, arguing that most MLM participants lose money once costs are counted and that the model depends on continuous network growth; several countries, including China, restrict or ban multi-level plans.[^s7]

## In practice

For a brand, direct selling trades margin for reach: it can enter markets without shelf space or a retail partner, uses sellers' social networks as a low-cost acquisition channel, and suits products that benefit from demonstration.[^s1][^s7] The risks are compliance and reputation. Since the 2016 Herbalife order, the safer design separates customers who buy for their own use from distributors who resell, and documents retail sales before paying any reward.[^s5]

## Connections

Direct selling is a type of **business model** and a **distribution channel** that bypasses retail intermediaries.[^s1] It contrasts with **direct marketing**, which reaches consumers without a salesperson, and with the **pyramid scheme**, the illegal form in which rewards flow from recruitment rather than customer sales.[^s1][^s2] It is a form of **personal selling** and shares with **franchising** the use of independent operators to extend a brand. Its abuses rest on **information asymmetry** about likely earnings, which is why U.S. oversight under the **Federal Trade Commission Act** focuses on compensation design and earnings claims.[^s2][^s3] **Herbalife** exemplifies the multi-level form and its post-2016 compliance model.[^s5]
