# Sharing Economy: Access-Based Peer-to-Peer Exchange

The **sharing economy** is a business model in which an online platform connects people who own underused assets, such as a car, a spare room or spare hours, with people who want to use them for a while, and takes a fee for brokering the deal. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission's 2016 staff report used the term for peer-to-peer platforms and the largely commercial activity on them, noting that the label itself is disputed. Unlike online retailers, these platforms do not sell the goods or services themselves; they run marketplaces in which suppliers are usually individuals or small firms, as on Uber, Lyft, Airbnb, eBay and TaskRabbit.[^s1] Academic work often uses the near-synonym **collaborative consumption** for peer-to-peer sharing of goods and services enabled by information and communications technology.[^s4]

## Origin

The model grew out of smartphones, GPS and app-based payments, which cut the cost of finding a trading partner and completing a small transaction with a stranger. By the mid-2010s it had reached large scale: the FTC report cited a PricewaterhouseCoopers estimate that five sharing sectors earned about $15 billion of worldwide revenue in 2013, projected to reach $335 billion by 2025, and a 2015 Pew survey in which 11% of U.S. adults had used a home-sharing service and 15% a ride-hailing app.[^s1] The FTC held a public workshop on the topic in June 2015 and published its report in November 2016 after receiving more than 2,000 public comments.[^s1]

## How it works

The FTC staff identified a handful of economic features. Platforms need **thick markets** on both sides, which creates two-sided network effects and a chicken-and-egg problem at launch. They reduce **search and matching** frictions, for example by pairing a rider with a nearby driver. They lower **transaction costs** through mobile technology. Suppliers face **low fixed costs** because they reuse personal assets, so entry is easy and supply can expand quickly.[^s1] Finally, platforms build **trust** with reputation ratings, background checks, guarantees, refunds and insurance. The report warned that ratings can be inflated, skewed toward extreme experiences, faked, unhelpful for newcomers, or "milked" by users about to leave.[^s1]

Revenue usually comes from a commission on each transaction. Airbnb's FY2025 Form 10-K explains that it holds a guest's payment until check-in and then recognizes its service fee as revenue; the company reported more than 5 million hosts and more than 2.5 billion cumulative guest arrivals across over 220 countries and regions.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Flexible supply is the model's distinctive competitive weapon. Georgios Zervas, Davide Proserpio and John Byers studied Airbnb's entry into Texas using hotel revenue data from 2003 to 2014. They estimated that each 10% rise in Airbnb listings cut hotel room revenue by about 0.39% statewide, with an 8–10% impact on the most exposed hotels in Austin.[^s2][^s3] Lower-priced hotels and those without conference facilities were hit hardest, and hotels responded mainly by pricing less aggressively, especially at peaks such as the SXSW festival, when Airbnb supply swelled by about 60%.[^s3] That benefits even travellers who never use the platform.[^s2]

Researchers also question how much "sharing" is involved. Fleura Bardhi and Giana Eckhardt's study of Zipcar users described **access-based consumption** as market-mediated use without ownership, and found that users did not identify with the cars, showed negative reciprocity toward them, invited surveillance-style governance by the provider, and formed little brand community.[^s5] Juho Hamari, Mimmi Sjöklint and Antti Ukkonen surveyed 168 members of a collaborative-consumption site and found that participation was driven by enjoyment and economic gain; sustainability mattered mainly for people already positive about the practice, and favourable attitudes often did not translate into use.[^s4]

Regulation is the main business risk. The FTC catalogued disputes over driver vetting, insurance, unpaid lodging taxes, conversion of housing into full-time rentals, access for riders with disabilities, privacy, and whether incumbents and platforms should face the same rules; it urged that regulation be evidence-based and no more restrictive than necessary, and set aside worker classification as outside its remit.[^s1] As of its FY2025 10-K, Airbnb still listed short-term rental laws as a material risk, citing New York City's effective ban and the EU short-term rental regulation taking effect in May 2026.[^s6]

## In practice

Airbnb illustrates the asset-light logic: the platform owns no rooms, earns a service fee, and scales supply through hosts, but must manage trust, payments and local rules in every market it serves.[^s6] Its effect on hotels shows the strategic consequence for incumbents, which lose the ability to charge peak prices when peer supply can expand on demand.[^s2][^s3]

## Connections

The sharing economy is a variant of the **platform business model** and a type of **business model**, and its marketplaces are **two-sided markets** governed by cross-side **network effects**.[^s1] Its rationale draws on **transaction cost economics**, since technology lowers the cost of trading with strangers, and its reliance on ratings is a response to **information asymmetry** between parties, which is why it **depends on reputation systems**.[^s1] It contrasts with **access-based consumption** offered by firms that own the assets, as in Zipcar.[^s5] It overlaps with the **gig economy** where the shared asset is labour, is **exemplified by Airbnb** and **Uber**, and is **regulated by short-term rental regulation** in many cities.[^s1][^s6]
