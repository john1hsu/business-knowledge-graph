# Platform Business Model

A **platform business model** creates value by connecting outside parties rather than by making and selling products itself. Marshall Van Alstyne, Geoffrey Parker and Sangeet Paul Choudary, writing in *Harvard Business Review* in April 2016, set it against the traditional **pipeline**, a firm that wins by optimizing the activities of a value chain it largely owns or controls. A platform instead brings producers and consumers together, and its critical asset sits outside the firm: the community of members who transact on it.[^s1] Uber, Airbnb and Alibaba are the canonical examples; as Michael Cusumano, Annabelle Gawer and David Yoffie note, they own no cars, real estate or inventory respectively.[^s1][^s6]

## Origin and definitions

Platform thinking has two academic roots. Industrial-organization economists studied *multi-sided platforms* (MSPs) and how they price to two interdependent user groups. Andrei Hagiu and Julian Wright (2015) frame the MSP as one end of a spectrum of organizational choices, alongside vertically integrated firms, resellers and input suppliers, and model the trade-off between running an MSP and integrating vertically.[^s3] Management scholars, meanwhile, studied technology platforms: Gawer and Cusumano distinguish *internal* (company or product) platforms, shared assets a firm reuses across its own product line, from *external* or industry platforms that outside firms build complements on, so that innovation spreads across an ecosystem rather than staying inside one company.[^s2] Cusumano, Gawer and Yoffie's 2019 book *The Business of Platforms* combines these views into three types: **innovation platforms** (Windows, Android, iOS, AWS) that enable third-party complements, **transaction platforms** (Amazon Marketplace, Google Search, Uber) that reduce friction between buyers and sellers, and **hybrids** that do both.[^s5][^s6]

## How it works

Platforms rest on demand-side economies of scale, that is, network effects: each additional participant or offering makes the platform more valuable to others, and accumulating data improves matching, whereas industrial firms leaned on supply-side scale and defensive moats.[^s4] Van Alstyne, Parker and Choudary describe three resulting shifts in strategy. Firms move from controlling resources to orchestrating them, because the hard-to-copy asset is the community and what its members contribute. They move from internal optimization to facilitating external interactions, which often lets the platform shed the variable cost of production. And they move from maximizing individual customer value to maximizing the value of the whole ecosystem through feedback-driven growth.[^s1][^s4] Industry boundaries also blur, so that competitors can become complementors and rivals can come from unrelated industries or from inside the platform itself.[^s1][^s4]

Launching a platform means solving the chicken-and-egg problem. Common tactics are to give one side standalone value first, to subsidize one or both sides, or to bring both sides on together; Airbnb, for example, began with hosts, sourcing listings from Craigslist and paying for professional photography of homes.[^s6]

## Evidence and critiques

Successful platforms have outperformed conventional firms. In a study of 43 platform companies over 1995–2015, Cusumano, Gawer and Yoffie found higher sales growth, market-value growth and operating profits than non-platform firms in the Fortune Global 2000, achieved with fewer employees (under 10,000 on average versus about 19,000) and more R&D spending.[^s5] The headline example is mobile phones: in 2007 five incumbent handset makers held 90% of global industry profits, yet by 2015 Apple's iPhone took 92%.[^s1]

The same research tempers the hype. Yoffie's team counted 209 platform failures against about 45 lasting successes over 1995–2015.[^s6] Most digital platforms fail, usually within five years, and B2B marketplaces, web browsers and ride-sharing platforms fared worst.[^s5] The recurring causes were mispricing subsidies, failing to build trust between strangers (eBay's retreat from China by 2007 against Taobao's escrow payments), mistimed entry (Windows Phone after the iPhone), hubris toward rivals (Internet Explorer's fall from 95% share in 2004 to about 5% in 2016), and the expense of attracting outside participants, as with GE's Predix.[^s5][^s6] Network effects are necessary but not sufficient: winner-take-all outcomes also require that users find it hard to multihome and that few niches exist.[^s6] Hagiu and Wright add that the platform form is not always superior; vertical integration coordinates spillovers better, while an MSP is better when suppliers' effort and private information matter most.[^s3]

## In practice

Conventional firms have three routes into platforms: join an existing one (cheap, but exposed to hold-up by the owner), buy one (Walmart's purchase of Jet.com), or build one, which rarely succeeds.[^s6] Mature platforms also face governance costs; Yoffie points to Facebook's roughly 30,000 content-curation staff for over 2.5 billion users (as of 2019) as the price of keeping user trust.[^s6]

## Connections

The model is a type of **business model** whose core economics are those of the **two-sided market**, and it depends on **network effects**.[^s1][^s3] Hagiu and Wright position it against **vertical integration**, the pipeline extreme of the same spectrum.[^s3] Its external, complement-producing community is a **business ecosystem** in Gawer and Cusumano's sense,[^s2] and **aggregation theory** describes one way transaction platforms come to dominate demand. The **Apple iPhone launch** is the standard illustration of a platform overtaking pipeline incumbents.[^s1]
