# Cooperative: Member-Owned, Democratically Governed Enterprise

A cooperative (co-op) is an enterprise owned and run for the benefit of the people who use its services, rather than for outside investors.[^s2] The International Cooperative Alliance (ICA), the movement's global body, defines it as an autonomous association of people who unite voluntarily to meet shared economic, social and cultural needs through a jointly owned, democratically controlled enterprise.[^s1] The owners are the firm's patrons: shoppers in a consumer co-op, farmers or small businesses in a marketing or purchasing co-op, depositors and borrowers in a credit co-op, and employees in a worker co-op.[^s2][^s3]

## Origin

Modern consumer cooperation is usually dated to 1844, when the Rochdale Equitable Pioneers Society opened a store in northern England.[^s2] Its rules (open membership, democratic control, no religious or political discrimination, selling at prevailing market prices, and setting aside part of earnings for education) were widely copied, and the movement spread rapidly in the late 19th century and later into Latin America and Africa.[^s2] The ICA codified the descendant of those rules in its 1995 Statement on the Cooperative Identity.[^s1]

## How it works

The ICA's seven principles define the model: voluntary and open membership; democratic member control, with one member, one vote in primary co-ops; member economic participation, in which members contribute and control the capital and decide how surpluses are used; autonomy and independence; education, training and information; cooperation among cooperatives; and concern for community.[^s1] Two features distinguish co-op economics from those of an investor-owned company. Votes follow membership, not shares, so control cannot be bought. And surplus is returned as a patronage dividend in proportion to how much each member bought from or sold through the co-op during the period, not in proportion to capital held.[^s1][^s2]

Large co-ops add financial engineering around this core. CHS Inc., a U.S. agricultural co-op owned by farmers, ranchers and member cooperatives, distributes earnings from member business on the basis of patronage, paid in cash, in patrons' equities, or both; the board decides at its discretion when those equities are redeemed.[^s6] To raise outside capital without giving up member control, CHS lists five series of preferred stock on Nasdaq.[^s6] In fiscal 2025 (year ended August 31, 2025) it reported revenues of $35.5 billion and net income attributable to CHS of $597.9 million.[^s6]

## Why co-ops exist where they do: the Hansmann theory

Henry Hansmann's theory of enterprise ownership explains which patrons end up owning a firm. Ownership goes to the patron group that minimizes the sum of two costs: the cost of contracting through the market for the patrons who do not own the firm, and the cost of ownership for those who do.[^s3] Market contracting is costly when the firm has market power, when patrons are locked in after making transaction-specific investments, or when patrons cannot judge quality. Making those patrons owners removes the firm's incentive to exploit them.[^s3] Ownership costs include monitoring, risk-bearing and, above all, collective decision-making, which becomes expensive when owners' interests diverge.[^s3]

The theory predicts the observed pattern. Co-ops cluster where patrons are homogeneous and have a lot at stake: farm supply and marketing, wholesale purchasing groups for grocers and hardware stores, and rural electricity. Consumer retail co-ops are rarer because dispersed, transient shoppers are costly to organize.[^s3] Worker co-ops do best in professional services and trades where members do similar, separable work, not in large hierarchies with a fine division of labor.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Critics have long argued that worker co-ops under-invest, stay small and survive poorly. A 2015 review by Virginie Pérotin largely rejects those claims. Studies from France and Uruguay find worker co-ops survive at least as well as conventional firms.[^s4] Productivity studies of U.S. plywood co-ops and French co-ops find their way of organizing production is at least as productive as conventional firms' methods.[^s4] Co-ops in Italy and France are, if anything, larger than conventional firms, and French co-ops invest no less.[^s4] The clearest difference is behavior over the business cycle: co-ops tend to cut pay rather than jobs in downturns, which stabilizes employment but may slow hiring in booms.[^s4] Pérotin cautions that the evidence covers only a few countries and periods.[^s4] Hansmann's framework names the model's structural weakness: when members' interests diverge, collective decisions become slow and contentious, so a co-op works best with a homogeneous membership.[^s3] Equity redeemed only at the board's discretion also limits members' exit.[^s6]

## In practice

U.S. agriculture shows the model at scale. In USDA's survey of fiscal 2024 (released January 2026), 1,620 farmer, rancher and fishery co-ops had about 1.74 million members, $248.0 billion in net business volume, roughly $11.0 billion in pre-tax net income and 189,764 employees.[^s5] Both the number of co-ops and membership were lower than in 2023.[^s5]

## Connections

The cooperative is an ownership-based variant of the business model, an alternative to the investor-owned firm, and it contrasts with shareholder primacy because the firm is run for its patrons, not for providers of capital.[^s1][^s3] Hansmann's theory builds on transaction cost economics, and making patrons owners mitigates the hold-up problem that arises when they are locked in after making specific investments.[^s3] Like the separation of ownership and control in public companies, co-ops face agency problems between dispersed members and managers, and their community principle overlaps with stakeholder theory.[^s1][^s3] The Rochdale Pioneers are the model's originators, and CHS is a large contemporary exemplar; membership warehouse clubs borrow the membership fee but remain investor-owned.[^s2][^s6]
