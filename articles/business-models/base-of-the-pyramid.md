# Base of the Pyramid: Low-Income Markets as a Business Opportunity

The **base of the pyramid** (BoP, also "bottom of the pyramid") is the idea that the world's poorest consumers, long ignored by multinational companies, are a large market that can be served at a profit, and that doing so can also reduce poverty. C.K. Prahalad and Stuart L. Hart made the case in a 2002 strategy+business article. They described a bottom tier of about 4 billion people living on per-capita incomes below $1,500 a year at purchasing-power parity, more than a billion of whom lived on less than $1 a day. They argued that managers' assumptions about these consumers were outdated and should be tested.[^s1]

## Origin

Prahalad and Hart framed the poor as a growth opportunity rather than a charity case. They pointed to widening inequality: the richest fifth of the world's population had received about 70% of global income in 1960 and about 85% in 2000.[^s1] They listed a set of long-held assumptions that kept multinationals away from poor markets and urged managers to re-examine each one. Prahalad then developed the argument into his 2004 book. Knowledge at Wharton covered the book ahead of its release, describing the base as about two-thirds of humanity and a market worth several trillion dollars.[^s2]

## How it works

Prahalad and Hart grouped the commercial infrastructure needed at the base into four elements: creating buying power, shaping aspirations, improving access, and tailoring local solutions.[^s1] In practice this has meant several design choices:

- **Affordability before margin.** Firms aim for low unit prices and high volumes. Hindustan Lever launched its Wheel detergent in 1995 after a local rival, Nirma, grew quickly among poor buyers; the authors report the subsidiary's revenue grew about 20% a year and profits about 25% a year from 1995 to 2000.[^s1]
- **Credit and financing.** Poor households often pay heavily for credit, and the authors cite Mumbai moneylenders charging up to 20% per day. Grameen Bank's microloans, with 2.3 million customers and a 95% repayment rate as of 1996, showed that lending to the poor could work.[^s1]
- **New distribution and product formats.** Arvind Mills sold "Ruf & Tuf" jeans as a kit of about $6 assembled by thousands of local tailors, while branded jeans sold for $40 to $60.[^s1]

Later work tried to measure the opportunity. The 2007 report *The Next 4 Billion*, from the World Resources Institute and the International Finance Corporation, drew on income data for 110 countries and household spending surveys from 36.[^s4] It estimated that 4 billion low-income people had about $5 trillion in purchasing power. Food made up the largest share at $2,895 billion, followed by energy at $433 billion and housing at $332 billion. Asia, including the Middle East, accounted for 2.86 billion of those people and $3.47 trillion of the income. The report also described a "BOP penalty": the poor often pay more for basic goods, get lower quality, or have no access at all.[^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

The most cited critique is Aneel Karnani's, first a 2006 Michigan working paper and then a 2007 California Management Review article. Karnani argued that the BoP case rests on several fallacies and that, for most multinationals, the market is too small in money terms to be very profitable.[^s5][^s6] In his view, the way to reduce poverty is to raise the real incomes of the poor. Firms help most by treating poor people as **producers**, buying from them and employing them, rather than mainly as consumers.[^s5][^s6] He called the BoP proposition a harmless illusion at best and potentially a dangerous delusion.[^s6] The market-size figures do not settle the dispute, because the 2007 estimate of $5 trillion describes the total purchasing power of the poor, not the profit any one firm could earn from it.[^s3][^s5]

## In practice

CEMEX's Patrimonio Hoy programme in Mexico organized low-income customers into small groups of three and financed home-building materials for them. Knowledge at Wharton reported in 2004 that, after three years, it served about 36,000 customers and had extended more than $10 million in credit, with defaults below half of one percent.[^s2] Hindustan Lever's iodized salt and Lifebuoy hygiene campaigns in India were used as further examples of large firms adapting products and messages to poor consumers.[^s2]

## Connections

The base of the pyramid is a target-market choice within a firm's **business model**. It rests on **segmentation, targeting and positioning**, applied to segments that firms had written off.[^s1] Its emphasis on low-priced, good-enough offerings resembles **pricing strategy** built on volume, and links to **disruptive innovation** from below.[^s1][^s2] Its claim to combine profit and poverty reduction places it near **corporate social responsibility** and **creating shared value**, and Grameen's example ties it to **microfinance**.[^s1][^s5] The idea was created by **C.K. Prahalad** and **Stuart L. Hart** in their 2002 article, and **Hindustan Unilever** (then Hindustan Lever) and **CEMEX** are its standard examples.[^s1][^s2]
