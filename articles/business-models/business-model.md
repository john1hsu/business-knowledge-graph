# Business Model

A **business model** describes how a firm creates value for customers, delivers it, and captures part of it as profit. David Teece called it the design or architecture of these value creation, delivery and capture mechanisms, and stressed that it embodies management's hypothesis about what customers want and how the firm can be paid for meeting that need.[^s1] It is a conceptual rather than a financial model: a spreadsheet may express it, but the model itself is the logic of the business.[^s1] Specific patterns such as razor-and-blades, freemium, subscription and franchising are all instances of the general concept.[^s1]

## Origin and rise of the term

Ways of making money are as old as barter, but Teece notes that the term itself only entered wide use in the decade or so before 2010, driven by the knowledge economy, the internet and e-commerce, outsourcing, and the restructuring of financial services.[^s1] The dot-com boom and bust of 1998–2001 exposed many ventures with no viable way to capture value, which put the concept at the centre of managerial debate.[^s1] Mainstream economic theory had largely ignored it, Teece argues, because models of perfect markets assume that value capture happens automatically.[^s1]

A review by Christoph Zott, Raphael Amit and Lorenzo Massa (IESE working paper, 2010; later published in the Journal of Management) found 1,253 journal articles from 1975 to 2009 that mentioned business models and analysed 103 core works.[^s4] Of the publications reviewed, 37% did not define the concept at all, 44% defined it explicitly, and 19% borrowed someone else's definition.[^s4] The authors traced three largely separate research streams: e-business typologies, strategy, and innovation and technology management.[^s4]

## How it works

Most frameworks break a business model into a small set of interlocking elements. Teece groups them as the value proposition, the activities and value-chain position that create and deliver it, and the revenue, cost and risk structure that captures it.[^s1] Mark Johnson, Clayton Christensen and Henning Kagermann (2008) propose four elements: a customer value proposition built around a job the customer needs done; a profit formula covering revenue model, cost structure, margin model and resource velocity; key resources; and key processes.[^s5] They emphasise that the elements are interdependent, so a major change in one forces changes in the others.[^s5]

Ramon Casadesus-Masanell and Joan Enric Ricart offer a more formal view: a business model consists of managerial **choices** (policies, assets and governance structures) and their **consequences**, which may be flexible (sales volume responding to price) or rigid (a reputation that changes slowly).[^s2] These choices and consequences can form self-reinforcing loops; their Ryanair example links secondary airports, a single aircraft type and no-frills service to low costs, low fares and high volume.[^s2]

## Business model, strategy and tactics

The concept is often confused with strategy. Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart define strategy as a contingent plan for which business model to use, the business model as the reflection of the firm's realised strategy, and tactics as the residual choices the business model leaves open.[^s2][^s3] Every organization has a business model, but not every organization has a strategy.[^s2] Teece similarly holds that a business model is more generic than a strategy and must be filtered through strategic analysis, because many model features are easy to copy.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Business model innovation is linked to growth: Johnson and colleagues report that 11 of the 27 companies born in the preceding quarter-century that reached the Fortune 500 in the decade before 2008 did so through business model innovation.[^s5] Yet the evidence that models alone sustain advantage is weak. Successful models are often imitated and become shared across an industry, and they are poor candidates for patent protection; advantage lasts only when the model rests on hard-to-replicate assets, causal ambiguity, or incumbents' reluctance to cannibalize their existing business.[^s1] Scholars also criticise the construct as fragmented and inconsistently defined, which hampers cumulative research.[^s4]

## In practice

Hilti, the power-tool maker, moved from selling tools to leasing managed tool fleets for a monthly fee covering maintenance, repair and replacement. The shift required a new profit formula, new resources and processes, and retrained sales staff working much longer sales cycles.[^s5] Teece contrasts such successes with Xerox and EMI, whose technological successes failed commercially because they were not paired with a workable business model.[^s1]

## Connections

The business model is a core concept within **strategic management**, and it **contrasts with competitive strategy**, which governs how a firm deploys a model against rivals.[^s1][^s2] **David J. Teece** shaped the strategy literature on the subject.[^s1] Its value-proposition element draws directly on **jobs to be done**, and changing it is the subject of **business model innovation**, a theme studied within **innovation management**.[^s4][^s5] **Southwest Airlines** exemplifies a tightly integrated no-frills model, while **razor-and-blades** and **freemium** are named patterns of the general concept.[^s1]
