# Advertising-Supported Business Model

An **advertising-supported business model** gives users content or a service free of charge, or below cost, and makes money by selling advertisers access to the resulting audience.[^s2] The firm effectively sells a joint product to two groups of buyers: content to readers, viewers or users, and audience attention to advertisers.[^s2] Free newspapers, commercial broadcast television, web search and social networks are the classic cases; many newspapers and magazines mix the model with a cover or subscription price.[^s2]

## Origin and development

Commercial radio established the model's modern form. In 1922 the New York station WEAF began selling airtime under a scheme called "toll broadcasting," and its first commercial, aired on 22 August 1922, was a 15-minute pitch for apartments in Jackson Heights, Queens.[^s6] Acceptance was slow because broadcasters feared offending listeners, but after NBC bought WEAF in 1926 and used it as the hub of a permanent network, national sponsored programming became the standard way to pay for American broadcasting.[^s6] Economists later formalized the logic. Simon Anderson and Stephen Coate modeled a broadcast as a public good consumed by two groups, viewers who value programs and advertisers who value access to viewers, with advertising acting as a price that broadcasters can charge because they control access to the audience.[^s1]

## How it works

The firm is a platform in a two-sided market. Advertisers want more users, while users treat ads as a nuisance, so the platform must balance the two sides rather than maximize ad volume.[^s2] In Anderson and Gabszewicz's survey, when audiences dislike ads, competition between media outlets is partly competition over ad clutter, so more rivals can mean fewer ads per outlet; when readers actually seek ads, as with classifieds, the relationship reverses.[^s2] Content choices are shaped by the goal of assembling audiences that advertisers will pay for, which is why advertisers' preference for certain demographics feeds back into programming.[^s2]

Digital platforms price attention more precisely than broadcasters could. Alphabet's 2025 10-K distinguishes performance advertising, designed to prompt clicks and direct engagement, from brand advertising aimed at awareness, and it tracks cost-per-click and cost-per-impression as its core pricing metrics.[^s4] In fiscal 2025 Google advertising (Search, YouTube ads and the Google Network) generated about $294.7 billion of Alphabet's $402.8 billion in total revenue, roughly 73%.[^s4] Meta Platforms reported for 2025 that it earns substantially all of its revenue from advertising, sold mostly through a self-service ad platform.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Theory does not support the claim that ad-funded markets deliver the efficient amount of advertising. Anderson and Coate showed that equilibrium ad levels can be too high or too low, and that the market can supply too few or too many programs; allowing subscription prices lowers ad levels but can exclude viewers and does not always raise welfare.[^s1] They also caution that ads may be worth less to society than their price suggests if they mainly persuade or steal business from rivals.[^s1] Long-standing critiques of ad-funded broadcasting, such as Steiner's duplication principle and Beebe's lowest-common-denominator result, hold that competitors chase the same mass audience and leave minority tastes unserved.[^s2] Ad clutter is a recurring tension: by 2003 the ABC, NBC and Fox networks exceeded 15 minutes of non-program content per hour, while the EU then capped advertising at nine minutes per hour.[^s2]

The model also carries concentrated business risk. Alphabet's 10-K warns that advertisers can end contracts at any time, that ad spending falls in downturns, and that ad-blocking, privacy changes and AI-driven shifts in how ads are delivered could hurt revenue.[^s4] Meta's 10-K says Apple's iOS changes have limited its ability to target and measure ads since 2021, and that the European Commission ruled in April 2025 that its "subscription for no ads" option did not comply with the Digital Markets Act, a decision Meta has appealed.[^s5]

## Strategic choice: ads, subscription or both

Firms often choose between, or mix, ad-funded and paid offers. Ramon Casadesus-Masanell and Feng Zhu modeled an incumbent facing a free, ad-sponsored entrant and found that the best reply often involves changing business model, not just price; pure subscription tends to win when advertising rates are low, and only an ad-sponsored response can drive the entrant out.[^s3] In their example, ignoring business-model changes could cost the incumbent up to about 60% of its profit.[^s3] They cite the Boston Globe's stake in the free daily Metro Boston, which helped push out the free rival BostonNow.[^s3]

## Connections

The model is a type of **business model** and a canonical **two-sided market**, since the platform subsidizes users to sell their attention to advertisers.[^s1][^s2] It is an **alternative to the subscription business model**, and the two are frequently combined in tiered or mixed offers that resemble **freemium**.[^s3] **Google** and **Meta Platforms** exemplify its digital form, and **aggregation theory** and **network effects** help explain why dominant ad-funded platforms are hard to dislodge.[^s4][^s5] It applies most directly to the **media industry**, and Anderson and Coate's **2005 broadcasting paper** supplies its standard economic analysis.[^s1] Like the **open-source business model**, it gives away the core product and earns money elsewhere.[^s2]
