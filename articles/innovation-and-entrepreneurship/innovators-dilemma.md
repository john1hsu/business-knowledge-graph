# The Innovator's Dilemma

*The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail* is a book by Harvard Business School professor Clayton M. Christensen, published by Harvard Business School Press in Boston in 1997.[^s1] Its title has since become shorthand for a broader managerial trap: a leading company can do everything conventional management recommends and still lose its position, because the practices that made it successful steer it away from emerging technologies and markets.[^s1] HBS describes the book as a classic bestseller and one of the most influential business books ever written, and the Christensen Institute calls it his best-selling first book.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin and argument

The book grew out of Christensen's doctoral research on the disk-drive industry, which he first published as business history, including a 1993 article in *Business History Review*.[^s2] Harvard historian Tom Nicholas traces how that work linked incumbent failure to Alfred Chandler's account of large, vertically integrated firms. Christensen's disk-drive evidence suggested those integrated incumbents were losing ground, and that many of the entrants displacing them were spinoffs founded by the incumbents' own engineers.[^s2] The industry's history, running from IBM's 1956 RAMAC disk file through the Winchester drive of 1973 and successive smaller formats, gave the book its core cases.[^s2]

The "dilemma" is that the behaviours which keep a firm healthy in its current market, such as listening to major customers and investing where margins are high, are the same behaviours that make it neglect new segments where an initially inferior product is improving.[^s2] The book's prescription was that managers must know how and when to abandon established practice.[^s1] In particular, Christensen argued that an incumbent's most reliable defence was to place the new business in a separate unit or spinoff, free from the parent's resource-allocation rules.[^s2] The theoretical framework the book introduced is covered in the node on disruptive innovation; this node concerns the book itself, its evidence and its afterlife.[^s5]

## Influence

The book reached practitioners quickly. Intel's Andy Grove recommended it to attendees of the company's 1998 sales and marketing conference. Intel launched its cheaper, less sophisticated Celeron processor that year, an explicit attempt to avoid being disrupted from below.[^s2] Nicholas also reports that Steve Jobs took the ideas seriously and that Amazon applied them, with Jeff Bezos telling an executive in 2004 that his job was to kill Amazon's own business, a push that led to the Kindle.[^s2] HBS notes the book has been cited by figures including Jobs and Malcolm Gladwell.[^s1] Inside Harvard, Christensen's MBA elective built on the ideas enrolled 7,648 students between 2001 and 2018.[^s2] He extended the argument in *The Innovator's Solution* (2003, with Michael Raynor), which moved the emphasis from technology toward business models.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Nicholas argues that grounding the theory in history was its strength, but that the book generalised further than the historical record supported.[^s2] He reviews business historians who found mixed evidence in other industries, often pointing to managerial mindset rather than customer-driven inertia. He also notes Jill Lepore's 2014 critique, which flagged factual problems such as describing Seagate as disrupted when it remained a leading manufacturer in 1997.[^s2] Other scholars showed that incumbents' complementary assets or ambidextrous structures can protect them, and that disruption can come from above, as when the iPhone displaced the iPod.[^s2]

The most rigorous test came from economics. Mitsuru Igami re-estimated the disk-drive story with a dynamic oligopoly model of manufacturers from 1981 to 1998, published in the *Journal of Political Economy* in 2017.[^s3] He found that incumbents innovated reluctantly even though they had strong pre-emptive motives and a sizeable cost advantage over entrants. Cannibalisation of their existing products explained at least 57 percent of the innovation gap between incumbents and entrants.[^s3] In Igami's reading, incumbents can lag while acting rationally, because replacing their own profit stream adds little, whereas a start-up begins from zero; Christensen had stressed organisational blind spots instead.[^s4] The practical lesson he drew is that survivors must be willing to cannibalise old products, even against shareholders who prefer existing profits.[^s4]

## Connections

The book is the work in which Clayton Christensen introduced **disruptive innovation**, and it sits within **innovation management**.[^s1][^s5] Its sequel, **The Innovator's Solution**, reframed the remedy around business models.[^s5] **Organizational ambidexterity**, O'Reilly and Tushman's evidence that incumbents can explore and exploit at once, is the leading organisational answer to the dilemma.[^s2] Igami's work ties the dilemma to **cannibalization** and to the **industrial organization** literature on **creative destruction**, which explains incumbent lag through incentives rather than managerial error.[^s3][^s4] **Intel**'s Celeron launch is the classic early corporate response.[^s2]
