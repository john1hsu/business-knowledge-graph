# Customer Development

**Customer development** is a process for building a startup's market in step with its product. Steve Blank, a Silicon Valley entrepreneur turned educator, describes it as a way to get founders out of the office so they can put every part of their business model, from features and pricing to channels, in front of prospective users, buyers and partners.[^s1] Its premise is that a startup is not a scaled-down large company: an established firm executes a business model it already knows, while a startup is still searching for one, and Blank argues the two jobs require different methods.[^s1]

## Origin

Blank spent roughly two decades in startups before questioning the product development model he had followed throughout that career.[^s3] He began outlining customer development in a Berkeley course in 2003 and published the method in detail the same year in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*.[^s1] His diagnosis was that the conventional sequence of concept, development, alpha/beta test and launch ignores the biggest risk a new venture faces. Sales and marketing are bolted on after the product is built, plans revolve around the first customer ship date, and business model assumptions go untested until the cash is already spent.[^s2][^s3] He also faulted the old model for delegating customer contact to sales staff, so founders hear feedback late and secondhand, and for encouraging fully featured products whose code is often thrown away.[^s3] In his lectures he puts the core claim bluntly: more startups fail for lack of customers than because product development failed.[^s2]

## The four steps

Blank's model runs in parallel with product development and replaces ship-date milestones with customer milestones.[^s2]

1. **Customer discovery.** Founders convert their vision into hypotheses about the customer's problem and the product concept, then test whether customers have the problem, would pay to solve it, and see the proposed product as a solution.[^s2] A minimum viable product is often used to put the solution in front of customers early.[^s1]
2. **Customer validation.** The startup tries to prove that interest is real, through early orders or product usage, and to find a repeatable sales roadmap and a financial model that works.[^s1][^s2] If the evidence is weak, the team pivots by changing one or more hypotheses and loops back to discovery.[^s1]
3. **Customer creation.** With a validated model, the firm moves from a few customers to many through positioning, launch and demand creation; Blank's slides equate this with crossing the chasm.[^s2]
4. **Company building.** The organization, management and mission are rebuilt to suit a company that now executes rather than searches.[^s2]

Blank stresses that spending should match **market type**. A product entering an existing market, a resegmented niche or low-end market, or an entirely new market faces very different growth curves, so the same launch budget can be sensible in one case and ruinous in another.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

The method's largest institutional test is the U.S. National Science Foundation's Innovation Corps (I-Corps), which NSF began in 2011 to apply these ideas to commercializing academic research.[^s1] As of 2026, NSF's national teams program runs about seven weeks and requires each team to complete at least 100 interviews with potential customers and other stakeholders.[^s4] Education researchers writing in ASEE proceedings argue that customer discovery is less novel than it appears: its interviewing and iteration draw on established qualitative research, action research and design-based research methods.[^s6]

Systematic evidence on hypothesis testing by founders is still limited but encouraging. In a randomized trial with 116 Italian startups, Camuffo, Cordova and Gambardella found that founders trained to frame theories and rigorously test hypotheses performed better and pivoted more often than a control group that received the same general training on gathering market feedback.[^s5] The authors read the result as a gain in decision precision: disciplined testing made founders less likely to chase ideas that only looked promising and more likely to keep ideas that first looked weak, a caution that customer feedback gathered without clear hypotheses can mislead.[^s5]

## In practice

A typical I-Corps team trains in a cohort of roughly 20 to 30 teams, committing at least 15 hours a week, and ends with a lessons-learned presentation summarizing what its interviews revealed about customers and the business model.[^s4] Blank reported in 2013 that Stanford, Harvard, Berkeley and Columbia were leading the lean startup curriculum built on these ideas, and that his course for educators trained more than 250 instructors a year.[^s1]

## Connections

Customer development sits within **innovation management** and is a specialized form of **market research**, carried out by the founders themselves rather than delegated to sales or marketing staff.[^s3][^s6] It was created by **Steve Blank** and introduced in **The Four Steps to the Epiphany**.[^s1] It directly **influenced the lean startup**, which combined Blank's customer process with agile engineering.[^s1] Its validation step is the search for **product-market fit**, and its customer-creation step borrows the chasm idea from the **technology adoption life cycle**.[^s2] NSF's **Innovation Corps** program is its most prominent large-scale application.[^s1][^s4]
