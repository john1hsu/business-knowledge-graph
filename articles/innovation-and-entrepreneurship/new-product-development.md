# New Product Development

New product development (NPD) is the business process of transforming a market opportunity into a product available for sale. V. Krishnan and Karl Ulrich's widely cited 2001 review in *Management Science* frames it this way and stresses that the subject sits across three academic traditions: marketing, operations management and engineering design.[^s1] Rather than treating development as a black box whose success depends on context such as market growth or top-management support, they model it as a business process made up of hundreds of decisions, each of which can be informed by better knowledge and tools.[^s1]

## What counts as "new"

"New" covers more than inventions. The OpenStax *Principles of Marketing* text distinguishes five categories: new-to-the-world products that create markets, products new to the firm but not to the market, additions to existing lines, improvements or revisions of current products, and existing products repositioned for a new use.[^s3] It also classifies newness by how much a product changes customer habits, from continuous innovations that leave behavior intact, through dynamically continuous ones, to discontinuous innovations such as the shift to smartphones.[^s3] The category matters because risk rises with novelty: the same text lists lack of differentiation, financial and technical feasibility, and poor timing as the main hazards of launching something new.[^s3]

## How it works

Most NPD processes move a portfolio of ideas through screening, concept definition, design and engineering, testing, and commercial launch, with fewer projects surviving each step. Krishnan and Ulrich's decision view treats each of these steps as a set of choices, made deliberately or by default, about what to build and how, and their review concentrates on development of physical goods within a single firm while noting that much of the work generalizes.[^s1] Formal phase-and-review systems such as the Stage-Gate process put management checkpoints between these steps, a way of formalizing the kind of front-end planning that the success-factor research below rewards.

A contrasting tradition emphasizes speed and overlap. In a January 1986 *Harvard Business Review* article, Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka argued that the sequential, relay-race approach was too slow for competitive markets. They described a holistic "rugby" approach used by Japanese and US firms, built on six features: built-in instability, self-organizing project teams, overlapping development phases, multilearning, subtle control and organizational transfer of learning.[^s2] The authors presented the approach as fast and flexible, and as a change agent that could carry creative, market-driven ideas into rigid organizations.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Research on success factors points consistently to differentiation and front-end work. Summarizing that literature, OpenStax reports that products offering unique user benefits succeed about five times as often as weakly differentiated ones, and that projects with well-defined predevelopment activity succeeded 75% of the time versus roughly 31% for those without.[^s4] Fit with the firm's existing technology and marketing resources also raises the odds, with marketing synergy associated with a 2.3-fold higher success rate.[^s4]

Popular claims about failure are less reliable. OpenStax notes that a figure of 95% failure is often attributed to Clayton Christensen, while a Product Development and Management Association (PDMA) study found failure rates from 35% in health care to 49% in consumer goods.[^s4] PDMA's 2012 Comparative Performance Assessment Study, covering 453 business units in 24 countries, found that 61% of launched products succeeded as of 2012.[^s5] The same study found that the best-performing firms needed about 4.5 ideas per successful product against 11.4 for the rest, and that use of formal cross-functional processes fell from 69% of firms in 2004 to 49.1% in 2012, even as top performers relied on them more.[^s5] A broader critique, implicit in the Takeuchi-Nonaka argument, is that heavily sequential processes trade speed and flexibility for control.[^s2]

## In practice

Failure cases show how the factors play out. OpenStax cites Amazon's Fire Phone, which reached the market in 2014 after four years of development, when rival smartphone platforms were several generations ahead, and ended production about a year later; it also cites Google Glass, announced in 2013 and discontinued in 2015, as a product without a clear customer need.[^s4] By contrast, PDMA's top performers paired a first-to-market strategy (47.7% of them versus 24.8% of other firms) with heavy use of voice-of-the-customer research and regular redesign of their own NPD process.[^s5]

## Connections

NPD is a core activity within innovation management, and the Stage-Gate process is one specific, widely adopted way of organizing it.[^s1] It depends on market research, because concept and specification decisions rest on understanding customer needs.[^s4][^s5] Its intellectual foundations are surveyed in Krishnan and Ulrich's 2001 review and reshaped by Takeuchi and Nonaka's 1986 article, whose overlapping, team-based model links NPD to agile software development.[^s1][^s2] Related nodes include design thinking, a human-centered approach to the front end of development, and cannibalization, the risk that a firm's new product takes sales from its existing ones, as with line additions and revisions.[^s3]
