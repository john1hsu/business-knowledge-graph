# Organizational Ambidexterity

Organizational ambidexterity is a firm's ability to do two different things at once: exploit, by competing in mature technologies and markets where efficiency, control and incremental improvement win, and explore, by competing in new technologies and markets where flexibility, autonomy and experimentation are required.[^s3] Raisch and Birkinshaw describe it as running today's business efficiently while also adapting to changes in the environment.[^s6] The concept is the most influential organizational answer to the problem of incumbents being displaced by new technologies, because it treats the failure as a matter of design and leadership rather than of missing technology.[^s7]

## Origin

The intellectual root is James G. March's 1991 *Organization Science* paper, which framed adaptation as a trade-off in how organizations allocate resources between exploring new possibilities and exploiting old certainties.[^s1] March argued that the costs and benefits of the two activities are distributed unevenly over time and across the organization, so adaptive processes tend to refine exploitation faster than exploration; that bias pays off in the short run but becomes self-destructive in the long run.[^s1]

Charles A. O'Reilly III of Stanford and Michael L. Tushman of Harvard Business School turned that trade-off into a managerial prescription. Their April 2004 *Harvard Business Review* article, "The Ambidextrous Organization", described companies that house new, exploratory units separately from traditional, exploitative ones, give each its own processes, structures and cultures, and keep tight links across them at the senior-executive level.[^s2] In a 2013 review they noted that research on the topic had grown rapidly over the preceding 15 years.[^s3]

## How it works

The literature distinguishes two main routes.

- **Structural ambidexterity.** Exploration and exploitation are assigned to different units. The exploratory unit is protected from the efficiency metrics and routines of the core business, while a shared senior team allocates resources between them and integrates where the units can share assets.[^s2] O'Reilly and Tushman stress that the two kinds of unit need different metrics, incentives and cultures, held together by a compelling strategic intent and a common identity.[^s7]
- **Contextual ambidexterity.** Cristina Gibson and Julian Birkinshaw defined ambidexterity as the simultaneous capacity for *alignment* (coherence of activities toward shared goals) and *adaptability* (quick reconfiguration as the task environment changes).[^s4] In their model, leaders do not mandate the balance; they build a context of discipline, stretch, support and trust in which individuals decide for themselves how to divide their time between alignment-oriented and adaptation-oriented work.[^s4] They presented this as a complement to structural separation, which can isolate R&D and new-business groups from the core.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

Gibson and Birkinshaw surveyed 41 business units in 10 multinational firms, with more than 4,000 respondents across hierarchical levels. Cluster analysis grouped units as aligned, adaptive, moderately ambidextrous and highly ambidextrous; the highly ambidextrous group had the highest performance score (4.60 on a 7-point scale) and the purely aligned group the lowest (3.21).[^s4] In the MIT Sloan Management Review version, they described the work as a three-year study and illustrated it with Nokia, Ericsson, Oracle and Renault.[^s5] O'Reilly and Tushman's later research on 27 long-lived companies, with an average age of 130 years, found that nearly all had moved into different businesses over decades of disruption.[^s7]

The concept has limits. Gibson and Birkinshaw argued that structural separation causes duplication, weak integration and low morale in units run purely for efficiency; they also acknowledged that some of their own results weakened under sensitivity checks.[^s4] Raisch and Birkinshaw's 2008 review found the research fragmented across several literatures and called for an integrated model of antecedents, moderators and outcomes, along with work on gaps between those streams.[^s6] O'Reilly himself observed that ambidexterity can depend on a single leader and fade when that leader departs, and that how much exploration a firm needs depends on how exposed it is to disruption, with Exxon facing slower change than General Motors.[^s7]

## In practice

The best-known contrast is film photography. Fujifilm began repositioning in 2000, when global film sales peaked, and used its film-related capabilities to enter cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, regenerative medicine and LCD films; by 2016 it was described as a $23 billion company. Kodak, which had comparable technologies but stayed focused on film, went bankrupt in 2012.[^s7] O'Reilly also cites Smith Corona, which had an early word processor that its leaders chose not to fund, as evidence that the failure is usually one of leadership rather than technology.[^s7] In practice, managers apply the idea by funding exploratory ventures with separate metrics, reviewing them at the top team rather than inside the core business, and deciding explicitly which ventures to scale and which to shut down.[^s2][^s7]

## Connections

Ambidexterity is a branch of **innovation management** and of **organizational learning**, since it rests on March's exploration-exploitation trade-off.[^s1] It is the organizational response O'Reilly and Tushman, its main **creators**, offer to the **innovator's dilemma** and **disruptive innovation**, which explains the `mitigates` edge.[^s7] It overlaps with **dynamic capabilities**, the capacity to reconfigure assets as markets change, a theme close to what Gibson and Birkinshaw call adaptability, the quick reconfiguration of activities as demands change.[^s4] The **IBM turnaround** under Gerstner and Palmisano is a commonly cited example, while the **Kodak decline** is the standard counterexample.[^s7]
