# Open Innovation

**Open innovation** is the idea that a firm should use purposive inflows and outflows of knowledge to speed up its own innovation and to widen the markets for what it invents.[^s1] Instead of assuming that every good idea must be discovered, developed and commercialized in-house, the firm treats outside ideas as legitimate inputs and treats its own unused technologies as assets that others might take to market.[^s1] Henry Chesbrough, now executive director of the Garwood Center for Corporate Innovation at UC Berkeley's Haas School, set out the concept in 2003 in the book *Open Innovation: The New Imperative for Creating and Profiting from Technology* and in an *MIT Sloan Management Review* article, "The Era of Open Innovation".[^s1][^s2]

## From closed to open innovation

Chesbrough framed open innovation against a **closed** model that dominated twentieth-century industrial research. Under it, large companies such as DuPont, IBM and AT&T treated their central laboratories as a strategic asset and a barrier to entry: rivals had to build comparable labs to compete.[^s2] The closed logic held that successful innovation requires control, so a firm generated its own ideas, ran its own development, manufacturing and distribution, and kept its intellectual property locked up.[^s1]

Chesbrough argued that this model was eroding. Upstart entrants were winning while doing little basic research of their own; Cisco kept pace with Lucent, whose Bell Labs was among the world's strongest research organizations, by partnering with and investing in startups rather than inventing everything internally.[^s2] Useful knowledge had become widely distributed as skilled workers moved between employers and venture capital funded spin-offs, so no single company could monopolize it.[^s1] Closed firms also wasted "false negatives": projects that looked unpromising inside the company but proved valuable elsewhere, such as technologies from Xerox PARC that others went on to commercialize.[^s1]

## How it works

A decade after the original book, Chesbrough and Marcel Bogers refined the definition: open innovation is a distributed innovation process built on purposively managed knowledge flows across organizational boundaries, using both pecuniary and non-pecuniary mechanisms consistent with the firm's business model.[^s3] They distinguish three modes:[^s3]

- **Outside-in (inbound):** opening the innovation process to external knowledge through scouting, in-licensing, university research programs, startup funding and crowdsourcing.[^s3]
- **Inside-out (outbound):** letting unused internal ideas leave the firm through out-licensing, spin-outs, corporate venture capital, IP donation and joint ventures.[^s3]
- **Coupled:** combining inflows and outflows with partners in alliances, consortia, ecosystems and platforms.[^s3]

The business model is central: Chesbrough's argument is that a firm should acquire outside inventions when they advance its model and license or spin out its own when they do not fit.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The concept spread quickly. By Chesbrough and Bogers's count, the 2003 book drew over 6,000 Google Scholar citations within roughly a decade, and a Web of Science search found 941 articles that cited it or used the term.[^s3] The best-known empirical test is Laursen and Salter's 2006 study of 2,707 UK manufacturers from the 2001 UK Innovation Survey. Firms that searched more broadly (more external knowledge sources) and more deeply (heavier reliance on a few sources) innovated more, but only up to a point: both relationships formed an inverted U, implying that firms can over-search.[^s4] The same study found depth mattered more for radical innovation and breadth for incremental innovation, and that internal R&D intensity acted as a substitute for openness rather than a complement.[^s4]

Critics question the novelty. Paul Trott and Dap Hartmann of Delft University of Technology argued in 2009 that open innovation rests on a false dichotomy, presenting itself as the only alternative to "closed innovation". Examining its six principles one by one, they accepted that the limits of closed innovation are real but contended that the concept repackages long-standing practice, hence their title's "old wine in new bottles".[^s5] Others note intellectual antecedents such as Eric von Hippel's research at MIT on user-led innovation.[^s6]

## In practice

Procter & Gamble's Connect + Develop program, launched in 2001, is the standard example. P&G committed to sourcing ideas from outside partners; one result was Pringles Prints, which used a pastry ink-jet printing technique developed by an Italian professor and reached market at a fraction of the cost and time of in-house development.[^s6] The textbook account reports that P&G's innovation success rate doubled while its innovation costs fell.[^s6] Other variants include innovation contests and intermediaries such as InnoCentive, which was spun out of Eli Lilly in 2005, and Shell's GameChanger program, which funds promising ideas from outside the company.[^s6]

## Connections

Open innovation is a strategy within **innovation management** (`kt:innovation-management`), the broader discipline of sourcing, selecting and commercializing ideas.[^s1] It **contrasts with closed innovation**, the vertically integrated research model it was defined against.[^s2] It was **created by Henry Chesbrough** and **introduced in** his 2003 book.[^s1] It draws on **user innovation**, von Hippel's earlier work on outsiders as innovators.[^s6] Because it ties innovation choices to the **business model** and requires firms to sense and absorb outside knowledge, it sits next to **dynamic capabilities** and **organizational learning**; and **venture capital** both feeds the spin-offs that disperse knowledge and serves as an outbound channel through corporate venturing.[^s1][^s3] **Procter & Gamble** exemplifies the inbound mode at scale.[^s6]
