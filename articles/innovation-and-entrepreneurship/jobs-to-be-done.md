# Jobs to Be Done

**Jobs to be done** (JTBD) is a theory of why customers buy what they buy. Its central claim is that people do not purchase products for their attributes; they "hire" a product to get a job done, meaning to resolve a problem or make progress in a specific circumstance, and they "fire" products that do the job poorly.[^s1] Christensen and his co-authors argue that the job, not the customer's profile or the product category, should be the basic unit of market segmentation and the starting point for innovation.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

The idea draws on Theodore Levitt's remark that buyers of a quarter-inch drill really want a quarter-inch hole, an observation Christensen and Intuit's Scott Cook used in a *Harvard Business Review* article, excerpted by HBS in 2006, to argue that marketers over-rely on demographic segments.[^s3] Christensen developed the theory in teaching at Harvard Business School, crediting collaborators including Bob Moesta of the ReWired Group, and set it out most fully in the 2016 *Harvard Business Review* article "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'" with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David Duncan.[^s1][^s2] A parallel practitioner line, Anthony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation, also treats jobs as the source of customer information but measures desired outcomes more formally.[^s5]

## How it works

Every job has functional, emotional and social dimensions, and their weight varies: a work truck is hired mainly for function, while an aspirational car is hired largely for emotional and social reasons.[^s1] The HBS module note describes a three-level architecture: understand the job and the progress the customer seeks, define the purchase and use experiences needed to do it well, and then integrate the firm's activities to deliver those experiences.[^s1] A switch happens only when the forces pushing a customer toward a new solution outweigh the forces holding them back, such as anxiety about change or attachment to current habits.[^s1]

Because competition is defined by the job, the relevant market includes every alternative that does the job, which often makes job-defined markets much larger than category-defined ones.[^s3] Averaging the preferences of a demographic segment, by contrast, can yield a product that fits no one's actual circumstances.[^s3]

## Example: the milkshake

The theory's best-known illustration involves a fast-food chain that had tried to improve milkshake sales by surveying target customers about an ideal shake, with no result.[^s4] A researcher working with Christensen instead observed a restaurant all day and found that about 40% of shakes were bought in the morning, mostly by lone commuters taking them to go.[^s4] Interviews showed the job was to make a long, dull drive more interesting and stay full until late morning; the shake competed with bagels, bananas and doughnuts rather than other shakes.[^s3][^s4] Later in the day, parents hired the same product for a different job, placating children, which called for a different design.[^s1] The 2016 article adds a housing case: a condominium developer selling to downsizing retirees grew sales only after recognizing buyers' anxiety about leaving a family home and adding moving and storage help.[^s2]

## Evidence and critiques

Most published support for JTBD is case-based, drawn from teaching notes and practitioner articles by its proponents.[^s1][^s4] Academic testing is thinner. One peer-reviewed study of 109 business customers of a multinational chemical firm found that an index of how well important jobs were solved was positively associated with customers' performance, but it explained only 22.8% of the variance and covered a single company and industry.[^s5] The same authors note that links between JTBD and service-dominant logic still need empirical work.[^s5]

Critics from market research argue that framing behaviour as discrete jobs can flatten the psychological, social and cultural forces that shape choice, and that JTBD is often paired with light-touch interviews that weaken the resulting insight.[^s6] They suggest treating it as one thinking tool alongside rigorous research rather than as a complete account of consumer behaviour.[^s6] Proponents also concede the method is hard to adopt because firms' data, retail shelves and org charts are organized by product category and demographics.[^s4]

## Connections

JTBD sits under **innovation management** and was created by **Clayton Christensen**, also the originator of **disruptive innovation**; the module note setting out JTBD was written for his HBS course Building and Sustaining a Successful Enterprise.[^s1] It builds on **Theodore Levitt**'s outcome-over-product insight.[^s3] It deliberately contrasts with demographic **segmentation, targeting and positioning**, proposing the job rather than the customer as the segment.[^s1][^s3] **Outcome-Driven Innovation** is an alternative, more quantitative school built on the same job concept.[^s5] Understanding the job also informs **customer lifetime value**, since products that do a job well are retained rather than fired.[^s2]
