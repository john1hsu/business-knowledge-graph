# Product-Market Fit

**Product-market fit** (PMF, often written product/market fit) is the condition of serving a good market with a product able to satisfy it.[^s1] The idea is less a feature checklist than a relationship: in a strong market, demand is so intense that customers effectively pull the product out of the company, whereas in a weak one no amount of product polish or team quality creates momentum.[^s1][^s4] Entrepreneurship teaching and venture investing use the term to mark the boundary between a startup's search phase, when it is still testing whether anyone truly needs what it offers, and its growth phase, when it should commit resources to scaling.[^s1][^s2]

## Origin

Investor and entrepreneur Marc Andreessen set out the concept in a June 2007 essay, "The Only Thing That Matters," part of his *Pmarca Guide to Startups*; Stanford's EE204 course later reposted it for students.[^s1] Andreessen asked which of a startup's three core elements, team, product or market, matters most, and answered that the market does.[^s1] He grounded the claim in what he called Rachleff's Law, after investor Andy Rachleff: a lack of market is the leading cause of startup death, and a great market paired with a great team produces exceptional companies.[^s1] He then split a startup's life into "before" and "after" product-market fit and argued that before fit, founders should do whatever it takes to get there, including changing people, rewriting the product or moving to a different market.[^s1]

## How it works and how it is detected

Andreessen described fit as something founders can feel. Without it, customers do not get enough value, word of mouth stalls, usage grows slowly, press coverage is lukewarm, sales cycles drag and deals fail to close. With it, customers buy as fast as the firm can supply, usage outruns server capacity, cash piles up and the company hires as fast as it can.[^s1] Floodgate investor Mike Maples, speaking at Stanford's Technology Ventures Program in 2016, recast fit as a continuing "dance" between product and market rather than a single switch. His practical test is internal: when the market is pulling, teams stop arguing about what to build because customers demand specific fixes; when it is not, teams debate why customers fail to appreciate the product.[^s4] Maples added that merely adequate products rarely achieve fit; a product has to clear a threshold of customer delight.[^s4]

Because these signals are qualitative, practitioners have tried to turn them into a leading indicator. The best-known is the **Sean Ellis test**, which asks active users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product and tracks the share answering "very disappointed"; Ellis found that startups below about 40% generally struggled to grow.[^s5] Superhuman, an email startup, scored 22% on the survey in summer 2017. It narrowed its target segment to the users most attached to the product, which raised the score to 33%, and it split its roadmap between deepening what loyal users loved and fixing what held back near-fans. Three quarters later it reported 58%.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The prescription to reach fit before scaling now has large-sample support. Lee and Kim studied about 6.3 million job postings from more than 38,000 U.S. startups founded after 2010, dating each firm's scaling by its first manager and sales hires, and found that early scalers were more likely to fail, with no offsetting gain in successful exits; the effect was especially pronounced for platform companies.[^s2][^s3] Early scalers were also less likely to run A/B tests, consistent with the view that scaling early locks in ideas before experimentation can show whether they fit a market.[^s2][^s3] The study did not measure fit directly, however; it used hiring as a proxy for scaling, so it supports the timing logic rather than validating any single fit metric.[^s3]

Critics question how fit is usually pursued and measured. Felin, Gambardella, Stern and Zenger argue that customer-led validation, the main route lean methods use to search for a market, biases founders toward incremental and obvious opportunities, because customers struggle to evaluate genuinely novel products and easy experiments are not always informative ones.[^s6] Fit is also easier to recognize in hindsight than in real time: Andreessen himself noted that founders of successful startups often credit their success to other factors.[^s1] Survey thresholds such as 40% are heuristics drawn from one practitioner's experience, not validated research findings.[^s5]

## Connections

Product-market fit sits within **innovation management** as the central milestone of the **lean startup** method, whose validated-learning loop and pivots are designed to reach it, and of the **customer development** process that preceded it.[^s6] It was **introduced in** Andreessen's 2007 essay and **influenced by** Andy Rachleff's emphasis on market over team.[^s1] It is commonly **measured by** the Sean Ellis survey test.[^s5] **Jobs to be done** helps founders define the need a product must satisfy to fit, and the **technology adoption life cycle** explains why fit with early adopters may not carry over to mainstream buyers.[^s4] Its opposite failure mode is **premature scaling**: committing to growth before the market is pulling.[^s2][^s3]
