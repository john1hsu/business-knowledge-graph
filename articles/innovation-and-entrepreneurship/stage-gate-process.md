# Stage-Gate Process

The Stage-Gate process is a structured system for taking new products from idea to market launch. Work is divided into a series of stages, and before each stage sits a gate: a meeting at which senior managers review the project and decide whether to continue funding it.[^s1] The approach was developed by Robert G. Cooper of McMaster University, who first set it out in print in 1988 and 1990, and it remains one of the most widely used formal processes for managing product innovation.[^s1][^s5]

## Origin

Cooper built the model from his research into why some new products succeed and most do not. In a 2006 paper for the Project Management Institute he reported that only about one in seven product concepts becomes a commercial winner and that 44% of development projects miss their profit targets, figures he used to argue that firms need a disciplined way to cull weak projects early and put resources behind strong ones.[^s1] He presented the idea-to-launch system as one of four drivers of innovation performance, alongside an innovation strategy, portfolio management with adequate resources, and a supportive culture.[^s1] Stage-Gate International, the consultancy that promotes Cooper's model, dates the first printed use of the term "Stage-Gate" to 1988.[^s5]

## How it works

In the version described by Stage-Gate International, a discovery or ideation phase feeds five stages: concept, building the business case, development, testing and validation, and launch.[^s5] Each stage is staffed by a cross-functional team doing parallel marketing, technical and operations work, so that a single accountable team carries the project rather than handing it between functional silos.[^s5]

Gates are where resources are committed. Each gate has deliverables specified in advance, criteria covering project readiness and business value, and four possible outcomes: go, kill, hold or recycle. A "go" also fixes the budget, people, timeline and next gate date.[^s5] The gatekeepers are senior managers who own the resources the next stage needs, and Cooper recommends that they score projects against explicit criteria so that go/kill decisions are made on evidence rather than on advocacy.[^s1] The general logic mirrors the textbook new-product development sequence of idea screening, concept testing, business analysis, development, test marketing and commercialization, in which a concept that fails a review is shelved before more money is spent.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Adoption is broad. Cooper cited Product Development and Management Association (PDMA) data showing that nearly 60% of surveyed firms used some form of Stage-Gate as of the mid-2000s.[^s1] Much of the supporting evidence, however, comes from Cooper and his collaborators, and the practitioner claims of shorter cycle times and higher success rates come largely from firms' self-reports.[^s5][^s2]

The main academic critique is that the model is sequential, slow and rigid. A 2022 review in *Prometheus* by Trott, Baxter, Ellwood and van der Duin argued that Stage-Gate suits development in known markets with known solutions but copes poorly with the uncertainty of discontinuous products. It also noted that rigorous gate criteria can make projects less flexible, and that projects can pass every gate and still fail commercially.[^s3] Cooper himself conceded that classic gating systems can be too linear to absorb change once a product definition is locked.[^s2] He responded with lighter "NexGen" variants that use overlapping stages, spiral development and a post-launch review.[^s1]

## In practice

The best-known adaptation is the Agile-Stage-Gate hybrid. Cooper and A. F. Sommer's 2018 study of six manufacturers, including Danfoss, GE, Honeywell, Chamberlain and LEGO Education, described firms that kept the gates but ran each stage as a series of time-boxed sprints of roughly two to four weeks, with gate deliverables shifting from reports toward working prototypes.[^s2] Danfoss reported a 30% reduction in time to market, and LEGO Education launched a product within 12 months after years of struggle under its classic process. The authors cautioned that most such gains were interviewee estimates, since only one firm kept formal metrics.[^s2] The firms typically reserved the hybrid for roughly a fifth of their projects, usually the larger and riskier ones.[^s2]

## Connections

Stage-Gate is a component practice within the broader field of innovation management and a specific way of organizing new product development.[^s1][^s4] It is closely tied to product portfolio management, because the same gate decisions that judge one project also allocate scarce resources across the whole pipeline.[^s1] It contrasts with agile software development, whose iterative sprints Cooper later grafted onto the gate structure,[^s2] and its critics argue it fits discontinuous, disruptive opportunities poorly because its linear structure resists new information.[^s3] The graph links it to the lean startup as a contrasting, more iterative way of organizing early-stage product work.
