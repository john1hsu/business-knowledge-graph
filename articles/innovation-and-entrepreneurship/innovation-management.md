# Innovation Management: Organizing the Innovation Process

Innovation management is the discipline concerned with how organizations repeatedly generate, select, develop and commercialize new ideas, rather than with any one invention. Andrew Van de Ven framed innovation as the development and implementation of new ideas by people who interact with others over time inside an institutional context, so managing it means managing ideas, people, transactions and context together.[^s1] OpenStax's management textbook places it within the broader management of technology and innovation (MTI): planning, implementing, evaluating and controlling resources and capabilities to create value and competitive advantage, with innovation defined as the part of technology activity that adds newness to products or processes.[^s2] On this view the job is to build systems that let valuable ideas surface, not merely to plan the next product.[^s2]

## Origin

Innovation became a general-management problem, and not only an R&D one, in the organization-studies literature of the 1980s. Van de Ven's 1986 article in *Management Science* identified four central problems for executives: managing attention so that people notice the need for novelty (a human problem), shepherding new ideas into accepted practice (a process problem), managing part–whole relationships as an innovation spreads across units (a structural problem), and providing institutional leadership (a strategic problem).[^s1] The paper also set out an agenda for longitudinal, process-based research on how innovations actually unfold.[^s1]

## How it works

**Choosing the kind of innovation.** Gary Pisano of Harvard Business School argues that a firm first needs an explicit innovation strategy. His typology sorts projects by whether they require new technology, a new business model, both or neither: *routine* (neither), *disruptive* (new business model, little new technology), *radical* (new technology, same model) and *architectural* (both).[^s4] Most firms should hold a portfolio across these types, weighted by their capabilities, market and technology cycles; he notes that much of Apple's growth came from routine iPhone upgrades and that Alphabet devotes most resources to its core search business while funding a smaller set of "other bets."[^s4]

**Sourcing the innovation.** OpenStax describes three routes to new technology: buying or partnering (acquisitions, joint ventures, licensing), internal development through R&D, and entrepreneurial exploitation of an opportunity through a new venture.[^s3] External routes are faster but risk culture clashes and not-invented-here resistance; internal R&D gives clear ownership but is slower and costly; new ventures are agile but carry the highest risk.[^s3]

**Building organizational capacity.** Managers are advised to install sensing systems for technology opportunities and threats, ensure information flows to decision makers, keep employee skills current, and support new technology with deliberate change management, balancing short-term efficiency against long-term effectiveness.[^s3]

**Process and system standards.** Many firms run new products through phased development with decision gates, a family of methods derived from Robert Cooper's Stage-Gate concept, in which cross-functional reviews at each gate decide whether a project advances.[^s6] At the whole-organization level, ISO/TC 279 published ISO 56001 in September 2024 as the first requirements standard for an innovation management system; it applies to organizations of any size or sector and uses the harmonized structure shared by other ISO management-system standards.[^s5]

## Evidence and critiques

The evidence on innovation processes is mixed. A paper presented at PMI's 2000 symposium reported that phase-gate methods were associated with predictability, faster time-to-market and better cross-functional links, but that many managers also experienced added overhead, stifled decisions and accountability drifting away from project teams, and that development could become costlier and less innovative.[^s6] Its author found no universal evidence of the tools' effectiveness and stressed that results depended on management commitment and redesigned reward and leadership systems.[^s6] Pisano criticizes the opposite error of treating disruption as the right strategy for every firm, arguing that routine innovation can be highly profitable and that the mix should be chosen deliberately.[^s4] Van de Ven's attention problem helps explain why successful firms still miss shifts: routines that make the core business efficient also dull sensitivity to new needs.[^s1]

## In practice

OpenStax uses Kodak as a cautionary case: the company missed opportunities in instant photography and digital cameras and later went bankrupt, while IBM kept building mainframes as desktops and laptops took hold.[^s2] In contrast, GE found that a portable MRI designed for less-developed countries also sold to large-animal veterinarians in richer markets, showing the value of sensing systems that notice unexpected demand.[^s2]

## Connections

Innovation management sits under general management theory and builds on Van de Ven's 1986 problem framing.[^s1] Organizational ambidexterity is one of its structural answers to balancing exploitation and exploration, and dynamic capabilities describe the sensing and reconfiguring capacity it tries to build.[^s3] Disruptive innovation is one category in Pisano's portfolio rather than the whole field.[^s4] The Stage-Gate process is a component practice,[^s6] and the Kodak decline illustrates what happens when the attention problem goes unsolved.[^s2]
