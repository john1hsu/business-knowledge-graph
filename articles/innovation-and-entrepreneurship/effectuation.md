# Effectuation: Sarasvathy's Logic of Entrepreneurial Expertise

**Effectuation** is a theory of how experienced entrepreneurs make decisions when the future cannot be forecast. It was introduced by Saras Sarasvathy in "Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency", published in the *Academy of Management Review* in 2001 (vol. 26, no. 2), which the University of Virginia's Darden School identifies as the paper that founded the field.[^s1] The theory contrasts two logics. *Causation* takes a particular effect as given and chooses among means to reach it, relying on prediction; *effectuation* takes a set of means as given and chooses among the effects those means could produce, relying on control.[^s4] The contrast is often summarized as the claim that "to the extent we can control the future, we do not need to predict it."[^s4] Sarasvathy argued that this logic is needed to explain how new firms and markets come into existence, which mainstream economic and management theory tends to take as already given.[^s1][^s4]

## Origin

Sarasvathy earned her PhD at Carnegie Mellon University, where she worked with the Nobel laureate Herbert Simon, after early ventures of her own in Mumbai in the 1980s had failed.[^s2] For her doctoral research in the late 1990s she studied expert American entrepreneurs, each with at least 15 years of founding multiple ventures (including failures) and at least one company taken public.[^s2] Using think-aloud verbal protocols, she gave each expert a hypothetical startup case with ten decisions and recorded their reasoning; she concluded that their expertise lay not in better forecasting but in a different, non-predictive logic.[^s2] She later developed the theory in the 2008 book *Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise* and, with Sankaran Venkataraman, argued in 2011 that entrepreneurship is a teachable method, comparable to the scientific method.[^s2] She joined Darden in 2004.[^s2]

## The five principles

The Society for Effectual Action summarizes effectual reasoning in five principles:[^s3][^s2]

- **Bird in hand:** start with your means (who you are, what you know and whom you know) and let goals emerge from action.[^s3]
- **Affordable loss:** commit only what you can afford to lose, instead of choosing projects by forecast returns.[^s3]
- **Crazy quilt:** build the venture with stakeholders who self-select in and commit resources, rather than starting from competitive analysis.[^s3]
- **Lemonade:** treat surprises and contingencies as potential opportunities rather than deviations from plan.[^s3]
- **Pilot in the plane:** focus on the parts of the future that human action can shape rather than on predicting trends.[^s2][^s3]

In empirical work these map onto contrasts with causal reasoning: experimentation versus fixed objectives, affordable loss versus expected returns, alliances and pre-commitments versus competitive analysis, and flexibility versus exploiting pre-existing capabilities.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Chandler, DeTienne, McKelvie and Mumford built and validated survey scales in two studies of founders of firms no older than five years (111 in instrument manufacturing; 196 in plastics and packaged software).[^s4] They found causation and effectuation to be distinct constructs, with causation negatively and effectual experimentation positively correlated with perceived uncertainty, as the theory predicts; pre-commitments, however, appeared in both logics.[^s4] The authors cautioned that their retrospective, single-informant data did not test whether either logic improves performance.[^s4] An earlier meta-analysis by Read, Song and Smit, drawing on data covering 9,897 new ventures reported in the *Journal of Business Venturing*, found that three effectuation principles were positively associated with new-venture performance.[^s5]

The most prominent critique is Arend, Sarooghi and Burkemper's 2015 *Academy of Management Review* article, which assessed effectuation against formal theory-building criteria, judged that it had received too little empirical testing and critical scrutiny, and proposed five ways to strengthen it.[^s6] The literature has nonetheless kept growing: Darden notes that more than 2,000 peer-reviewed articles have now been written on the topic.[^s1]

## In practice

Effectuation is widely taught as an alternative to business-plan-first entrepreneurship courses, on the premise that the method can be learned rather than being a trait of "born" entrepreneurs.[^s2] Its reach extends beyond universities: Sarasvathy has studied nationwide entrepreneurship education in Danish secondary schools, introduced after a reform required two types of school to teach it, and in 2023 she gave the Schumpeter Lecture at the European Union's SME Assembly.[^s2] Companion papers have extended the logic to marketing under uncertainty (*Journal of Marketing*, 2009) and to angel investors' decisions (*Journal of Business Venturing*, 2009).[^s1]

## Connections

Effectuation is a theory within **entrepreneurship** and a specialized account of **decision making** under uncertainty, and its focus on how people reason when optimization is impossible reflects the **bounded rationality** tradition of Sarasvathy's mentor **Herbert Simon**.[^s2][^s4] It **contrasts with** the predictive **business plan**, which fixes goals and then gathers resources.[^s4] It shares with the **lean startup** and **customer development** an emphasis on cheap experiments and early contact with customers and partners, while its affordable-loss principle, which caps the downside instead of maximizing expected return, is comparable to the logic of **real options**.[^s3][^s4] The theory was **created by** **Saras Sarasvathy** and **introduced in** her 2001 AMR article.[^s1]
