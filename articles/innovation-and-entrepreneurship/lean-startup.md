# Lean Startup

The **lean startup** is a methodology for creating new ventures that favors experimentation over detailed planning and customer feedback over founder intuition.[^s1] It rests on Steve Blank's definition of a startup as a temporary organization searching for a repeatable and scalable business model, as opposed to a small version of a large company executing a known one.[^s1] Instead of writing a multi-year business plan, founders state their vision as testable business model hypotheses, test them with a series of minimum viable products, and then decide whether to persevere, pivot or abandon the idea.[^s2]

## Origin

Blank developed **customer development**, the practice of leaving the building to test assumptions with real customers, over his career as a Silicon Valley entrepreneur and teacher, and set it out in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2003).[^s1] Eric Ries, a founder Blank advised, combined customer development with agile software practices and called the result "lean startup," a nod to lean manufacturing; Ries's book *The Lean Startup* followed in 2011.[^s1] Harvard Business School's Thomas Eisenmann describes the method as borrowing the Toyota Production System's focus on eliminating waste, here meaning time and money spent building products nobody wants.[^s3] Blank's 2013 *Harvard Business Review* article brought the approach to a general management audience and folded in Osterwalder and Pigneur's business model canvas.[^s1]

## How it works

Blank summarizes the method in three practices. First, founders map their hypotheses about customers, value proposition, channels, revenue and costs on a one-page business model canvas. Second, they test those hypotheses through customer development, using minimum viable products to gather reactions. Third, they build the product through agile development in short, incremental cycles.[^s1]

A **minimum viable product** (MVP) is the smallest set of features needed to rigorously validate a concept.[^s2] Eisenmann's teaching cases give early examples: Dropbox drew sign-ups with a bare-bones launch, and Aardvark answered user questions with people behind the scenes before automating the service.[^s3] A **pivot** is a change to one or more hypotheses after evidence shows the current model is failing; Blank cites Blue River Technology shifting from robotic lawn mowers for golf courses to weed-control machines for farms.[^s1] Startups are urged not to scale spending on sales and marketing until they reach product-market fit.[^s3] The HBS background note argues that turning beliefs into explicit tests also counters cognitive biases that lead founders to persist with bad ideas.[^s2]

## Adoption

By Blank's 2013 account, the U.S. National Science Foundation had used lean methods since 2011 in its Innovation Corps program to help scientists commercialize research, more than 25 universities taught the approach, and Harvard Business School had replaced its business plan contest with a business model competition in 2012.[^s1] He also reported large-company adopters, including GE, Qualcomm and Intuit.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

The strongest evidence comes from field experiments. In a randomized controlled trial with 116 Italian startups tracked for about a year, Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella and Spina gave both groups training in collecting market feedback, but taught the treatment group to frame and rigorously test explicit hypotheses; treated firms performed better and pivoted more often without dropping out at higher rates.[^s4] That result supports disciplined hypothesis testing, not necessarily lean practice as commonly applied. A 2025 trial with 151 Tanzanian agribusiness entrepreneurs found that adding a "theory of value" before testing produced higher profits than a pure lean, validation-first training, although termination rates were similar (10% vs. 9%) and the revenue difference was not statistically significant.[^s6]

Felin, Gambardella, Stern and Zenger argue that lean's emphasis on quick, easily observed customer feedback steers founders toward incremental opportunities, that customers struggle to judge truly novel products, and that the method offers little help in generating a distinctive theory in the first place.[^s5] They recommend starting from a theory of value, running critical experiments that test it, and making real commitments when cheap pivots cannot reveal what is needed.[^s5] Even supporters note limits: Eisenmann concedes lean methods fit web startups far better than clean tech or biotech ventures that need heavy capital before any working product exists, and that an MVP can be misread as license for sloppy work.[^s3] Blank's own 2013 claim that 75% of startups fail, citing HBS research by Shikhar Ghosh, frames the method as reducing failure across a portfolio rather than guaranteeing success.[^s1]

## Connections

Lean startup is a practice within **innovation management**, created by **Eric Ries** on the foundation of Blank's **customer development** and influenced by **lean manufacturing**'s war on waste.[^s1][^s3] It applies the same logic as **hypothesis-driven problem solving** to venture creation, and it uses the **business model canvas** as its hypothesis map.[^s1][^s2] Its goal of finding **product-market fit** before scaling links it to **jobs to be done**, which sharpens what customers are actually trying to accomplish.[^s3] The **theory-based view** of strategy contrasts with it by putting theory development ahead of rapid validation.[^s5][^s6]
