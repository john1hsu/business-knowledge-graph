# Andrew Van de Ven

Andrew H. Van de Ven was an American management scholar who spent most of his career studying how organizations innovate and change. He earned his PhD at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1972, taught at Kent State University and the Wharton School, and joined the University of Minnesota's Carlson School of Management in 1981, where he held the Vernon H. Heath Professorship of Organizational Innovation and Change until retiring in 2016.[^s1][^s2] He died on 30 April 2022, aged 76.[^s1][^s3] By the time of his death his work had been cited more than 90,000 times on Google Scholar, and he had mentored over 30 doctoral students who went on to faculty posts at schools including MIT, Michigan and Wharton.[^s1][^s2]

## Major contributions

**Group decision methods.** Early in his career, Van de Ven worked on structured group processes, notably the Nominal Group Technique, alongside research on organization design and assessment and on inter-organizational relationships.[^s2][^s3] The technique separates silent, individual idea generation from group discussion and voting.

**The Minnesota Innovation Research Program (MIRP).** In the 1980s he directed a program in which about 30 faculty and doctoral students followed fourteen innovations from initial concept through implementation, in public and private organizations; corporate partners included 3M, Honeywell and Cargill.[^s1][^s2] Rather than reconstructing innovation histories after the fact, the researchers tracked events as they unfolded.

**The Innovation Journey.** MIRP's findings were synthesised in *The Innovation Journey* (Oxford University Press, 1999). The book argues that innovation is neither a neat sequence of stages nor random trial and error, but a nonlinear dynamic process of repeated divergent and convergent cycles, shaped by resources, structure and institutional rules. Its advice to managers is that they can learn to navigate the journey but not control it.[^s6]

**Process theories of change.** With Marshall Scott Poole, Van de Ven proposed in 1995 that explanations of organizational change rest on four ideal-type "motors": life cycle (an immanent program drives prescribed stages), teleology (purposeful goal-setting and adaptation), dialectics (conflict between opposing entities produces a synthesis) and evolution (variation, selection and retention across a population).[^s4] The motors are distinguished by unit of change (a single entity versus multiple entities) and mode of change (prescribed versus constructive), and combining them yields 16 logically possible theories for classifying existing work and spotting gaps.[^s4]

**Engaged scholarship.** In a 2006 *Academy of Management Review* article with Paul Johnson, Van de Ven reframed the theory–practice gap. Instead of treating it as a knowledge-transfer problem, they treated it as a knowledge-production problem, and they proposed engaged scholarship: researchers and practitioners jointly study complex real problems over long periods, comparing rival models and using the differences in their perspectives as a resource.[^s5] He continued promoting this approach after retirement, including through an engaged-scholarship conference and professorship that he and his wife funded.[^s1]

**Service to the field.** He served as president of the Academy of Management, was a founding senior editor of *Organization Science* and founding editor of *Academy of Management Discoveries*, received distinguished-scholar awards from three Academy divisions, and was named an Academy Fellow.[^s1][^s3]

## Evidence and critiques

Van de Ven's own work is frank about its limits. The 1995 typology uses "evolution" in a deliberately narrow, biology-based sense, and its authors present the four motors as ideal types that real change processes combine rather than as complete descriptions.[^s4] Van de Ven and Johnson likewise acknowledge that engaged scholarship is demanding and prone to conflict, since academics and practitioners hold different goals and kinds of knowledge; they argue that those tensions are the source of its value rather than a reason to avoid it.[^s5] The MIRP findings that innovation is nonlinear also make prescriptive advice harder: the book's conclusion stresses navigating uncertainty rather than offering a reliable stage-gate formula.[^s6]

## In practice

Managers meet Van de Ven's ideas most directly in two forms. The Nominal Group Technique, one of the research topics his university highlights, gives facilitators a structured way to generate and rank options in a meeting.[^s2] And the four-motor typology gives change leaders a diagnostic question: is a given transformation following a prescribed sequence, a purposeful plan, a clash between factions, or competitive selection? The answer suggests different levers, from milestone planning to coalition-building.[^s4]
