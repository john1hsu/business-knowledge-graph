# Venture Capital: Funds, Stages and Startup Investing

Venture capital (VC) is equity financing that professional investors provide to young companies whose products are unproven but whose upside, if they succeed, is very large. Such firms usually have no earnings and little collateral, so banks will not lend to them and public markets cannot price them; the venture capitalist fills the gap by selecting a few promising firms, buying minority stakes, and working closely with management until an exit.[^s4][^s5] It is a small but outsized channel: by Kaplan and Lerner's 2010 estimate, only about 1,000 of the roughly 600,000 new U.S. employer businesses founded each year received a first VC round, about one-sixth of one percent, yet more than 60% of U.S. IPOs from 1999 through 2009 were VC-backed, and Microsoft, Apple, Google and Cisco were partly venture funded.[^s5]

## Origin

The modern industry is usually traced to American Research and Development Corporation (ARD), set up in Boston after World War II by a group that included MIT president Karl Compton, Boston Fed president Ralph Flanders and Harvard Business School dean Donald David.[^s1] Georges Doriot, an HBS professor, became ARD's president in December 1946 and ran it on the principle of backing creative people with both money and managerial help, choosing a small fraction of the proposals it received.[^s1] HBS's Baker Library describes ARD as among the first firms to supply risk equity from outside family fortunes; its portfolio included High Voltage Engineering, Tracerlab and Ionics, and Doriot preferred long-term support to quick turnarounds.[^s1]

## How it works

**Fund structure.** Most VC firms raise closed-end funds organized as limited partnerships: the firm is the general partner (GP) that makes decisions, and pension funds, endowments and wealthy individuals are limited partners (LPs) who supply most of the capital.[^s2] Funds typically run about ten years, with new investments made mainly in the first five.[^s2] In Metrick and Yasuda's 2007 sample of 94 VC funds, the modal annual management fee was 2% and 87 funds took 20% carried interest on profits, yet about 60% of expected GP revenue across their full sample came from fixed fees rather than performance.[^s2]

**Screening.** In a survey of 885 VCs at 681 firms, the median firm closed about four deals a year and looked at roughly 100 opportunities for each one it funded.[^s4] Respondents ranked the management team above the product, market or business model as both the main selection criterion and the main driver of success or failure, and they rated deal selection as a bigger source of value than sourcing or post-investment help.[^s4] Few used discounted cash flow; cash-on-cash multiples (63%) and IRR (42%) dominated, with an average target multiple of about 5.5x.[^s4]

**Contracts and staging.** VC deals typically use convertible preferred stock that lets investors split cash-flow, voting, board, liquidation and control rights separately.[^s3] Those rights, and future financing rounds, are often tied to measurable milestones: if the company does poorly investors gain control, and if it does well the founders gain it back, while vesting and non-compete terms limit hold-up.[^s3] Survey respondents reported pro-rata rights in 81% of deals and participation rights in 53%.[^s4]

**Monitoring and exit.** Most VCs talk to portfolio companies at least weekly and offer strategic guidance, investor introductions and help hiring.[^s4] Returns are highly skewed: respondents estimated that about 15% of exits were IPOs, 53% acquisitions and 32% failures, with roughly 9% of investments returning more than ten times capital.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Researchers generally credit the model with financing a disproportionate share of high-growth, innovative companies.[^s5][^s6] The industry is cyclical, however: commitments surged in the dot-com boom and fell back afterward, and fund returns move inversely with prior inflows, which Kaplan and Lerner read as a self-correcting market rather than a broken one.[^s5] Lerner and Nanda raise three broader concerns: VC money flows to a narrow range of technologies, a small number of investors direct much of the capital for radical innovation, and many firms have relaxed the intense governance once central to the model.[^s6]

## In practice

Doriot's ARD set the template of patient equity paired with mentoring of portfolio leaders.[^s1] Today a founder raising a seed or Series A round faces the same logic, negotiating a priced round of preferred stock whose terms — liquidation preferences, board composition, anti-dilution — matter as much as the headline valuation.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Venture capital is a branch of **private equity** and of **entrepreneurial finance**, and it **contrasts with the leveraged buyout**, which buys control of mature firms with debt rather than minority stakes in young ones.[^s2] For founders it is an **alternative to angel investing** by individuals, and VCs themselves name other investors as a major source of their deals.[^s4] Staged, contingent contracts **mitigate information asymmetry and moral hazard**, and the GP–LP fund structure is itself a **principal-agent** arrangement governed by fees and carry.[^s2][^s3] VC funding decisions interact with **lean startup** practice and the search for **product-market fit**, it is a key mechanism of **creative destruction** and **innovation management**, and it feeds **open innovation** as established firms acquire VC-backed startups.[^s5][^s6] **American Research and Development Corporation** exemplifies the early model.[^s1]
