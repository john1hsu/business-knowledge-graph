# Disruptive Innovation

**Disruptive innovation** is a theory of competitive change developed by Clayton Christensen. It describes how a smaller entrant with fewer resources can successfully challenge well-run incumbents. The entrant begins by serving customers the incumbents overlook, improves its offering, moves upmarket, and "disrupts" the incumbents once mainstream customers start adopting it in volume.[^s1] The theory's central puzzle is why leading firms fail even when managers do the things good management prescribes, such as listening closely to their best customers and investing where margins are highest.[^s1]

## Origin

The idea first appeared as "disruptive technologies" in Joseph Bower and Clayton Christensen's article "Disruptive Technologies: Catching the Wave" (*Harvard Business Review*, January 1995). It was developed in Christensen's book *The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail* (Harvard Business School Press, 1997). Christensen and Bower's paper "Customer Power, Strategic Investment, and the Failure of Leading Firms" (*Strategic Management Journal*, 1996) set out the academic version.[^s2] The empirical base was the rigid disk-drive industry. Incumbents led on innovations that improved what existing customers valued, such as storage capacity. On smaller, lighter or more rugged drives that were initially weaker on capacity, entrants led and incumbents repeatedly lost out. This happened across several product generations.[^s1]

## How it works

The theory separates **sustaining** innovations, which improve performance on dimensions mainstream customers already care about and let incumbents sell more at higher margins, from **disruptive** innovations. Disruptive innovations start out inferior on those dimensions but bring a different bundle of attributes, such as lower price, smaller size or greater convenience, that appeals to fringe customers.[^s1] Entrants gain a foothold in one of two ways. In **low-end disruption** they take the least profitable customers in an existing market, as steel minimills and discount retailers did. In **new-market disruption** they serve people who previously went without the product entirely, as early personal computers and Sony's transistor pocket radios did.[^s1]

Incumbents do not usually fail because the technology is hard to build. Their resource-allocation processes favor projects with high margins and large, identifiable markets, and their most important customers are not asking for the new product. So disruptive projects get starved or reshaped to fit the existing business.[^s1] Later work drew on resource-dependence theory, the idea that the customers and investors who supply a firm's resources constrain what it can do, to explain this mechanism.[^s1] Disruptiveness is also relative to the firm. The internet was a sustaining innovation for mail-order retailers but a disruptive one for store-based retailers, so no technology is disruptive by nature. Over time the theory has been reframed as more about business models than technology.[^s1]

## Evidence and critiques

Christensen's later co-authored review describes how the theory moved from a descriptive account of one industry to a causal theory, refined largely through anomalies raised by critics. These critics include King and Tucci on the disk-drive evidence, Danneels on definitions and predictive use, and Sood and Tellis on whether disruptive technologies really begin cheaper and follow the assumed performance paths.[^s1] The sharpest empirical challenge came in 2015. Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh asked 79 experts to assess 77 cases that Christensen and Michael Raynor had cited as disruption. Only 9% of the cases met all four of the theory's core conditions they tested. The authors did not call for discarding the theory but for combining its best parts with classical strategic analysis.[^s3] Defenders replied that more than 40 published studies had already tested or challenged the theory, and that the theory has more than four elements.[^s4] Historian Jill Lepore's 2014 *New Yorker* essay also criticized the quality of the original case studies.[^s3]

## Example

Video rental is a case the theory's authors return to. When Netflix challenged it, Blockbuster responded by bolting an online offering onto its brick-and-mortar business. Christensen and colleagues cite this as a "hybrid" incumbent response. They compare it to earlier hybrids such as sailing ships fitted with steam engines and Bucyrus Erie's hybrid excavator, both of which failed to hold off the new architecture.[^s1] Steel minimills, which entered at the bottom of an existing market, are the theory's standard example of the low-end path.[^s1]

## Connections

Disruptive innovation sits within **innovation management** and is closely tied to its source work, **The Innovator's Dilemma**, and to its creator, **Clayton Christensen**.[^s2] It is defined in contrast to **sustaining innovation** and draws on **resource-dependence theory** to explain incumbent inertia.[^s1] It complicates the logic of **barriers to entry** and **industrial organization**. Entrants succeed not by overcoming incumbents' barriers head-on but by competing where incumbents do not want to compete.[^s1] New-market disruption competes against non-consumption, a theme shared with Christensen's **jobs-to-be-done** thinking, and the **Netflix** business-model shift is a case the theory's authors discuss.[^s1]
