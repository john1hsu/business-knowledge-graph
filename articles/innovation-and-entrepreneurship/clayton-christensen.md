# Clayton Christensen

Clayton M. Christensen was a Harvard Business School (HBS) professor whose research on why successful companies lose to upstarts made him one of the most cited management thinkers of his generation. HBS described him as the world's leading authority on disruptive innovation, and he held the Kim B. Clark Professor of Business Administration chair from 2001 until his death in Boston on January 23, 2020, at age 67.[^s1] Beyond scholarship, he founded or co-founded several organizations that turned his theories into consulting, investing and policy work.[^s2]

## Background and career

Born in Salt Lake City, Christensen earned a BA in economics from Brigham Young University (1975), an MPhil in applied econometrics at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar (1977), and an MBA with high distinction from HBS (1979).[^s1][^s2] Before returning to academia he served as a White House Fellow in 1982 and co-founded the advanced-materials company CPS Technologies in 1984 with three MIT professors.[^s2] He completed a doctorate in business administration at HBS in 1992, joined its faculty that year, and was tenured in 1998.[^s1] He went on to found the innovation consultancy Innosight (2000), the investment firm Rose Park Advisors (2007), and a nonprofit think tank now known as the Christensen Institute.[^s2]

## Ideas

Christensen's theory grew out of business history. His 1993 *Business History Review* article traced how leading, vertically integrated disk drive makers focused new component technology on their largest customers and so overlooked emerging segments; entrants with different drive architectures served those segments, improved quickly, and eventually took over the established markets.[^s3] That study preceded *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997) by four years, and historian Tom Nicholas has argued that the approach drew on Alfred Chandler's account of the large integrated enterprise and on a broader habit, common in innovation research at the time, of building theory from the histories of firms and industries.[^s6] The book won the Global Business Book Award as the best business book of 1997, and Christensen eventually wrote eleven books and more than a hundred articles.[^s2]

His second major idea concerned demand rather than supply. In *Competing Against Luck* (2016), written with co-authors, he argued that customers do not simply buy products but "hire" them to get a job done, and that understanding the job, rather than customer demographics, makes innovation more predictable; the book illustrates the point with firms such as Intuit, Airbnb and Chobani.[^s4]

## Evidence and critiques

Christensen's influence is not in doubt: he was named the most influential living business thinker in polls in 2011 and 2013.[^s2] The empirical generality of disruption theory is more contested. Tuck School professor Andrew King and co-author Baljir Baatartogtokh asked 79 experts to assess 77 cases Christensen had cited as disruptions and found that only 9% displayed all the elements of his own theory; their fall 2015 *MIT Sloan Management Review* study nonetheless recommended keeping the theory's strongest parts alongside classical strategic analysis.[^s5] Nicholas's historical review similarly surveys the agenda's impact and critiques and asks where the limits of drawing lessons from history should be set.[^s6]

## In practice

The disk drive case remains the canonical illustration of his work: successive generations of smaller drives, first sold into segments that incumbents judged unattractive, displaced the established integrated producers and left the industry to a network of more specialized firms.[^s3] He carried the same lens into practice by founding Innosight to advise companies on innovation and Rose Park Advisors to invest along its lines, while HBS's dean called him one of the world's greatest scholars on innovation.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

Christensen is the originator of `kt:disruptive-innovation`, set out at book length in `kt:innovators-dilemma`, which grew directly from his disk drive research.[^s3][^s6] He also developed and popularized `kt:jobs-to-be-done` as a theory of customer choice.[^s4] His work sits within `kt:innovation-management`; it is related to `kt:creative-destruction` as another account of incumbents being displaced by new entrants and technologies, and to `kt:organizational-ambidexterity`, a later research stream on how established firms can pursue new markets without abandoning their core, which is the problem his disk drive history exposed.[^s3][^s6]
